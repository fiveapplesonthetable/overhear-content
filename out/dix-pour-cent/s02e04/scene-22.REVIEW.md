## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I can hear you from my office.' Gabriel complains about the noisy laughter drifting down the corridor. The verb entends indicates hearing sensory input.
- 'Hello, could you be more discreet?' Camille asks the men to keep their voices down near client offices. The conditional pourriez softens a polite request.
- 'We are just relaxing for a moment.' Hicham defends their brief pause in the work day. The reflexive verb se detend means to unwind.
- 'If it bothers you, I can leave.' The touchy executive threatens to walk away from the area. The verb derange means to disturb or bother.
- 'Do not take things like that.' Gabriel urges his partner not to react defensively. The negative imperative ne prenez pas advises moderation.
- 'I am used to these remarks.' Hicham shrugs off the criticism with weary indifference. The idiom avoir l'habitude means to be accustomed to something.
- 'Goodbye, Camille. Have a good day.' He bids a polite farewell to the young assistant. The formulaic farewell au revoir is standard in professional settings.
- 'Goodbye Hicham, see you very soon.' Camille responds courteously as he departs down the hall. The expression a tres bientot means see you very soon.
- 'What does he have against us?' Gabriel wonders why tension lingers between the agency partners. The preposition contre denotes opposition or resentment.
- 'I do not know at all.' Camille declines to take sides in the ongoing executive friction. The phrase du tout intensifies the negation.
- 'Is he still very angry with me?' Gabriel fears that past disagreements have poisoned their working relationship. The idiomatic structure en vouloir a quelqu'un means to hold a grudge against someone.
- 'He still did you a favor.' The assistant points out that Hicham helped out despite their differences. The phrase rendre service means to do someone a favor.
- 'He did it for you only.' Gabriel remains convinced that Hicham acts solely out of personal loyalty to Camille. The adverb seulement restricts the scope of the action.
- 'Stop imagining the worst, Gabriel.' Camille encourages her colleague to stop overthinking every interaction. The superlative le pire refers to the worst possible outcome.
