## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Gabriel, do you have a minute?' Herve approaches his boss with a hesitant expression. The formal 'you' pronoun sets a courteous, professional distance around 'have'.
- 'Yes, what is going on?' Gabriel stops and gives his assistant his attention. Notice how the short vowel drops before a vowel sound around 'is happening', making the phrase flow smoothly as one unit.
- 'I have something to confess to you.' Herve prepares to admit his secret acting job. The clause hinges on the everyday verb meaning 'have'.
- 'What then? Nothing serious?' Gabriel hopes there is no crisis at the agency. The adverb meaning 'then' gives the reply its conversational emphasis.
- 'No, but it is very delicate.' Herve tries to soften the news before speaking. The adverb meaning 'very' gives the reply its conversational emphasis.
- 'Come on, speak, I am listening to you!' Gabriel impatiently gestures for him to get to the point. Gabriel uses the polite 'you' form alongside 'speak' to keep a respectful professional tone.
- 'I played a role in a movie.' Herve finally reveals his appearance on the big screen. Notice how 'have' leads directly into the noun meaning 'role'.
- 'I acted in a real movie!' Herve emphasizes that it was a legitimate feature production. This conversational past tense combines a helper verb with the past verb form for 'have'.
- 'The Donzelli movie? I know.' Gabriel immediately names the director without surprise. Notice how 'know' leads directly into the noun meaning 'movie'.
- 'Elise already told me.' Gabriel explains how the news reached him first. The short 'self' word sits right in front of the verb for 'has', turning the action back onto the subject.
- 'What? That is not true!' Herve is stunned that his confidence was betrayed. A short, idiomatic conversational reply that keeps the exchange moving naturally.
- 'She was supposed to keep the secret!' Herve complains that Elise promised not to tell. The verb meaning 'was supposed to' pairs naturally with the noun for 'secret' in everyday conversation.
- 'My God, you are so naive!' Gabriel teases Herve for trusting a rival agency head. The formal 'you' pronoun sets a courteous, professional distance around 'God'.
- 'You cannot trust anyone.' Herve laments the lack of discretion in the film industry. The two-part French negation brackets the verb for 'can' on both sides.
