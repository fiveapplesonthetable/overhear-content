## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I am the seagull.' A young actress in Chekhov's play says this about herself. The word for the bird is feminine, so it follows the feminine form of 'the'.
- 'No, that's wrong.' The second part literally says 'that is false'. It is a short, direct way of correcting yourself or rejecting an answer.
- 'Mum?' This familiar word for a mother can stand alone as a call. The rising tone makes it a surprised question here.
- 'Well, well!' Repeating this short word expresses surprise. It comes from 'hold', but here it is a reaction rather than an instruction to take something.
- 'It's not easy to learn lines with all this noise.' The negative wraps around the verb 'to be'. The word for lines here is simply 'a text'. A mother's advice, given in a busy reception area.
- 'Really work on it at home.' Nathalie gives a casual command, with the little word for 'it' joined on after it. The last two words mean 'at your place'.
- 'Thanks, Mum.' The first word is enough for an everyday thank-you. Adding the person's name or family title makes clear whom you are addressing.
- 'Sorry, we were in a crisis meeting.' The verb describes what was going on, in the past tense used for background.
- 'A little hitch.' A hitch is a small problem. Gabriel always makes things sound smaller than they are.
- 'A crisis or a hitch?' The short word in the middle means 'or'. Nathalie repeats Gabriel's own description to challenge his attempt to make the problem sound small.
- 'For Gabriel, even a disaster is just a hitch!' The word for 'even' comes before what it stresses. Everybody knows Gabriel.
- 'Laura.' A name on its own can call someone back. Laura has started to leave with Gabriel when her mother asks for her attention.
- 'Yes?' The same word that agrees with a statement can answer someone calling your name. Here it invites Nathalie to continue.
- 'Emmanuel, she must stand up straight.' The verb for 'must' is followed by a reflexive verb meaning 'to hold oneself'. Nathalie is asking Gabriel to watch Laura's posture, but she calls him by the wrong name.
- 'It's Gabriel, Mum!' French simply names Gabriel and then Mum. Laura is correcting the wrong name; the fuller English makes that purpose clear.
- 'Gabriel, right.' Nathalie repeats the corrected name. French leaves the reaction to her tone; English can add 'right' to convey the acknowledgement.
