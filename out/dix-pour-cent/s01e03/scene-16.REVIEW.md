## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I stayed at my mother's.' The past form of 'stay' uses the helper 'be', and its written ending agrees with Laura. The last three words locate the stay at her mother's home.
- 'I completely lost it. We said horrible things to each other.' The first action literally means cracking or snapping. In the second sentence, the small word before 'said' makes it reciprocal: each person said things to the other.
- 'It's going to break her heart.' French literally says 'break the heart to her'. The small word before 'break' identifies whose heart Laura means.
- 'You're right, I can't refuse.' French says 'you have reason' to mean 'you are right'. Laura fears that saying no will hurt her mother. Herve offers another route.
- 'Yes, you can.' This special yes contradicts Laura's negative statement. It firmly pushes back against her claim that she cannot refuse.
- 'You can make sure that she refuses.' The expression in the middle means arranging for something to happen. The person meant by 'she' is the director, not Laura's mother.
- 'Who?' This single question word can stand alone. Laura needs Herve to make clear which woman he means.
- 'Solveig! Just do a Bardot.' Herve gives a direct instruction, with no separate word for 'you'. The actress's surname stands for playing a demanding star.
- 'What's that?' Literally 'it is what, that?'. This casual question puts 'what' after 'is', a pattern you will often hear in conversation.
- 'You become capricious, a difficult star like Brigitte Bardot.' The describing word for 'capricious' has a feminine ending because it refers to Laura. Herve wants a performance that will put the director off.
- 'Stop that now!' Gabriel gives a short command to Herve. The final word means 'now', making his objection immediate.
- 'Akerfelt hates that!' The word for 'that' points back to difficult-star behaviour. Herve argues that only this particular director would reject Laura for it.
- 'That's ridiculous! It's a very bad idea.' The word for 'bad' takes a feminine ending beside the feminine word for 'idea'. Gabriel opposes his assistant's plan.
- 'You're not actually going to do a Bardot?' The two words inside the negative add the feeling of 'really' or 'all the same'. Gabriel asks the question hoping that the answer will be no.
