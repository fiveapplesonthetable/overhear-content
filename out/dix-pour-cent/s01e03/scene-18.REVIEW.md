## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'It's the isolation of the mother and the daughter that interests me.' The frame 'it is ... that' puts special emphasis on isolation. The small word before 'interests' identifies the person feeling that interest.
- 'And their intense relationship on the island.' French places 'intense' after the noun it describes. The possessive 'their' stays singular because it describes one relationship shared by the two women.
- 'It's a time bomb.' Literally 'a bomb with a delay mechanism'. Solveig is very proud of her metaphor.
- 'Yes. That's what I thought.' Literally 'it is that which I was thinking', using the past tense for an ongoing thought.
- 'It's pure intimacy.' Again the adjective 'pure' follows the noun. And right in the middle of this deep speech, Laura launches her act.
- 'I had asked for tea.' She uses the 'had done' tense, as if she has been waiting for hours.
- 'Himalayan flower tea.' When you name a flavour or an ingredient in French, you link it with 'to the'.
- 'Hervé, please! I want my tea.' She calls the assistant straight into the room, using the casual 'please'.
- 'Your tea, Miss Laura.' The possessive 'your' belongs to polite speech. It fits the formal title Herve uses while he plays along with Laura's performance.
- 'With a drop of almond milk, as you like it.' Literally 'a drop of milk of almond'. The little word before 'like' is 'it'.
- 'Not brewed for too long?' Laura leaves out the opening 'has it been'. The phrase at the end means 'too long'; she checks the timing instead of thanking Herve.
- 'That was last time, I'm sorry.' The word for 'last' has a feminine ending because it describes a feminine noun meaning 'time' or 'occasion'. Herve apologises for an earlier mistake.
- 'Are we done now?' Literally 'it's good now?'. The last word points to the current moment. Solveig wants Laura and Herve's tea performance to stop.
- 'Of course.' Literally 'well sure': the two words together express certainty or ready agreement. The same expression can reassure someone or, as here, brush aside a complaint.
- 'Pharrell? Oh, Pharrell Williams!' Laura pretends a famous singer is calling. Herve stages the call from nearby; its English dialogue is left out of this French retelling.
- 'Did I have a rehearsal with John Malkovich tonight?' The opening word means 'I had'. The phrase for 'tonight' literally says 'this evening'. Laura turns to Herve and drops another famous name.
- 'Say that I'm ill, that I can't come.' Laura starts with a casual command: 'say'. Cancelling a rehearsal for a party is the final straw for Solveig.
