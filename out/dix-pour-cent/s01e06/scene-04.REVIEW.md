## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What is so bad about that scene?' The opening asks 'what'. In the middle, the small word before 'bad' strengthens it to 'so bad'. The final noun names the scene she is questioning.
- 'Do not try to understand, fix this problem quickly.' The first command is negative: do not try. The second is positive: fix the problem. The adverb after 'fix' tells him to do it quickly.
- 'I will act in his play, but without a pool.' The opening uses 'I am going' followed by 'act' to state his intention. The final word means 'without'. That one word carries his condition: no swimming pool.
- 'No pool? The entire set design is built around it.' The first question leaves out the action entirely: 'No pool?' The following feminine words refer back to the set design. The final word means 'around', with the pool understood.
- 'Then they change it. I am in no hurry.' The short object before 'change' stands for the set design. In the second sentence, the negative says he is not in a hurry; the final adjective describes François.
- 'That is madness! Opening night is in a week.' The word for madness makes the first outburst. The word that literally means 'first' refers to opening night here. The final time words say how far in the future it is: one week.
- 'And why work with Christophe Bel if we stop him being Christophe Bel?' The first question word means 'why'. The later 'if' introduces Andréa's condition. The little object attached before 'stop' means 'him': preventing the director from being himself.
- 'Him, a genius? With a giant duck on the stage?' The detached opening means 'him?' and expresses disbelief. The word for 'giant' follows the duck. The final few words place it on the stage.
- 'Stop it, you are being really unfair! You are very lucky.' The first word is a command to stop. To say he is lucky, Gabriel uses 'have' with the noun for luck. French expresses this as having a lot of luck.
- 'A sexy atmosphere, swimsuits... Little Mathurine is gorgeous!' The plural clothing expression means swimsuits: literally, suits for bathing. In the second sentence, the final word describes the young actress as gorgeous. Its written shape is the same for men and women.
- 'Drop it, forget about it.' The first command literally says 'let fall', but the expression means 'drop it'. The second command repeats the request with 'forget that'. François wants the subject abandoned.
- 'François, help me understand! Why are you like this?' The command for 'help' has 'me' attached by a hyphen. After 'why', Andréa uses ordinary statement order: informal 'you', then 'are'. She is pressing him to explain his behavior.
- 'What is it? Come on, tell me!' The first short question asks what it is. The next encouragement means 'come on'. The last hyphenated command says 'tell me', making the request more direct.
- 'I do not know how to swim.' The two negative words surround 'know'. Followed by another action, that word means knowing how to do it. François can finally say the specific problem: he cannot swim.
