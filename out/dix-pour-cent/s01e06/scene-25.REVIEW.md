## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Refusing to swim is refusing the norm!' The basic actions 'refuse' and 'swim' begin this statement. The speaker treats refusing to swim as an example of rejecting a social norm.
- 'Dom Juan's thirst for life is right there.' The word for thirst is followed by 'to live'. Together they make a metaphor for wanting to experience life, rather than needing a drink.
- 'Everything is allowed, except the norm of monogamy.' The first word means 'everything'. The following description says it is allowed; the word for 'except' then introduces the one limit he identifies.
- 'He is so afraid of this norm.' French describes fear with 'have'. The amount word after it means 'so much', strengthening the fear he attributes to the character.
- 'He takes refuge with anything wearing a skirt.' The action means taking refuge. The final clothing word is a skirt, used here in the psychoanalyst's account as a way to refer to women.
- 'It isn't his desire that stops him being faithful.' The negative denies desire as the cause. The object attached to 'prevents' means 'him'; the ending names being faithful as the action affected.
- 'It is his fear!' The possessive means 'his', but takes a feminine shape to match fear. The speaker emphasizes fear as his interpretation of the character's behavior.
- 'Pardon? Can you repeat that?' Now Andréa asks for repetition. The first word politely requests another hearing, and the following question asks whether he can repeat what he said.
- 'About the fear of the norm?' The opening word means 'about' here. The fear belongs to the norm: Andréa singles out the idea that caught her attention.
- 'I am going to give you my book.' The opening action introduces what he is going to do next. The object 'you' identifies the recipient, and the final words mean 'my book'.
- 'You will understand.' The ending of 'understand' places it in the future. He tells Andréa that reading the book will help her understand the argument.
- 'I am going to sign it.' The little object before 'sign' means 'it', the book. The action specifically means writing a dedication, rather than merely signing a legal document.
- 'Shall I put my phone number too?' The attached place word at the start means 'in it', referring to the book. The final word means 'also': he offers to add his phone number too.
- 'No. The dedication is enough.' Andréa declines the extra offer. The action 'suffices' means 'is enough'; she says only the dedication is needed.
