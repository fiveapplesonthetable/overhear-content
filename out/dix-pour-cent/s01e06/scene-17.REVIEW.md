## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What do you want?' The opening question frame asks 'what'. It is followed by familiar singular 'you' and 'want'. Catherine is asking her husband what he has come for.
- 'I just want to talk to you. To apologize.' The opening says 'I just want'. The short form before 'apologize' means 'myself', but the complete action simply means apologizing in English.
- 'Apologize as much as you want, I will never forgive you.' The command says 'apologize'. The following words allow any amount of that action, before the future negative states that forgiveness will never follow.
- 'Just five minutes.' The number gives five minutes. The final word means 'only', minimizing the amount of time being requested.
- 'I'm worried about you.' The shortened word attached to 'worry' goes with worrying oneself. The final word is the stressed familiar 'you', the person Mathias is worried about.
- 'Are you afraid for your agency?' French expresses fear with 'have'. The word for 'your' takes its masculine-looking shape before the vowel in agency, even though agency is feminine.
- 'No, darling.' The second word is an affectionate form of address, 'darling'. Its feminine ending matches Catherine, the woman being addressed.
- 'Don't call me darling! Ever!' The opening negative command tells him not to call her that. The last word makes it absolute: 'never'. She refuses the affectionate label.
- 'We'll meet there. At what time?' The first action means meeting up. The hyphenated place word means 'over there'. The following question asks the time of that meeting.
- 'François, he invited you personally. And now you're hesitating?' We have moved to Andréa addressing François. The completed invitation has the object 'you' attached; the final question challenges his present hesitation.
- 'Isn't that what you wanted?' The negative question asks whether this is what he wanted. The final action looks back to the wish he had before receiving the invitation.
- 'Sorry, François, I don't follow you.' The action literally says 'I do not follow you'. As in English, following someone can mean understanding their reasoning, rather than walking behind them.
- 'Right, I'll be there at eight-thirty.' The future action says 'I will be there'. The time is twenty hours thirty: half past eight in the evening.
- 'We'll meet outside.' The final word means 'outside'. They will meet up there before going into the party together.
- 'Yes, Colette, it's me. Something came up at work.' Now Andréa phones Colette. The final completed action means 'something happened', explaining why she says her work plans have changed.
