## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Will You Sleep with Me Tonight?' The title asks whether someone will sleep with the speaker tonight. The ending of the first action makes it a future question.
- 'Darn!' This mild exclamation expresses annoyance. It is a softened way to react to a frustrating surprise, translated as 'darn'.
- 'Love and Muffins.' The first title word means love. The short linking word means 'and', joining that romantic subject with muffins.
- 'Is it a conspiracy or what?' The last two words make a blunt 'or what?' The opening calls it a conspiracy: Andréa jokes that the script titles are targeting her.
- 'Are you okay?' The two words literally ask whether 'it goes'. As a greeting or check-in, the whole question means 'are you okay?'.
- 'Yeah.' This is the familiar spoken version of yes, closer to 'yeah'. It answers the previous question without any explanation.
- 'Do we have news of that Brancillon?' The opening means 'we have'. The plural word for news asks whether they have heard anything from the tax auditor.
- 'It has been three days since she came.' The opening uses a French time expression meaning 'it has been'. The middle words count three days; the final negative says she has not come during that time.
- 'Strange, isn't it?' The last word normally means 'no'. At the end of this positive remark it invites agreement, like 'isn't it?'.
- 'She needed papers.' French expresses needing something as 'have need of'. Here the action looks back: the auditor needed documents.
- 'She didn't call?' The question ends with the completed action 'called'. The two negative words ask whether she has failed to call, as well as failing to visit.
- 'No.' One word gives a negative answer. Camille confirms there has been no call to report.
- 'I will leave you to it.' The little object before 'leave' is familiar singular 'you'. Camille uses this everyday expression to excuse herself from the conversation.
- 'Madame Brancillon!' The first word is the polite title used to address a woman. Andréa uses it with the auditor's surname to get her attention.
- 'Can I have a word with you?' The opening asks permission: 'can I'. The short words after it literally mean 'say a word to you', a request for a brief private conversation.
