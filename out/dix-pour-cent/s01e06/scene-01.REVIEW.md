## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Do you know what an agent is?' Listen for the short word after 'you': it means 'know'. The rest asks what an agent is, so Andréa is checking how Christophe understands their job.
- 'Ten percent of a star's pay?' The three opening words make 'ten percent'. The payment word means an actor's fee here. The little word before it joins 'of' and 'the'.
- 'This project with François Berléand was my idea.' Hear the change from 'this project' to 'it was'. Andréa is looking back at her idea. The word for 'my' takes the shape normally heard before a masculine word, because 'idea' starts with a vowel sound.
- 'The young director with the older actor.' The description puts 'young' before the director and 'older' after the actor. A stage director is named with three words that literally include 'putting on stage'.
- 'An exciting prospect, right?' The final word normally means 'no'. At the end of this positive statement, it works like 'right?' and invites agreement.
- 'I love it!' This is an enthusiastic 'I love it'. The word for 'I' is shortened before the vowel of the verb, so the whole reply is written as one word.
- 'I am all yours.' The closing words make the fixed expression 'all yours', meaning completely at your service. The last word can address several people or one person politely.
- 'But I'm not jumping on the duck!' The two negative words surround the action 'jump'. The final noun is the duck, and the word before its article means 'on'. François refuses to jump onto it.
- 'Just once, to see!' The feminine word after 'one' means an occasion, so the opening is 'just once'. The final two words mean 'to see': try it and find out what happens.
- 'I'm not jumping on the duck, is that clear?' He repeats the same negative about jumping. This time he adds 'is that clear?' The last word is the adjective 'clear', used to demand that his refusal be understood.
- 'The water is lovely, you can try.' The water is described as lovely. You hear the feminine ending because water is feminine in French. The second half says 'you can', followed by the action 'try'.
- 'It's ridiculous, the pool and the duck!' The opening means 'it is ridiculous'. The word for 'it' loses its vowel before 'is'. François then names the two things he objects to: the pool and the duck.
- 'That is not Dom Juan!' Here the negative surrounds 'is': 'is not'. The final 'that' points back to the staging he has just criticized. Dom Juan is the title of the play.
- 'I have had enough!' This is a complete everyday expression for having had enough. Although its verb literally means 'have', translate the whole expression as a complaint, not as possession.
