## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Christophe, you cannot say no.' The negative surrounds 'can'. After it, the action 'refuse' gives the thing Andréa insists Christophe cannot do: turn down her invitation.
- 'My pride won't allow it.' The hyphenated noun means pride or self-respect, literally self-love. The small object before 'allows' means 'me'; the other object means 'it'.
- 'I have lots of things to tell you.' The middle amount expression means 'lots of'. The final action means telling or recounting; the short 'you' before it identifies whom she wants to tell.
- 'No, I have to go home.' The action 'must' is followed by going home. The last two words mean 'to my place', rather than simply anywhere indoors.
- 'I still have a big part of my report to write.' The word after 'have' means 'still'. The report has a large part left; the last action specifies the task is writing it.
- 'No, stay, please!' The one-word command means 'stay'. The familiar polite ending says please, addressed to one person Colette knows well.
- 'You dress like that for a work dinner?' The shortened object before 'dress' belongs to getting oneself dressed. The question contrasts her clothes with a dinner that is supposed to concern work.
- 'Tonight, I play it sober and classy.' The first two words mean 'tonight'. The action literally means 'play', but here Andréa describes the impression she wants her outfit to create.
- 'It is a present for you.' The ending means 'for you'. The thing named before it is a present; in this scene Andréa is offering Colette a toothbrush.
- 'That way you won't need to bring your own anymore.' The negative with the future 'will have' means she will no longer need something. The final words mean 'yours', referring to her own toothbrush.
- 'I celebrate my birthday tomorrow evening at the theatre.' The action means celebrating. The time words specify tomorrow evening, and the final location is the theatre: that is where the birthday will take place.
- 'I would really like you to come.' The opening 'I would like' makes the invitation warmer. The last action is 'come', with 'you' as the person he wants to attend.
- 'As a stage director, you always get what you want.' The opening means 'as a stage director'. The final words mean 'what you want'; Andréa describes his ability to get that result.
- 'I am not bad.' The two negative words surround 'am'. Describing himself as 'not bad' is a modest-sounding way to claim he has some skill.
