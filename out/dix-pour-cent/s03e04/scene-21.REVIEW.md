## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hello Isabelle, how are you feeling this morning?' Gabriel picks up the phone with concern. The polite greeting opens the urgent telephone call.
- 'What do you mean, you have no voice left?' Gabriel reacts with sheer shock to her hoarse whisper. The negative partitive expression indicates total voice loss.
- 'Did the cortisone have no effect then?' Gabriel asks about the prescribed anti-inflammatory drugs. The negative compound past verb expresses surprise at medical failure.
- 'Is the doctor stopping you for a week?' Gabriel hears the medical verdict with dismay. The transitive verb denotes official sick leave from filming.
- 'Is the American shoot going to stop too?' Andrea immediately grasps the catastrophic consequences. The near-future verbal construction anticipates an immediate work freeze.
- 'Yes Andrea, the film is totally suspended.' Gabriel confirms the grim reality. The passive past participle indicates an indefinite pause on set.
- 'A shutdown costs millions of dollars.' Andrea calculates the staggering financial damages. The present tense verb introduces the huge monetary stakes.
- 'Insurances generally reimburse these unforeseen costs.' Gabriel tries to reassure Andrea. The present verb describes typical indemnification policies.
- 'The companies love to nitpick.' Andrea points out that insurers scrutinize claims. The colorful French idiom describes finding fault in minor details.
- 'Why should they worry about this flu?' Gabriel struggles to see any immediate suspicion. The interrogative modal construction questions the risk of an audit.
- 'Isabelle was shooting a second film at night!' Andrea exposes their blatant contractual fraud. The imperfect verb highlights the exhausting concurrent nocturnal production.
- 'They can pin the blame on us.' Andrea warns that ASK will be held fully liable. The idiomatic causative phrase expresses scapegoating and financial ruin.
- 'Let us not panic before knowing.' Gabriel desperately appeals for rational composure. The first-person plural imperative urges emotional self-control.
- 'The insurances will simply do their normal work.' Gabriel clings to hope that the audit stays routine. The future tense verb anticipates a standard claim settlement.
