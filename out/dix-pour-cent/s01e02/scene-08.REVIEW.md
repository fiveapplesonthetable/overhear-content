## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Everything okay?' Literally 'all goes well?'. You can ask it of a friend, a colleague, or someone fighting with a machine.
- 'It's playing up.' Literally, it is having tantrums. The machine is treated as masculine in this line, so French uses the word also used for he. That follows the gender of the French name for the machine; it does not make it a living person.
- 'No, it's working fine.' The verb here is 'to walk', which in French also means 'to work', for machines and for plans. Then he looks at her copies like an art critic.
- 'The contrast is really good.' Good describes contrast, a masculine word, so it has its masculine shape. Contrast here is the difference between light and dark areas of the copies; he uses the technical comment as an opening to flirt.
- 'You're Camille, right?' The tag at the end, literally 'it's that?', turns any sentence into a check. He uses the casual 'you' straight away, which is very him.
- 'Yes. Do you work here?' She mirrors his familiar you, instead of using the formal version expected with a stranger. Work stays in ordinary statement order and her voice makes it a question.
- 'I've never seen you.' The negative is wrapped around the helper verb: not, have, never, then 'seen'. The little word for 'you' loses its vowel before the verb.
- 'I'm an actor. Hippolyte Rivière.' Notice: no word for 'an'. For a job, French just says 'I am actor'.
- 'Haven't you seen me in Andréa's files?' Hippolyte asks a negative question. And now listen to how Camille answers it.
- 'Yes, I have.' This is a special yes. When someone asks a negative question and you want to say 'yes, actually', you don't use the normal word for yes, you use this one. It means 'oh yes I did'.
- 'Do you have an appointment?' French says have appointment, without a word for an in this fixed wording. Camille is returning to her duties as an assistant, even though she still uses the familiar you.
- 'No, but I'm glad I came.' The word for 'glad' is in the masculine form, because he is a man. Camille stays professional.
- 'What can I do for you?' Listen: now she switches to the polite 'you'. It's her way of keeping him at arm's length. He doesn't take the hint.
- 'Start by relaxing.' Hippolyte gives a casual command: start, then 'by relaxing yourself'.
- 'Will you come for a coffee on the terrace?' Take a coffee means have or drink a coffee here. The ending places it at the outdoor café tables; he is inviting her to leave the office with him.
- 'I'm busy.' Here the word for 'busy' has a feminine ending, because Camille is a woman.
- 'You need an appointment.' This phrase means 'it is necessary', 'one needs'.
- 'Well... thanks anyway.' The last two words mean 'all the same', a polite way to accept a no.
- 'Have a nice day.' Literally good day, as a wish. This word for the day is feminine, which gives good its feminine ending. Hippolyte says it as he leaves after Camille has refused his invitation.
