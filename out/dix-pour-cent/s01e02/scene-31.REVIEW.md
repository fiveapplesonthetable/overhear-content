## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What are you playing at with my son?' The opening is literally at what are you playing. Play can describe behaving in a way someone finds suspicious, as in English playing at. Mathias uses familiar you with Camille, whose family connection he is keeping secret.
- 'I didn't know he was your son.' Camille uses two past forms here for states that lasted: 'I knew' and 'it was'. With the 'not' around the first one.
- 'He didn't tell me.' Camille speaks a short sentence packed with small words: 'me' and 'it' both sit before the action word, inside the 'not'.
- 'You're completely irresponsible!' The long word in the middle means 'completely'. That ending often works like English 'ly'.
- 'You're the one who lies to everybody!' Literally 'it's you who lies'. That's how French puts weight on one person. And 'everybody' is literally 'all the world'.
- 'You're a coward!' The word for coward is a noun after a. It can name a man or a woman without changing its spelling; here the article is masculine because Camille is addressing Mathias.
- 'You never told the truth about us!' Never replaces the usual second negative word after the past helper. About introduces the subject of that truth: the relationship he has kept hidden.
- 'Stay away from my family.' Stay is a command to one familiar person. Far from becomes away from in this warning. Mathias excludes Camille with my family, and her answer challenges that exclusion.
- 'I'm part of your family too!' Literally 'I make part of your family'. The last two words, 'me too', add strong emphasis on who she is.
- 'Have you got a light, Noémie?' Literally, do you have some fire? Fire here means a flame for the cigarette. Noémie says she does not smoke and leaves; Arlette then comes over with a lighter.
- 'But wait... There you go.' Wait is another familiar command, now used to help rather than to warn. There you go accompanies the offer of a light. Arlette's gesture changes the mood after the argument.
- 'Thanks.' One short word acknowledges a small act of help. Camille does not need to name the lighter or explain the favour: Arlette has just seen what she needed.
- 'It's nothing.' Arlette treats the favour as no trouble. The first negative word is absent in this ordinary spoken phrase, but nothing still makes the meaning negative.
- 'Sorrows, men, it all goes up in smoke.' Everything gathers the two topics into one idea. Goes up in smoke is a fixed image for things disappearing. Arlette links it to the cigarette in front of them and offers comfort without asking questions. Let's hear the scene once more.
