## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'And the cause of death?' Françoise uses a rather formal word for death, the kind you see on official papers. Françoise has heard some wild rumours from Rio, and Andréa sets her straight.
- 'He swallowed a wasp.' The helper verb here means to have, and the following past participle carries the action. Together they form the everyday past tense: he swallowed.
- 'Andréa, Samuel is dead.' Said simply, between two people who both loved him.
- 'I've lost a very dear friend.' Literally I have lost. The description very dear follows friend here: dear means loved, rather than expensive.
- 'I know, Françoise. So have I.' The short reply literally means me too. Its opening word is the standalone way to say me; it is different from the I that starts I know.
- 'But I've also lost my agent.' And now we see where this is going: business.
- 'I'm ready to take over.' Ready has a feminine ending because Andréa is a woman. The last expression means taking over from someone else, here as Françoise's agent. You can also use it when relieving someone on a shift.
- 'I don't think I'm going to do it.' Going to do expresses a future plan, much as it does in English. The short word meaning it comes before do and refers back to the film; Françoise means accepting the role.
- 'The shoot starts in three weeks.' Here in places an event ahead of now. The same word can locate something inside a room, but with three weeks it gives the length of the wait, not time already passed.
- 'I need optimism.' Literally 'I have need of optimism'. That's a very useful pattern: 'to have need of' followed by what you need.
- 'Think of Samuel.' Andréa gives a polite command, ending in the formal 'you' form. Andréa tries an emotional argument.
- 'I want to see my daughter, to travel.' After want, both see and travel stay in the infinitive. The possessive my is feminine because daughter is feminine; it does not tell you the parent's gender.
- 'Let's be professional.' This is a 'let's' command, made from the 'we' form of the verb 'to be'. Andréa switches to business.
- 'You have no family. I have a life.' Françoise turns it round on Andréa: you only have your job. Notice 'no family': after a negative, French replaces 'a' or 'some' with the short word that normally means 'of'.
- 'But people are counting on this film!' Count on means depend on someone or something. It is stronger than simply hoping: Andréa means that other people's work relies on Françoise going ahead.
- 'At what age do these whims stop?' The demonstrative these is plural, matching whims. At the end, the pronoun they is attached to the verb with a hyphen to make an inverted question. Calling her grief a whim is the insult.
- 'Sorry.' Andréa realises it immediately. She is a woman, so on paper the word gets an extra e.
- 'Why do I stay with this agency?' Françoise asks a question with no change of word order at all: in speech, just raise your voice at the end.
- 'Yes, it's perfect.' This approves the finished arrangement as a whole. Perfect also works as a short response when someone checks a plan or an order. The florist shows them the wreath, though the agents' relationship is in trouble.
