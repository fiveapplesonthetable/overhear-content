## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I'll see if she can see you.' The first part is the near future: 'I'm going to see'. The polite 'you' at the end is for the actress, a stranger. Then Camille calls out to her boss.
- 'Andréa! One of your actresses is here, Rebecca Vialle.' Notice that Camille uses the polite 'you' with Andréa, and Andréa uses it back. That's our first point today: in this scene, both women stay formal. Then the question.
- 'Can you see her?' The little word for 'her' comes before the verb, not after it, as it always does in French. Andréa looks up.
- 'She's here?' No question word at all, just a rising voice. Camille answers in three words.
- 'In the corridor.' A useful word for any office. Andréa answers with a very pointed question.
- 'Is Rebecca Vialle in my diary today?' The two little words at the start simply turn a sentence into a yes-or-no question. Of course the answer is no. Camille stands up for the actress.
- 'But she's still a client.' The middle words mean 'all the same' or 'still'. Camille has a good heart. Andréa is less sentimental.
- 'She hasn't worked for two years.' Here's our second point, the negative with a past verb: the two little 'not' words go around the helper verb 'has', not around 'worked'. She adds that the actress is just a name on her list. Camille insists.
- 'If we don't help her, she won't find work.' Two negatives in one sentence, and the second one is in the future. Then Camille plays her last card.
- 'She's started crying.' Literally 'she has begun to cry'. Surely that will move Andréa?
- 'Actresses can cry whenever they want.' Not at all. Andréa has seen too many tears in her job. And she changes the subject to something that worries her more.
- 'Did you get the shawl for Françoise Fabian?' It was a soft cashmere shawl, a present to win back the star. Camille is proud of herself.
- 'Yes, it's been sent. Here's the bill.' The last word is the invoice, the paper that shows what it cost. Then Andréa notices something.
- 'You got green?' Literally 'you had some green?'. Her voice already tells us something is wrong. Camille doesn't see it coming.
- 'Yes, a very nice green.' The word for 'nice' here is the one for beautiful, in its masculine form, because the colour word is masculine. And then the bad news.
- 'You don't give green to an actress. It brings bad luck.' In French theatre, green is said to bring bad luck on stage, an old superstition. The little word at the start means 'one' or 'people in general'. Camille realises what she's done.
- 'Oh no!' In the show she says something much ruder. Whatever the words, the shawl is already on its way to Françoise.
