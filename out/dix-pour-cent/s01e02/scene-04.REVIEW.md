## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I swear, it's a wonderful film.' The first part is a little oath, 'I swear to you', that people use all the time to sound convincing. Gabriel sighs with her.
- 'What a waste.' This little word in front of a naming word turns it into an exclamation: what a waste, what a film. Then he tries to cheer her up.
- 'You'll find someone else.' The action word is in the simple future, with that ending you can hear at the end of 'you will find'. Andréa doesn't think it's that easy.
- 'Shooting starts in three weeks.' Shooting here means making a film. The French word refers to the whole production period, not an individual camera shot; in three weeks tells us how soon it begins.
- 'Do you think Mathias is right?' Literally 'you believe that Mathias has reason?'. In French you 'have reason' when you're right. What did Mathias say?
- 'I'm not reassuring for my clients?' Listen to the word for reassuring: it has a feminine ending, because Andréa is talking about herself.
- 'No, no, not at all.' The ending at all makes the negative much stronger. You can use this short answer to deny something completely, although Gabriel goes on to suggest that Andréa could work on her temper.
- 'Dinner's ready!' Literally 'to the table!'. It's what French families shout from the kitchen. On the table there's a framed photo.
- 'Who's this?' Literally 'it's who?'. Putting the question word at the end is very relaxed, everyday French.
- 'Florence, my ex. But that was a long time ago.' The word ex works for a former partner of either gender. My keeps its vowel-friendly masculine shape before this word even when the ex is a woman. He later specifies that the breakup was a little over a year ago.
- 'Why did she leave you?' Here leave takes you as its object: she left a person, not just a place. The little word for you joins the past helper with an apostrophe. Andréa is asking why their relationship ended.
- 'I wanted a child, and she didn't.' 'I wanted' is in the past form used for wishes and feelings that lasted a while. He tries to sound brave.
- 'It's in the past. I've turned the page.' Turning the page is an image for moving on from an experience, just as in English. Gabriel claims the relationship is over emotionally, although he still has Florence's photo on display.
- 'I think I could have a child with you.' Could makes this a possibility, not a settled plan. This is the conditional: it lets Andréa put forward a large, sensitive proposal tentatively. Gabriel initially laughs it off.
- 'That's the wine talking.' Literally, it is the wine that talks. That highlighting pattern points to what is supposedly responsible: Gabriel blames the wine, while Andréa insists she means what she says.
- 'I'm sure you'd be a great dad.' Notice two things here: 'sure' has the feminine ending again, and 'you'd be' is the hypothetical 'would' form of 'to be'. His clients even call him 'Daddy', she says. Then she explains her thinking.
- 'Between an anonymous donor and a friend, I choose the friend.' Between sets up two alternatives, joined by and. The second mention of friend uses the rather than a because it refers back to the friend already introduced.
- 'Is that an offer? I'm touched.' The last word means moved, touched in the heart. A tender moment between two friends who are both a bit lost this week. Here's the whole evening once more.
