## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'That's cheeky of Gabriel.' Cheeky describes someone with the nerve to do something daring or presumptuous. On the part of identifies whose action is being judged; Arlette means Gabriel's casting suggestion.
- 'You think it's cheeky?' The action word here is 'to find', used for opinions, just like 'do you find it cheeky?' Andréa is new, so she doesn't see the problem.
- 'Yes! There's an old story between Line and Françoise.' Three little words at the start mean 'there is'. You'll hear them constantly.
- 'What story?' This question word agrees with the word; story is feminine, so it takes the feminine spelling, though it sounds the same.
- 'They can't stand each other.' Literally 'they can't smell each other'. The little self-directed word makes it mutual: each other. That's the first language point of the scene.
- 'Really? I've never heard that.' The word for 'never' takes the place of the usual 'not' in the two-part negative. Arlette settles in to tell the story.
- 'Line was a well-known singer. Françoise was a young actress.' The past descriptions give the background before the particular events. Both singer and actress are feminine here, and the words describing them take feminine endings too.
- 'Françoise had a boyfriend. Guess who!' Had describes a relationship that existed then. Arlette follows it with a playful invitation to guess the man's identity, rather than naming him immediately.
- 'I don't know.' The classic two-part negative around the verb 'to know'.
- 'Samuel!' The surprise is a name, so it needs no sentence around it. It answers who: Samuel, the agency founder who has just died, was Françoise's partner.
- 'Françoise was with Samuel?' To be 'with' someone means to be their partner, in French as in English. Andréa is amazed.
- 'Yes! And one day, she introduces him to Line.' The little word for him comes before introduces; to then introduces the person meeting him. The present makes that turning point vivid in a story about the past.
- 'Since then, the two women don't speak to each other any more.' The reflexive word again, for 'each other', and the negative with 'any more' at the end.
- 'Line became a star, and Françoise worked in art-house cinema.' Became describes a change in what Line was. The phrase for art-house cinema identifies the kind of career Françoise made, rather than saying she personally directed the films.
- 'Deep down, Françoise would have liked to be a singer.' Would have liked looks back on an unrealised wish. Deep down introduces an underlying feeling, even if the person does not openly admit it.
- 'And Line, a film actress.' Arlette doesn't repeat the verb; we understand it: Line would have liked to be an actress.
- 'Each one hates the other?' Each one has a feminine ending for the two women and still takes a singular action word. The full source question links their hostility to envy: each had the career the other did not.
- 'Exactly.' And that, Arlette says, is why Gabriel's idea is so cheeky. Now the whole story again.
