## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'So I keep seventeen percent of the shares?' The first word means 'so', to sum up what she's heard. Big numbers in French are built like in English: seventeen, then the word for percent.
- 'Yes. You have nothing to do.' The negative here wraps around the verb with a word meaning 'nothing' instead of the usual 'not'. And notice the polite 'you': these two stay formal all evening.
- 'You simply collect the profits.' The verb is the one for 'to touch', and here it means to receive money. It sounds like a gift, but Hélène is not fooled.
- 'How kind of you to think of my retirement.' The first word, which usually means 'like', here turns the sentence into an exclamation. She is being sarcastic.
- 'We've made a huge effort, Hélène.' Here is the everyday past tense: 'we have' plus the past form of 'to make'. That's our first grammar point, and you'll hear it all through this scene.
- 'Andréa has taken out a twenty-year loan.' The past tense again: 'has' plus 'taken'. The agents are putting their own money on the table.
- 'And you become the boss of ASK.' She sees straight through him. The word for 'boss' is the ordinary one you'd use for the head of any company.
- 'And in a year, we buy everything back.' French often uses the present tense for a plan in the near future, just like 'we fly out tomorrow' in English.
- 'Have you ever cheated on your wife, Mathias?' Our second grammar point: in a polite, careful question, the verb and 'you' can swap places, joined by a hyphen. The question takes Mathias completely by surprise.
- 'Forgive me, but I don't talk about that.' A polite command, 'forgive me', then a firm negative around the verb 'talk'.
- 'Samuel cheated on me a lot, mostly with actresses.' The past tense once more. There's an extra letter on the end of the past form, silent, because the person cheated on is a woman: Hélène herself.
- 'But you knew that, didn't you?' A different past tense, for something that went on over time. The little 'no?' at the end works like 'didn't you?' in English.
- 'Not really, no.' Mathias tries to look innocent.
- 'I'll tell you something.' The near future: 'I am going' plus 'to tell'. The little word in the middle means 'to you', and it sits before the verb.
- 'I hate actresses.' Short and brutal, for a woman whose husband ran a talent agency full of them.
- 'Sorry, Mathias, but I have to look at the other offers.' The word for 'sorry' takes its feminine spelling because Hélène is a woman, though it sounds the same. Then 'I must' plus an infinitive.
- 'The bill, please.' A sentence worth knowing for any restaurant. The last part is the polite 'please'.
- 'Other offers?' Mathias didn't see that coming at all.
- 'Oh, yes. Everyone wants Samuel's place.' The phrase for 'everyone' is literally 'all the world', and it takes a singular verb. In the show she puts it much more darkly, about people jumping on dead bodies.
