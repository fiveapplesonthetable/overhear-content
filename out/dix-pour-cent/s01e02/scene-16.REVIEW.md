## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'All right, darling? You're late.' The word for 'darling' is feminine here, because she's talking to a woman. And Arlette uses the casual 'you' with her younger colleague.
- 'A coffee?' The drink alone, with a word for a, makes a complete offer in context. Damien the waiter does not need to say would you like.
- 'Yes, thanks, Damien.' French thanks can stand alone or follow yes, as here. Andréa adds the waiter's name to make the answer personal rather than just answering the offer.
- 'I slept badly.' The everyday past tense: the verb 'have', then the action. The word for 'badly' sits in the middle.
- 'Were you out partying?' Literally 'did you make the party?'. That's the French way to say you went out and had fun.
- 'Yeah, it was wild!' The first word is the casual 'yes', and the last is a slangy word for 'crazy'. She's being ironic, of course.
- 'I spent the night on my bank's website.' In French it's 'the site of my bank': the owner comes last.
- 'And you?' The strong form of the casual 'you', used after 'and'.
- 'Me too, I did my accounts.' Doing one's accounts means working out income, expenses and available money. My is plural because accounts is plural; the money itself is not being described as plural.
- 'I might as well spend what I have on the agency.' The first word gives the idea 'might as well'. Arlette has made up her mind.
- 'Your life insurance? Are you sure?' The word for 'sure' takes an extra letter, because Arlette is a woman.
- 'I have no children, apart from Jean Gabin.' After a negative, the word for 'some' shrinks to a tiny linking word before the noun. And Jean Gabin, named after a famous French actor, is her dog.
- 'So, what's the point?' Arlette uses a short, fixed expression that literally means 'to what good?'.
- 'You are my whole family.' You is plural here, referring to the people at the agency. Whole has a feminine ending because it describes family, not because every person in the group is a woman.
- 'All of you, and my actors.' All has a plural ending when it stands for the people she means. Compare whole family in the previous line: that has a feminine singular ending because family is one word for the group.
- 'Thank you, Arlette.' A name after thanks directly addresses the person being thanked. There is no extra word for to; Andréa's gratitude answers Arlette's offer of support.
- 'Do you need to pee, Jean Gabin?' Literally 'do you want to do pee-pee?', the way you'd talk to a child, or a dog.
- 'Wait, he's peeing!' Too late. The waiter's command uses the formal 'you', the polite form a waiter uses with customers. So the tender moment ends with a puddle on the terrace. Now the whole breakfast once more, in French.
