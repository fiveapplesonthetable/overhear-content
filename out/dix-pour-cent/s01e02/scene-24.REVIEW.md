## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Good evening.' This greeting suits an evening arrival; it does not depend on whether it is already dark. Hippolyte has come to the agency after the usual working hours.
- 'It's really late.' To talk about the time, French uses 'it is', just like English.
- 'Andréa has already left.' Left uses the helper be in French: literally, Andréa is already left. The silent feminine ending agrees with Andréa. The other verb for leaving something behind, which Hippolyte uses next, works differently.
- 'Yes, sorry.' He apologises for the hour, but he doesn't leave.
- 'Andréa left a script here for me.' Compare: this verb, 'to leave something', takes 'to have' in the past. The little word for 'me' is squeezed in before the verb.
- 'No, she didn't leave anything.' The negative wraps around the helper verb, with the word for 'nothing' as the second half.
- 'Help yourself!' Literally, give yourself pleasure. The short yourself attaches to the command with a hyphen. Camille is using the familiar you here; the original spoken invitation is even more abbreviated.
- 'Thanks, Camille.' A name can follow thanks directly, just as in English. He has found the script, but the conversation continues. Later we see them sitting together in a bar.
- 'Let's suppose there's an invisible axis between you and me.' Let's suppose invites Camille to imagine a line between them. The two people appear as you and me after between. The familiar you makes their conversation sound close and relaxed.
- 'It's a weird thing directors do.' The word for 'thing' here is the casual one, 'thingy'. He is showing off a film trick, framing her with his hands.
- 'When you watch films, love scenes...' When introduces a familiar situation, rather than asking a question about time. Scenes of love uses the short word for of before love; the two nouns stay separate words.
- 'They really kiss.' The short sound attached to kiss means each other. Both people take part in the kiss. Really stresses that the action happens, rather than being only a camera trick.
- 'No!' Camille doesn't believe a word of it.
- 'Yes, they do it for real.' To contradict a 'no', French has a special 'yes', different from the usual one. Then the expression 'for real'.
- 'They do it like that?' Camille asks a question made just with her voice going up. The words for 'like that' are worth learning as a pair.
- 'More or less.' More and less join with or to make an approximate answer. Here more is positive: it is not the expression meaning no longer. Hippolyte uses the answer to continue their flirtation.
