## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Gabi! He wants me!' Line uses a pet name for Gabriel, then a tiny sentence where the little word for 'me' goes before the action word. Gabor wants her for the part.
- 'I've just spent two hours with Gabor.' Come followed by the short linking word and spend means have just spent. The two hours describe how long the meeting lasted, not a time on the clock.
- 'We start shooting in three weeks, children!' The word for 'we' at the start is the everyday one. She calls them 'children', with affection.
- 'Thank you, Gabi. My little miracle!' Little agrees with the masculine word miracle. Here it adds affection, rather than describing Gabriel's size. Line credits him with getting her the meeting.
- 'So what do we look like now?' Literally 'what impression do we make'. Andréa now has two stars for one part, and she is furious.
- 'People who understand Gabor's films.' Gabriel answers with a straight face. The word for 'who' links the description to the people.
- 'Nobody asked you to step in.' The negative with 'nobody' comes first here, at the start of the sentence.
- 'I wanted to help you. You were panicking.' Gabriel uses two action words in the past for ongoing states: what he wanted, what she was doing.
- 'This part is for Françoise Fabian!' This points to the particular role they are arguing over. For names the person Andréa believes should get it: her own client, Françoise.
- 'Then go and tell Françoise the part is taken!' Go is a command to one familiar person. Tell follows it directly, and to introduces the person who should hear the news. Taken means the role has already been filled.
- 'Gabriel, I'm not playing! Do what I say!' Play means treating the situation as a game, so not playing insists she is serious. The next French statement literally says you do what I say. In this tone it gives an order without changing the ordinary wording.
- 'I'm not at your command!' At your orders means at your command. Gabriel rejects the idea that Andréa can order him about. The two negative words surround am; he is making a statement about their relationship.
- 'You don't need anyone, Andréa!' Literally 'you have need of nobody'. The phrase for 'to need' is built with the verb 'to have'.
- 'Well, not you and your stupid ideas, anyway!' The word for 'your' is the casual one, plural here, to match 'ideas'.
- 'And when you want a child, find someone else!' The first verb is in the future, because it's about later; the second is a command. It's a low blow: at dinner the other night, Andréa said she could imagine having a child with him.
- 'Exactly!' Andréa agrees with Gabriel's final rejection instead of trying to soften it. The same word can answer a factual question or confirm an argument; her delivery makes the agreement bitter. Here's the whole scene once more.
