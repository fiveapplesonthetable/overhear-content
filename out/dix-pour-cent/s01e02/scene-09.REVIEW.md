## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Samuel was an excellent agent.' The action word is 'to be' in the past form used for describing how things were. Then she gets to the point.
- 'But big parts are rare for a woman my age.' Literally, of my age. Big describes important roles rather than their physical size. My follows the gender of age, which is masculine, even though Line herself is a woman.
- 'I want movement, action, energy!' Notice the little words in front of each noun. They mean 'some': French puts them in where English puts nothing. The form changes with the noun: masculine, or shortened before a vowel.
- 'Find me something powerful!' Line gives a casual command, with 'me' stuck on after a hyphen.
- 'Do you follow me?' Follow means understand the thought here, just as in English. The action word has the same spelling as am after I, but here the subject you and the sense make it follow, not be.
- 'Yes. Line, we have a great project right now.' The little word for 'we' in everyday French is the same one that can mean 'people' in general.
- 'I thought of you.' And here's something to notice: Gabriel says the polite 'you' to her. She calls him the casual way, he stays respectful. A younger agent with a legend, that feels right.
- 'Really?' The same word can strengthen a description, as in really good, or stand alone to ask if something is true. Line's rising voice makes it a surprised question.
- 'It's a film by Gabor Rajevski, with Michel Piccoli.' By introduces the film's director, while with introduces a member of the cast. French uses different short linking words for those two relationships.
- 'I adore Michel!' The word for 'I' loses its vowel and sticks to the verb, because the verb starts with a vowel: a very common French sound.
- 'It's the story of an old couple who do hold-ups.' Couple is singular in French, so the following action uses the singular form too, even though two people are involved. A hold-up here is an armed robbery, not a delay.
- 'Wonderful!' Line exclaims with an easy word that sounds just like the English 'magnificent'.
- 'Why did you think of me?' The past of 'think' is made with 'have' plus a form of the verb, just like 'I have thought'. Gabriel has to think fast.
- 'Because the part is powerful, full of life!' Full is followed by of and what fills the part: life. The image describes a vivid, energetic role, rather than simply saying it is a long or important one.
- 'And shooting starts in three weeks.' The little word before 'three weeks' means 'in', counting forward from now.
- 'Three weeks? Why so soon?' So strengthens soon here. The same little word can also mean if in a condition or yes when contradicting a negative, but neither of those meanings fits this question.
- 'At first, Françoise Fabian was supposed to take the part.' The verb 'must' in this past form means 'was supposed to'. Now the truth is out, or half of it.
- 'She was just a bit tired.' The word for 'tired' has a feminine ending for Françoise. It's a polite little lie.
- 'Line Renaud in a Gabor Rajevski film?' She gives the director's name after the short word of, where English can place the name before film. She is imagining how that credit would sound on a poster.
- 'I think that sounds really good.' Literally 'I find that it sounds'. Using 'find' for 'think' is very common in opinions.
- 'Why not?' Two little words that leave the idea open. Now he only has to tell Andréa. Lovely. Let's hear the whole lunch again.
