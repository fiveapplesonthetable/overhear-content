## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I work until ten at night every day.' On the twenty-four-hour clock, ten at night is twenty-two hours.
- 'It's crazy.' A casual word, very common among young people.
- 'I'm exhausted after only ten days.' The word for exhausted takes a feminine ending, because Camille is a woman.
- 'How do you all manage to have a life?' Here the polite-looking 'you' is really the plural: she means all of the assistants. Hervé's answer is short.
- 'We don't have one.' The little word in the middle stands for 'of it', here 'of a life'. Then Camille admits something personal.
- 'I've been on my own for three months.' French uses the present tense with this word for 'since' or 'for': literally 'I am alone since three months'. In the show she puts it far more bluntly.
- 'Three months, Camille! It's unhealthy.' Hervé, who is gay and loves to tease, is outraged on her behalf.
- 'And I'm not going to meet anyone here.' The near future again, made negative: the two little negative words go around 'to go'.
- 'I'm walking up. Are you coming?' Literally 'I go up on foot'. Hervé takes the stairs, and they use the casual 'you', like all the assistants.
- 'No way!' Literally 'out of the question'. Camille takes the lift, and Mathias steps in.
- 'What's going to happen now?' She means now that Samuel is dead. Listen: she uses the casual 'you' with Mathias, as she does in private.
- 'I don't know. We're waiting for the lawyer.' They are waiting to hear Samuel's last wishes.
- 'Don't be so familiar.' Literally 'don't call me the casual you'. French has a verb that means 'to say the casual you to someone', and Mathias uses it in the negative, with the polite form himself. At work, he wants her to keep her distance.
- 'We're alone here.' The word for 'alone' is plural, because there are two of them.
- 'Camille!' Just her name, said as a warning.
- 'Have I done something wrong?' The two words at the start simply announce a question. Then the everyday past tense: 'I have done'.
- 'Apart from getting a job here?' That's what bothers him: she is working in his own agency.
- 'I'm just doing my job. I don't even look at you.' The word for 'even' sits right after the negative.
- 'Even I don't know that I'm your daughter!' A bitter joke: she plays along with the secret so well that she has almost forgotten it herself.
