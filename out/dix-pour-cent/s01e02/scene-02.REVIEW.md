## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I work until ten at night every day.' On the twenty-four-hour clock, ten at night is twenty-two hours.
- 'It's crazy.' Camille uses a casual word, very common among young people.
- 'I'm exhausted after only ten days.' The word for exhausted takes a feminine ending, because Camille is a woman.
- 'How do you all manage to have a life?' Here you means the assistants as a group. The wording literally asks how you do it in order to have a life: she wants to know how they manage, rather than what they are doing.
- 'We don't have one.' The short word between the negative and have refers back to a life. English says one here. Reusing that small word lets Hervé answer without repeating the whole question.
- 'I've been on my own for three months.' French uses the present tense with this word for 'since' or 'for': literally 'I am alone since three months'. In the show she puts it far more bluntly.
- 'Three months, Camille! It's unhealthy.' Unhealthy contains a beginning meaning bad and an ending meaning healthy. Hervé is exaggerating to tease Camille, rather than giving her medical advice.
- 'And I'm not going to meet anyone here.' The near future again, made negative: the two little negative words go around 'to go'.
- 'I'm walking up. Are you coming?' Literally 'I go up on foot'. Hervé takes the stairs, and they use the casual 'you', like all the assistants.
- 'No way!' Literally 'out of the question'. Camille takes the lift, and Mathias steps in.
- 'What's going to happen now?' The opening what asks about the thing that will happen. There is no you in this line: Camille is asking about the agency after Samuel's death.
- 'I don't know. We're waiting for the lawyer.' Waiting for takes its object directly in French: there is no extra word corresponding to the English for. The lawyer will explain Samuel's last wishes.
- 'Don't be so familiar.' Literally 'don't call me the casual you'. French has a verb that means 'to say the casual you to someone', and Mathias uses it in the negative, with the polite form himself. At work, he wants her to keep her distance.
- 'We're alone here.' The word for 'alone' is plural, because there are two of them.
- 'Camille!' Just her name, used as a warning rather than a greeting. His sharp tone tells her to drop the subject; the French spelling gives the name a final sound like the y in yes.
- 'Have I done something wrong?' The two opening words turn the statement into a yes-or-no question, without changing the order of the rest. Something is literally some thing, followed by wrong.
- 'Apart from getting a job here?' Apart from introduces the exception that Mathias holds against her. The French phrase describes a job she has already found. He objects to her working in his own agency.
- 'I'm just doing my job. I don't even look at you.' Just means only here. The ending not even strengthens the denial; even sits before the final negative word, rather than meaning the same.
- 'Even I don't know that I'm your daughter!' The opening means I myself and adds emphasis to the I that follows. Daughter and girl share one French word; your daughter makes the family meaning clear. Camille's bitter joke exposes how hard he is making her hide their relationship.
