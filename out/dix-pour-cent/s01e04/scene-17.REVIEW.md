## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Did you want to see me?' She uses the past tense for 'wanted', which sounds polite and tentative.
- 'Yes. Do you know Hippolyte Rivière?' The action word asks about being acquainted with a person. It is different from the word used for knowing how to do an activity.
- 'Yes, I know him.' The little word before the verb means 'him'.
- 'Come in. He has been chosen for a detective series.' Literally 'a police series'.
- 'We need photos of him as a child.' This everyday expression for 'to need' literally means 'to have need of'.
- 'Go with him and help him choose.' Andréa gives two polite commands, each with the word for 'him' joined to the action word by a hyphen.
- 'When? Next Monday?' In French, 'next' comes after the day of the week.
- 'No, Camille, now!' The last word is the everyday time word for 'now'. Andréa contrasts it with Camille's suggestion of next Monday.
- 'Okay.' The short agreement can confirm that you accept an instruction. Here Camille agrees to leave with Hippolyte straight away.
- 'It's great, to be chosen for that.' The plain form 'to be' is followed by the past form meaning 'chosen'. The same pair can describe receiving a part rather than choosing someone else.
- 'Yes, I'm pleased.' Notice the masculine form of 'pleased' for Hippolyte.
- 'Is it a good part?' The short word meaning 'good' goes before the role. It is an adjective here, unlike the conversational 'right' used to move on to a new topic.
- 'Not bad. I play a boy who has a broken heart.' Literally 'the broken heart'.
- 'Sorry about your messages. I've been very busy.' He switches to the casual 'your' with her, as he has since they met.
- 'But it's good to see you.' The short object for 'you' sits before 'see'. It is the casual form for one person, showing how differently Hippolyte and Andréa address Camille.
- 'We're going on my scooter.' The short place word before 'go' is part of a very common expression for setting off. Then 'on' introduces the vehicle they will use.
- 'You're not afraid?' In French you 'have fear' rather than 'are afraid'.
