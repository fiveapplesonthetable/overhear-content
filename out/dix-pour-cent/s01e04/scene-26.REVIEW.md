## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What are we going to say to him?' This long question phrase at the start means 'what', followed by 'we are going to say to him'.
- 'He helps us, or we stop sending our clients here.' After the verb for 'to stop', French puts the little word for 'of' before the next plain verb.
- 'As usual.' Literally 'like of habit'. A very common everyday phrase.
- 'Oh no! Look!' The second word is a casual command for one person. It draws Gabriel's attention to something Andréa has suddenly noticed.
- 'What? Who is there?' Gabriel asks two short question words: 'what?' and 'who is there?'.
- 'It's Brancillon! She already knows everything about the room!' The time word means 'already': the information may have reached her sooner than they hoped. The next word stands alone for 'everything'.
- 'Hello, Ms Brancillon.' A surname can follow the polite title for a woman. Gabriel switches from discussing her with Andréa to addressing her directly.
- 'It's not what you think.' Andréa tries to explain the apparent intimacy. French uses the expression for what someone believes to refer to their mistaken interpretation.
- 'Leave it.' Gabriel gives up trying to explain. The two actions literally mean letting something fall, and together form an everyday expression for dropping a subject.
- 'Hello. It's none of my business.' Literally 'that does not look at me'. Colette stays cool and polite.
- 'Come on!' Gabriel urges Andréa to move with him. The short final action is a casual command to one person.
- 'I'd like to see Mr Audibert, please.' Colette uses the polite conditional form 'I would like' at the desk.
- 'Our room, please.' The possessive for 'our' agrees with the room, which is singular. It does not become plural just because two people supposedly share it.
- 'The same as usual. Thank you.' Literally 'the same as of habit'. Now they have no choice but to go upstairs.
