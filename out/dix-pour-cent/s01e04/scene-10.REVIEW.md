## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Andréa! Brancillon came to my place at seven in the morning!' The verb 'to come' uses 'to be' in the past tense, so 'came' has a feminine ending for Brancillon.
- 'What? To your place?' The little preposition for 'at someone's home' followed by 'you'.
- 'To check my suits and my expense claims. Good luck!' Literally 'notes of expenses'. Gabriel warns her that she's next.
- 'Colette? Hello!' Andréa addresses her by name before the greeting. The tax inspector is checking records in Andréa's office while Andréa tries to charm her.
- 'Do you want to have dinner with me tonight?' Andréa stays on the polite 'you' and invites her out.
- 'Stop it, Andréa. I know who you are.' Colette keeps polite address while rejecting the invitation. The first action is a command; the last is the matching polite form of being.
- 'Really?' Andréa replies with two short words that mean 'oh really?' or 'is that so?'.
- 'Yes. I see everything in your bills.' The word after 'see' stands alone for 'everything'. It is different from the longer expression for 'everyone', which adds the words for 'the world'.
- 'What?' Andréa is caught off guard.
- 'Everything. You eat very badly, and you never take a holiday.' The negative wraps around 'take' with the word for 'never'.
- 'You pay for a gym, but you never go there.' The short place word before 'go' replaces the gym. It is joined to the first part of the negative, while 'never' comes after the action.
- 'And you have a problem with your hair.' Plural possessive before 'hair', because 'hair' on your head is plural in French.
- 'No!' A complete protest can be just this one word. Colette immediately contradicts it with the other kind of French 'yes'.
- 'Yes, you do. Always a different hairdresser.' Colette contradicts the denial. The short second sentence leaves out an action because the pattern of changing hairdressers is already clear.
- 'And you have lots of girlfriends.' Literally 'little friends' in the feminine.
- 'That's false.' The describing word after 'it is' means 'false' or 'untrue'. It stays masculine here because it describes the whole claim, not Andréa herself.
- 'Anyway, you're not someone for me.' This three-word phrase at the start means 'in any case' or 'anyway'.
