## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Morning! Hi, Mathias.' The first greeting works with strangers as well as colleagues. The second is more familiar; listen for how short it is.
- 'Yes, three hundred thousand.' A number before 'hundred' can multiply it. The following word means 'thousand', so the whole amount is three hundred thousand.
- 'I'm talking about his twenty years in the business.' The number comes first, followed by 'years'. The possessive refers to the actor's career, rather than to the agent speaking.
- 'Magali, I have to see the Koltes play this week.' The word meaning 'have to' comes before the action 'see'. You can reuse that pair with another action you need to do.
- 'Two tickets for Tuesday?' A 'place' can mean a seat or ticket at a theatre. Here the two places are for the play.
- 'On Tuesday you've got the Kechiche film.' A day named on its own can identify the coming appointment. There is no separate French word corresponding to English 'on' here.
- 'Thursday, then.' The last word can help someone draw a conclusion. A new day has solved the first diary clash.
- 'And tomorrow you're having lunch with Audiard.' French has one word for 'have lunch'. Adding 'tomorrow' lets this present form describe a planned event.
- 'I'll take that. Thanks.' The present form of 'take' suits a decision made right now. The small word after it points to the thing being offered.
- 'You're also having lunch with Dany Boon tomorrow.' The extra word meaning 'also' reveals the problem. Both lunches are on the same day.
- 'You're a pain, Magali.' The adjective can describe something difficult or tiresome. Applied to a person here, it sounds like an irritated complaint.
- 'One at noon, the other at two.' The pair 'one' and 'the other' separates the two appointments. Fourteen hours on the clock means two in the afternoon.
- 'I'm going to have lunch twice.' A form of 'go' followed by an action gives a plan for the future. The last two words literally say 'two times'.
- 'Call Clementine.' An instruction can begin straight with the action, without a separate word for 'you'. This one addresses a familiar person.
- 'Tell her I can't come tonight.' In this instruction the little word for 'to her' attaches after 'tell'. In an ordinary statement it would go before the action.
- 'Clementine? The one from last week?' The expression for 'the one' is feminine because it refers to a woman. The word for 'last' also has a feminine form beside 'week'.
- 'Is there another Clementine?' French uses a short expression meaning 'there is'. Here the rising question asks whether a second person with that name exists.
- 'What do I tell her?' The opening question expression asks about the message. The small word for 'to her' identifies who will receive it.
- 'I don't know. Think of something.' The first sentence puts two little negative words around 'know'. The second switches to a direct instruction.
- 'Say I've got a meeting. Improvise!' A meeting is described by a feminine noun, so listen to the little word before it. This is an excuse to invent, rather than a confirmed meeting.
- 'Gabriel. Aren't you calling Cecile de France back?' Calling back and calling use related French words. A short beginning adds the idea of doing it again.
