## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Magali! Have you got an envelope?' Andréa uses the casual 'you' with her assistant, as everyone does at the agency. But Magali isn't fetching envelopes today.
- 'I've had enough. I'm leaving!' The first sentence is a fixed expression for being fed up, worth learning as one block. Andréa doesn't take it seriously at first.
- 'Where are you going?' In everyday speech the question word can come at the very end, after the action word. Then Andréa gives an order.
- 'Don't do this, Magali.' Here's our first language point: commands. With the casual 'you', you simply drop the short word and use the action word on its own. To make it negative, you wrap it in the two little words of 'not'. Magali answers.
- 'I can't take any more, Andréa.' Literally 'I can no more of it'. People say it when they're at the end of their rope. And she says it plainly.
- 'I'm very tired.' Second language point: listen to the last word. Written down, it gets an extra letter because Magali is a woman, but it sounds exactly the same. Andréa has an answer for everything.
- 'Of course you're tired.' The same word for tired, and again in its feminine spelling. Then Andréa shrugs.
- 'We're all tired. It's winter!' This time the word for tired is plural, because it's about everybody. The little word at the start means 'we' in casual French. Now come her famous tips.
- 'Take some royal jelly.' Another command, built from the verb 'to take'. Royal jelly is a health food made by bees, which is a funny cure for a burned-out assistant. Andréa has another idea.
- 'Ride a bike! It gives you energy.' One more casual command, from the verb 'to do'. Magali's answer is not polite at all: she says that if she stays, she might end up hitting Andréa. So Andréa plays the victim.
- 'Is this how you thank me?' Literally 'it's like that that you thank me?'. A typical way to sound hurt. And she reminds Magali of everything she did for her.
- 'I'm the one who trained you!' The pattern 'it's me who' puts the spotlight on Andréa.
- 'I don't want to hear it!' The negative wrap again, around the verb for 'want'. And then, more quietly.
- 'I just want to leave.' The little word in the middle means 'just' or 'only'. That's all she asks. But Andréa will not be left.
- 'You're not leaving. I'm the one firing you!' The same spotlight pattern, 'it's me who'.
- 'You're fired!' Three short words, and the last one has the feminine ending you know by now. Andréa can't stand being left, so she turns Magali's resignation into a firing.
