## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Magali! Have you got an envelope?' Andrea uses the familiar 'you' with her assistant. The noun for envelope is feminine, which chooses the little word meaning 'an'.
- 'I've had enough. I'm leaving!' This first sentence is a fixed expression for being fed up, worth learning as one block. Andréa doesn't take it seriously at first.
- 'Where are you going?' In everyday speech the question word can come at the very end, after the action word. Then Andréa gives an order.
- 'Don't do this, Magali.' This instruction starts with the negative, without a separate word for 'you'. The command uses the same short form of 'do' that you hear with familiar 'you' in a statement.
- 'I can't take any more, Andréa.' Literally 'I can no more of it'. People say it when they're at the end of their rope. And she says it plainly.
- 'I'm very tired.' The description has an extra written letter because Magali is a woman. Here that extra letter does not change the sound.
- 'Of course you're tired.' The opening two-word expression means 'of course'. Andrea agrees with the description while dismissing Magali's complaint.
- 'We're all tired. It's winter!' The short subject word means 'we' in conversation but takes a singular form of 'be'. The description of the group is plural.
- 'Take some royal jelly.' This command comes from 'take'. The words after it name an unspecified amount of royal jelly, a substance produced by bees that Andrea offers as advice.
- 'Ride a bike! It gives you energy.' French literally says 'do some bicycle' for cycling. The next sentence uses a plural word for strength, although English naturally says 'energy'.
- 'Is this how you thank me?' Literally 'it's like that that you thank me?'. A typical way to sound hurt. And she reminds Magali of everything she did for her.
- 'I'm the one who trained you!' The opening puts the spotlight on Andrea. In writing, 'trained' agrees with the woman represented by the small object word placed before 'have'.
- 'I don't want to hear it!' The negative wrap again, around the verb for 'want'. And then, more quietly.
- 'I just want to leave.' The little word in the middle means 'just' or 'only'. That's all she asks. But Andréa will not be left.
- 'You're not leaving. I'm the one firing you!' The first sentence denies Magali's decision. The second repeats the emphatic pattern from 'I'm the one who trained you', this time with the action 'fire'.
- 'You're fired!' The last word describes Magali and has the feminine ending. As with 'tired', that extra written letter makes no change to its sound here.
