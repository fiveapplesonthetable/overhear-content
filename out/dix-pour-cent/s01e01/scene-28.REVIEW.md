## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Quentin has thought it over.' The past action uses 'have' and a short past form ending in a clear vowel sound. The thought is complete before this meeting begins.
- 'He really wants you.' The short object meaning 'you' comes before 'wants'. The final word strengthens Mathias's reassurance.
- 'Thanks, that's great!' The last word sounds just like its English cousin, and the French use it all the time for anything they like. But then comes the catch.
- 'But he wants a younger woman.' This phrase for 'younger' is 'more young': the word for 'more' goes before the adjective, a simple way to compare. Mathias spells it out.
- 'If you want the part, you have to change.' The second half uses the verb 'must'. Cécile doesn't see what he means.
- 'I don't understand.' Notice the two little negative words around the verb. Keep that in mind; there's a variation at the end of the scene.
- 'You're lucky.' Literally 'you have some luck'. French often uses 'to have' where English uses 'to be'.
- 'You just need to have your cheeks done.' The first two words mean 'it is necessary', a very handy way to say what has to happen, without saying who. Cécile can't believe it.
- 'An operation? Are you serious?' She asks with her voice rising, no special question words needed. Mathias says no, not surgery, something else.
- 'No. It's lipofilling.' The small word before the treatment name introduces an unspecified amount. Mathias gives it a technical name to make the proposal sound less alarming.
- 'It's totally different.' The long word before 'different' means 'completely'. It strengthens the producer's claim rather than naming a different procedure.
- 'Sorry, I'm not touching my face.' The word for 'sorry' has a feminine ending, because Cécile is a woman.
- 'Wait, Cécile. Let's listen to Miranda.' This second command ends in the sound for 'let's', which means 'we' are doing it together. Miranda makes it sound like nothing.
- 'It's so simple.' The middle word means 'so' or 'really', and it makes anything stronger.
- 'And there you go, it's done. Baby face!' The first short expression presents a finished result, like 'there you are'. Miranda makes the change sound quick and easy.
- 'No, it's easy to say done.' She throws Miranda's own word back at her. Then she says what she's seen around her.
- 'They start with Botox, then they don't stop anymore.' The second negative word means 'anymore', replacing ordinary 'not'. Here 'they' refers to other women having cosmetic treatments.
- 'Do you want to play the immortal woman?' The character's title is feminine, so the article and its ending refer to a woman. The part is the one Cecile still hopes to play.
- 'Yes, of course, but…' The final small word promises a qualification. Cecile leaves it unfinished because she is still unsure.
- 'I don't know anymore.' Compare it with 'I don't know', which would end with the usual negative word. Here, the word for 'anymore' takes its place. One small swap, and you go from not knowing to no longer knowing. Here's the scene again from start to finish.
