## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Tough day?' Just two words, no verb: the noun for 'day' and the adjective for 'difficult'. Cécile answers politely.
- 'Not tough. Just very strange.' Her word means 'peculiar' or 'unusual', and it has a feminine ending because it describes the day, which is a feminine word in French. Camille sympathises.
- 'Me too.' The short word for 'me' is the one that can stand alone. Adding 'also' gives the everyday response 'me too'.
- 'This Tarantino film...' The small word before 'film' points to a particular film. French links it to the director's name with 'of'.
- 'I understand.' The familiar sound at the end belongs to the present form used with 'I'. Camille means to show sympathy, but she has misunderstood what Cecile knows.
- 'It's a real blow.' Literally 'a hard hit': bad news that knocks you over. Cécile senses something.
- 'Meaning?' Literally 'that is to say?'. It's how you ask someone to explain what they just said.
- 'You're not missing much.' The ordinary two negative words remain around 'lose'. The final expression means 'much', so the literal sense is that Cecile is not losing much.
- 'What are you talking about?' Word for word: of what you speak? Cécile keeps her voice calm.
- 'About your age!' The opening word continues the idea 'talk about' from Cecile's question. The polite possessive matches the way Camille addresses the star.
- 'You're naturally beautiful.' The description has the feminine form for Cecile. The final adverb says that her beauty is natural, without cosmetic work.
- 'Thank you. Go on!' The command uses the polite form, just as Camille's questions do. It asks her to continue speaking.
- 'American actresses all have the same facelift.' Notice the endings: the actresses are feminine and plural, so 'American' and 'all' take feminine plural endings too.
- 'They say you're too old for the part.' The description of age has a special feminine form for Cecile. The small word in front means 'too', and makes the criticism explicit.
- 'But it's so stupid!' The adverb before 'stupid' strengthens the description to 'so stupid'. Camille is rejecting the judgement, although saying it aloud still hurts.
- 'Ah. Too old.' Cecile repeats the same description without the rest of the sentence. The short response shows that she has understood the reason for the rejection.
- 'I hope I'm like you at your age.' After 'hope', French uses the plain action 'be' when the hoped-for person is still the speaker. English can instead repeat 'I' in a new clause.
- 'Thank you. Goodbye.' The farewell literally refers to seeing someone again. It is a standard polite way to leave, even after an uncomfortable conversation.
- 'Goodbye!' Camille returns the same farewell. Her polite words cannot undo the news she has accidentally revealed.
