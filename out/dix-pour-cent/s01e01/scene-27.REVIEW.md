## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Unlucky me!' Literally 'no luck for me'. It's sarcastic: Camille is the one about to be in trouble. Then Andréa explains.
- 'I find an assistant I like.' Word for word, 'an assistant who pleases me'. That's how French says 'I like her': the person does the pleasing. And then the twist.
- 'But I was wrong.' Listen to the ending of the last word. Andréa is a woman, so the word gets a silent extra letter, the feminine ending. You won't hear it, but you'll see it written. Now she turns on Camille.
- 'Do you realise what you did?' The small expression after 'realise' introduces 'what you did'. The past action uses a form of 'have' followed by 'done'.
- 'Did you want a selfie? An autograph?' The past form of 'want' describes the motive Andrea imagines. Both souvenir nouns are masculine here.
- 'We almost lost a big star!' The word for 'almost' sits between the two parts of the action word, just like 'we have almost lost'. Camille tries to defend herself.
- 'Yes, but Cecile stayed with the agency.' This past action uses 'be' as its helping verb. The final written letter of 'stayed' agrees with Cecile.
- 'No, it isn't like that.' Here's the main language point of this scene. To say 'not', French wraps the action word in two little words, one before and one after. The first one loses its vowel before 'is'. Listen to it again in the next line.
- 'I'm not a chatterbox.' Same pattern: the two little negative words hug the verb 'am'. The word for 'chatterbox' ends in the feminine form, because Camille is talking about herself. Andréa doesn't want to hear it.
- 'Get your things.' Three words, and Camille is fired. It's a casual command, said to one person. Hervé, at the next desk, tries to soften the blow.
- 'It was short, but intense.' The two descriptions are linked by the small word 'but'. The first description has a different feminine form, although this sentence uses its masculine form.
- 'But there's a bright side.' Literally 'there is a positive side'. The opening three words mean 'there is', and you hear them everywhere in French.
- 'You're far from this business.' Hervé means she has escaped a nasty world. The word for business is simply the English one.
- 'I really mean it.' Literally 'I think it really'. Now Hervé turns to Noémie, who hasn't looked up from her screen.
- 'Noémie, say goodbye to Camille!' Another casual command, from the verb 'to say'. Noémie mumbles that she's too busy, and Camille covers for her.
- 'It doesn't matter.' Literally 'it isn't serious', with the same two negative words around the verb. It's what French people say all day long when something is no big deal.
- 'She's in a hurry.' One more feminine ending: Noémie is a woman, so the adjective takes the extra letter. Camille picks up her bag.
- 'Well, goodbye.' The first word is a little sigh that means 'right then'. Hervé answers warmly.
- 'Goodbye, Camille. Good luck!' This second phrase literally means 'good courage', and it's how you wish someone strength for something hard. And Camille leaves on a hopeful note.
- 'I'm sure we'll see each other again.' The description 'sure' has a feminine spelling for Camille. The action at the end points to a future meeting and includes the idea of seeing each other again.
