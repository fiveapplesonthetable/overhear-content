## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Where are you?' The question word comes last in this everyday spoken question. The polite word for 'you' also fits the relationship between an agent and his client.
- 'I'm at my horse-riding lesson.' The possessive 'my' goes with a masculine noun meaning 'lesson'. It tells us about the lesson, rather than the speaker's gender.
- 'We're at the Westin.' The first word can mean 'we' in conversation, but it takes the same verb form as 'he' or 'she'. Listen to the short sound after it.
- 'Everyone is here.' The three-word expression for 'everyone' literally says 'all the world'. French treats that expression as singular.
- 'Oh no! I'm late.' French uses a little expression meaning 'in lateness' where English says 'late'. Keep those last two words together when listening.
- 'You can ride, can't you?' The word for 'know' followed by an action can mean knowing how to do it. The little 'no' at the end asks for reassurance.
- 'Yes, yes. I just need a little lesson.' French says 'have need of' where English simply says 'need'. The short word meaning 'just' makes the request sound smaller.
- 'Can I trust you?' The French expression literally says 'make trust'. The little word meaning 'you' comes before that expression, although English puts 'you' last.
- 'I've almost got it.' The expression at the end means being ready or having something worked out. Saying 'almost' leaves a little room for doubt.
- 'I'll call you back.' French uses the present here for an immediate promise. The person being called is named before the action.
- 'When will you be here?' This time the form of 'be' points to the future. Again the question word waits until the end.
- 'In an hour.' The short word at the start locates a moment in the future. It does not say how long the journey lasts.
- 'I'm sorry. Really sorry.' The written apology has an extra e for a woman. That extra letter makes no difference to the pronunciation here.
- 'Are you making a film in the United States?' In this setting the French word for 'turn' means shooting a film. It is a useful second meaning to recognise.
- 'Nothing is signed. We're waiting for the call.' The French equivalent of 'nothing' works with the small negative before 'is'. There is no extra word for 'not' after it.
- 'It's Tarantino's next film.' French puts 'next' before the thing described. The director's name then follows a word meaning 'of'.
- 'Fantastic!' This familiar word expresses the journalist's delight. Meanwhile, the agent receives some bad news.
- 'Tarantino won't cast Cecile.' The two short negative words surround the future action. In this film conversation, 'take' means choosing someone for a part.
- 'A problem with her age.' The preposition meaning 'of' loses its vowel before 'age'. Written together, they still contain two pieces.
- 'It's like a dream for me.' The final word is the form of 'me' used after a word such as 'for'. She still has no idea about the bad news.
