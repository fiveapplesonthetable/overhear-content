## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Cécile! Are you okay? Can we talk?' This first question is just two short words, and it's the most common check-in there is. The second one uses the little word for 'we' that the French say all the time in conversation.
- 'Come into my office.' It's a casual command, the kind you give someone you know well. Mathias wants a quiet room for this.
- 'It's not a big drama, Cécile. It's simple.' Notice the two little words wrapped around the verb: that's how French says 'not'. One goes before the verb and one comes right after it.
- 'I know, Mathias.' The action here means knowing a fact, rather than knowing a person. Cecile is already familiar with the subject.
- 'I'm an actress.' There's no word for 'an' here: in French, you just say 'I am actress' when you talk about your job.
- 'My friends talk about it all the time.' The small word before 'talk' stands for 'about it'. French can refer back to the treatment without repeating its name.
- 'It's easy, but I didn't think I'd do that one day.' The past form of 'think' describes her earlier expectation. She then uses a plain action form for what she never imagined doing.
- 'Look. That's my wife.' Another casual command, and a surprise: he's going to use his own wife as the example.
- 'How old would you say she is?' Literally 'what age do you give her?'. The small word before the verb means 'to her'. And Mathias uses the casual 'you' with Cécile, because they've known each other for years.
- 'Forty? Forty-two?' Ages can be guessed with just a number and a rising voice. She says 'years' after the first number, then leaves it understood after the second.
- 'She's fifty-one.' Here's that 'to have' again: literally 'she has fifty-one years'. Much older than Cécile guessed.
- 'Botox every year, and her eyes two years ago.' This phrase at the end, 'there are two years', is how French says 'two years ago'. Mathias lists everything, perfectly calm.
- 'Impressive. It's well done.' The short word meaning 'well' describes how the work was done. French places it before the past form of 'do'.
- 'Doesn't it bother you?' Look at the 'not' again: the two little words now wrap around both the 'you' and the verb. This 'you' is the casual one, like the rest of their chat.
- 'No, quite the opposite.' The two words after 'no' form a useful reply meaning 'on the contrary'. Mathias says the treatment does not bother him.
- 'Does she have a good surgeon?' Cécile asks a question made just by raising her voice at the end. And with it, Cécile is really saying yes.
- 'The best.' This description has its own special form, rather than using 'more good'. Its masculine form refers back to the surgeon.
- 'Miranda? Cécile has said yes.' The action word means 'to accept', in the past, built with 'to have' and the past form of the action.
- 'She can get back in the saddle. Talk soon!' After 'can', the next action stays in its plain form. The saddle expression describes returning to the project as well as echoing Cecile's riding lessons.
