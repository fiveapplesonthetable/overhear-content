## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hello, I'm looking for Cécile de France.' The action word for looking for already contains the 'for', so nothing else is needed before her name. Someone points across the field, and he calls out to her.
- 'We have to talk.' The opening two words mean 'it is necessary', a very common way to say that something must happen. The little word in the middle is the everyday way of saying 'we'.
- 'Gabriel? What are you doing here?' The opening question expression asks 'what'. The final small word points to this place, where she did not expect to see him.
- 'Stop!' Gabriel gives a short instruction as he catches up with the horse. This warning has the same written form in French and English.
- 'I can't!' The two negatives surround 'can'. The action she cannot manage is left unsaid because the runaway horse makes it obvious.
- 'Cécile, you're really bad at this.' Not very kind, but he is hurt. The word for 'bad at' or 'useless' here ends in a feminine way, because he is talking about a woman.
- 'You told me you could ride.' After the past action 'told', another past form describes what she knew how to do. In this horse-riding setting, the final action means 'ride'.
- 'You lied.' Gabriel uses a short past form: the helper word meaning 'to have', then the past form of 'to lie'. Cécile protests.
- 'I didn't lie, Gabriel.' Here is the same negation pattern in the past: the two pieces wrap around the helper word, not around the word for 'lied'. She explains.
- 'I'm soon going to be good enough.' To talk about the near future, take the verb 'to go' and put a plain verb after it, exactly like 'going to' in English. The word for 'good' is feminine again.
- 'Look how bad you are!' The instruction begins directly with 'look'. The description of being bad at something has a feminine form for Cecile.
- 'And you lied to me too!' The small object attached to 'have' means 'to me'. The extra word meaning 'also' turns Gabriel's accusation back on him.
- 'There's lying and there's lying.' Literally 'there is to lie and to lie'. It's his excuse: some lies are worse than others.
- 'I didn't want to hurt you.' Again the negation wraps around the verb, this time a past form for wanting. And at that very moment, the horse decides it has heard enough.
- 'Oh no! He won't stop!' The French expression for 'stop' here includes a short reflexive piece attached with an apostrophe. The same expression works for a person or vehicle coming to a stop.
- 'Wait! Cecile, hold on!' The first instruction uses the polite or plural form. The second addresses Cecile familiarly, with the short piece meaning 'yourself' attached after the action.
- 'Careful, you're heading for the road!' The very first word is the classic warning shout. The argument will have to wait.
