## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Andréa, I don't know what to do.' The action word for 'to know' is wrapped in the two-part negative, and then comes 'what to do'. A line you can use any time you're stuck.
- 'Cecile has left. She wants to change agents.' After 'wants', the action 'change' stays in its plain form. The small word meaning 'of' then links it to 'agent'.
- 'Can't you talk to her?' Notice the casual 'you': Andréa and Gabriel are colleagues and friends.
- 'Me? What can I say to her?' The little word for 'to her' comes just before 'say'. At the beginning, the separate form of 'me' makes Andrea's surprise clear.
- 'I don't know. You're the same age, aren't you?' French says 'you have the same age'. Here 'you' refers to Andrea and Cecile together; Gabriel is comparing two people.
- 'The same age?' The description 'same' comes between 'the' and 'age'. Andrea repeats just that group of words to challenge his comparison.
- 'All old ladies get along, is that it?' Both the word for 'all' and the word for 'old' take the feminine plural form, to match 'ladies'. She is joking, but she is annoyed.
- 'I'm only thirty-four. Thanks a lot!' French again uses 'have' with a number of years. The small word after it limits the age to 'only' thirty-four; her thanks is sarcastic.
- 'Sorry, that's not what I meant.' Literally 'that's not what I wanted to say'. A useful sentence when you need to take something back.
- 'But you're so good at this!' The description means 'gifted' or 'talented'. Its feminine spelling matches Andrea, although the extra written letter is silent.
- 'Listen, give her a bit of space.' Both instructions address a familiar person. In the second, 'to her' attaches after the action with a hyphen.
- 'You'll only annoy her.' The present action 'go' introduces what will happen next. The small object meaning 'her' attaches to the beginning of 'annoy'.
- 'So I don't do anything?' Here the negative pairs with the word for 'nothing'. He sounds like a child being told no.
- 'Wait. Until tomorrow at least.' The opening instruction leaves out a separate word for 'you'. The last group gives a minimum waiting time, rather than a firm appointment.
