## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'So, actually, I'm not really a receptionist.' 'So' introduces Sofia's explanation, and the next short expression means 'in fact'. Before a job description, French can leave out the word meaning 'a'.
- 'It's just a job to make my family happy.' Literally 'to do pleasure to my family'. Then she adds, with a smile...
- 'My banker too.' The first small word, meaning 'to', carries on the previous expression about pleasing someone. The final word adds the banker to her family.
- 'Because actually, I'm an actress.' This noun can mean an actor on stage or screen. It does not mean that Sofia necessarily performs comedy.
- 'This wine is incredibly good!' The long word in the middle is just 'incredible' with an ending that makes it an adverb, like 'ly' in English. He asks her a question.
- 'Have you tasted it?' The small word before the verb stands for 'it', the wine. He's polite: the formal 'you'.
- 'No.' She hasn't touched it; she's too busy pitching.
- 'It's magnificent.' French uses the masculine word for 'he' to refer to the wine, since 'wine' is masculine. English naturally refers to the same thing as 'it'.
- 'Right now, I am acting in a new show.' The verb meaning 'play' also means 'act' in this setting. A short expression at the beginning tells us that she is performing at present.
- 'Porte des Lilas, right.' Gabriel repeats the place name. The small expression at the end signals agreement or acknowledgement, as English 'okay' does.
- 'It would be great if you could come.' The first action is a hypothetical 'would be'. After 'if', French uses a past form of 'can', even though the invitation concerns a future visit.
- 'Here's the flyer.' This handy word means 'here it is', when you hand something over. The French borrow the English word for the leaflet.
- 'It's called Absence.' Literally 'it calls itself Absence', the same pattern French uses to say your own name.
- 'Thank you.' This can acknowledge something someone hands you, as Gabriel does with the flyer. It does not promise that he will attend.
- 'You're not interested?' Sofia forms a question just by raising her voice at the end.
- 'No, it's not that.' The final word points back to Sofia's suggestion that he is uninterested. He denies that explanation without yet giving his own.
- 'I've got a problem at work.' The noun for 'work' is informal. The short word before it combines 'at' with the masculine article 'the'.
- 'A problem at work, Gabriel?' Cecile repeats his words as a question. Naming him at the end makes it clear that she expects an answer.
