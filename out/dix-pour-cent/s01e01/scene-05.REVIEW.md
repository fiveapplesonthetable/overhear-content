## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I'm going on holiday, not retiring.' French uses two different expressions for a holiday and retirement. The holiday noun here normally appears in the plural.
- 'Your first holiday in eight years!' Because the holiday word is plural, the words in front of it are plural too: your, first, all with a plural ending. Samuel thanks everyone and gets down to business.
- 'Right, last meeting before I go.' Literally 'before my departure'. The little opening word is how French speakers say 'right' or 'okay then' when they move on. He asks for news.
- 'Any important news?' The French noun for these pieces of news is plural, and the description agrees with it. English 'news' takes a singular verb, so the two languages differ here.
- 'My favourite, Pierre Niney, has a new part.' The possessive and 'favourite' are masculine because Andrea is talking about a male actor. Her own gender does not choose their form.
- 'He plays Guillaume Canet's brother.' French has no apostrophe s: you say 'the brother of Guillaume Canet'. Canet is an agency client too, so Samuel is pleased.
- 'Excellent! Two of our actors.' The small word meaning 'of' follows the number. The plural possessive then identifies the actors as clients of their agency.
- 'Anything else? Gabriel?' Literally, the short question says 'other thing'. The feminine form of 'other' goes with the word for 'thing'.
- 'Cécile de France and Tarantino?' Just two names and a rising voice make a question. Gabriel stays quiet, so Samuel guesses.
- 'Isn't it going well?' Two small negative words surround the action. There is also a short word just before it because the French expression for 'to happen' uses that extra piece.
- 'I wouldn't say that.' The ending of 'say' carries the idea of 'would'. It lets Gabriel correct Samuel's guess cautiously before giving the bad news.
- 'It's not happening at all.' The two negatives remain. The extra expression strengthens them to 'not at all': the film offer is not happening.
- 'That's not possible!' In the source conversation the first small negative is dropped, as it often is in speech. Our slower version includes both parts.
- 'They don't want Cecile.' Listen to the plural form of 'want'. The two negative words go on either side of it, while the person they do not want comes last.
- 'She's too old for the part.' The description of age has a special feminine form for Cecile. Its sound changes as well as its spelling; it is not just a silent extra letter.
- 'Too old? What's the part, a little girl?' The last word is a casual word for a kid. Then Mathias jumps in: he says it was a three-film deal worth millions, and that Gabriel let it slip away. Samuel stops him.
- 'Mathias, that's enough!' Samuel uses a very handy phrase that literally means 'that suffices'. Parents use it, bosses use it, and here it ends the argument.
