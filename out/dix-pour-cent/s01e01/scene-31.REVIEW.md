## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Here.' This short word presents something for the other person to notice. On Camille's note, it points Andrea to the figures.
- 'I have the figures you wanted.' The past form of 'want' describes Andrea's earlier request. Camille keeps the polite form of 'you' when writing to her boss.
- 'Goodbye, Andréa.' The standard polite goodbye, literally 'until seeing again'. Camille heads for the stairs. Andréa, on the phone, sees the note.
- 'I'll call you back, okay?' French uses the present form for this immediate promise. The object meaning 'you' comes before the action.
- 'Camille, wait!' This is a command with the formal 'you': it's simply the formal present form, used without the word for 'you'. Andréa runs down the stairs after her.
- 'Richard, hello! The Chekhov was fabulous.' The past form of 'be' describes a play Andrea has seen. The writer's name stands for the production in this theatre conversation.
- 'Andréa? But I'm not your assistant anymore.' Here, 'not anymore' is two little words around the action word: the first one, and then one that means 'more'.
- 'Okay, give me the figures.' In this polite instruction, 'me' attaches after the action with a hyphen. In an ordinary statement it would come before the action.
- 'Here. There they are.' The first word is what you say when you hand something to someone you call by the formal 'you'.
- 'So?' Literally 'and then?'. Camille waits while Andréa reads, with her heart beating.
- 'I'm keeping you.' The object meaning polite 'you' comes before 'keep'. The present form announces Andrea's decision immediately.
- 'Really? For sure?' Literally 'it's sure?'. Camille can't believe it.
- 'Yes, for sure.' The short expression literally says 'it's certain'. Andrea confirms that Camille is keeping her job.
- 'Oh, thank you! Thank you, Andrea!' Repeating the short expression of thanks lets Camille show how relieved she is.
- 'I want this job so much!' The verb is 'to want', and the word in the middle makes it 'so much'.
- 'I'm going to give it everything!' Here's a really useful pattern: the verb 'to go' plus another verb gives you the near future, just like 'going to' in English.
- 'Are you going to your desk?' The verb 'to go' again, this time on its own, with the formal 'you'. Andréa is telling her, without saying it, to get back to work.
- 'Yes! You bet!' The last two words literally mean 'and how!', and they're a happy, very French way of saying 'absolutely'. Right, let's hear the whole scene once more, start to finish.
