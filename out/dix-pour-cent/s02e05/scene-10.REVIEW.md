## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Does it still have that effect, even after 50 years?' Gabriel probes Arlette's lingering tension. The idiom 'faire cet effet' means to produce that particular emotional reaction.
- 'Pardon? What are you talking about?' Arlette feigns innocence. The question 'de quoi parlez-vous' uses the preposition 'de' with the interrogative 'quoi'.
- 'I mean, you and Guy Marchand.' Gabriel names the legendary singer. The idiom 'je veux dire' clarifies an implicit reference casually.
- 'What is it exactly? Rancor? Resentment?' Gabriel asks for precise psychological nuances. The nouns 'rancoeur' and 'ressentiment' contrast instinctive bitterness with conscious grudge.
- 'Resentment is deliberate, rancor cannot be controlled.' Arlette offers a subtle philosophical distinction. The reflexive passive 'ne se controle pas' denotes an uncontrollable emotion.
- 'So, which is it between the two of you?' Gabriel presses for a direct answer. The interrogative pronoun 'lequel' selects between the two distinct options.
- 'Nothing on my part. It is him.' Arlette disclaims any personal bitterness. The phrase 'de ma part' indicates one's own side of a dispute.
- 'He holds a grudge against me because I left him for Chet Baker.' Arlette reveals the historic romantic betrayal. The idiom 'en vouloir a quelqu'un' means to hold a grudge against someone.
- 'I loved life and artists, he wanted to get married.' Arlette contrasts their youthful ambitions. The imperfect verbs 'aimais' and 'voulait' describe ongoing desires in the past.
- 'He never forgave me for it.' Arlette notes his enduring heartache. The double pronoun 'ne me l'a jamais pardonne' places the indirect pronoun before the direct.
- 'So it is true, that story with Chet Baker?' Gabriel seeks confirmation of the showbiz legend. The adjective 'vrai' inquires into the factual truth of the anecdote.
- 'Did you take me for a liar?' Arlette reacts with sharp theatrical indignance. The expression 'prendre quelqu'un pour' means to assume someone is something.
- 'No, but it is crazy. Chet Baker!' Gabriel marvels at the legendary name. The colloquial adjective 'dingue' expresses utter amazement.
- 'It was in New York, at the jazz club where he was playing.' Arlette paints the romantic setting. The relative adverb 'ou' introduces the venue where Chet Baker performed.
- 'He fell madly in love with me. And I followed him.' Arlette describes her whirlwind adventure. The idiom 'tomber amoureux' combines with the intensive adverb 'eperdument'.
- 'Jealousy is so stupid.' Arlette philosophizes on romantic possessiveness. The definite noun 'la jalousie' receives a categorical judgment with 'tellement stupide'.
