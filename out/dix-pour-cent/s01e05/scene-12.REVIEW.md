## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Arlette, I want to talk to you about Jonathan Joubert.' The first you is polite, keeping some professional distance. The word for about introduces the person they are going to discuss.
- 'That's his armchair.' The possessive his follows the masculine word for armchair. It identifies the chair with someone already known in the conversation.
- 'Marie-Sophie doesn't want your flowers.' After a negative expression about wanting something, French can use of before the thing refused. Your is plural because it goes with flowers.
- 'You can't do anything against Ryan's illness.' The word for nothing follows can, while do stays in its basic form. Together the negative means that no useful action is possible.
- 'We're going to lose a lot of money!' The quantity a lot is followed by of before money. A form of go announces the financial loss he expects if the character is removed.
- 'Not everything revolves around money.' The negative does not mean nothing matters. It limits everything: money is not the centre of every consideration.
- 'Arlette, your share in this agency is very small.' The possessive your is polite, but his message is threatening. Small takes its feminine ending to match the word for share.
- 'And Samuel is no longer here to protect you.' The negative expression means no longer. The following action protect stays in its basic form after the connecting word for.
- 'Your threats don't impress me.' The plural threats are the subject, which makes the action plural too. The little word for me stands before that action.
- 'I'm not talking about money, I'm talking about dignity and loyalty.' The same pattern about speaking of something appears twice. Repeating it places money on one side and dignity and loyalty on the other.
- 'Marie-Sophie is worth a thousand of that idiot Jonathan Joubert!' The action means is worth, not costs. A thousand times makes the comparison deliberately extreme, showing her loyalty to the writer.
- 'So as for your Ryan, cut!' The instruction cut can also mean stopping a film take. Here its ordinary cutting sense makes the dismissal of the character especially pointed.
- 'Excuse me, we have to think of the real fans of the series.' The impersonal expression means it is necessary. The word for to combines with the plural article before the real fans she wants them to consider.
- 'Yes, Camille?' A simple yes followed by a name gives someone the floor. It can invite them to continue without agreeing to any proposal yet.
- 'Andréa wanted to show you something, but not now.' The past form of want reports an earlier intention. Something is an indefinite expression: Camille does not need to name the object she might show him.
- 'I'll come back later.' A form of go introduces the promise to return. The final phrase means later, literally more late, rather than never coming back.
