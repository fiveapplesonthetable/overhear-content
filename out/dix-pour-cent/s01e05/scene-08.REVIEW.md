## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'One is black and the other is yellow. What does it taste like?' The two expressions pair one with the other. The question about flavour literally asks what taste it has, using have rather than the English action taste.
- 'It's horrible! Gabriel!' The description keeps the same form for a masculine or feminine thing. Her next word is a name, calling Gabriel's attention rather than continuing to describe the snack.
- 'Gabriel Sarda! It's Gabriel Sarda, my agent!' The possessive my agrees with the masculine word for agent. Repeating his full name makes her announcement sound especially proud.
- 'He came to encourage me!' The completed past of come uses be as its helper. The little word attached to encourage identifies who receives his support: me.
- 'How's it going?' The greeting literally asks whether things go. It is a familiar everyday way of checking how someone is doing.
- 'Yes! I saw JoeyStarr. I rehearsed my scene!' Both completed actions use have as a helper. Seeing someone and rehearsing a scene are different actions, even though they share that past-tense pattern.
- 'He said I was great! Good, right?' After he said that, the reported description uses a past form of be. The short final question invites agreement with her good news.
- 'I'm really happy for you. One second. Hello?' The description happy is masculine for Gabriel. The word for for introduces the person whose success pleases him; one second then politely interrupts the conversation.
- 'It's Anna. Yes, Anna, is everything okay?' The first identifying expression announces who is calling. The question then uses everything as its subject, rather than addressing Anna as the thing that must go well.
- 'I'll ask my agent. When does it come out?' The present form of ask announces what she is about to do. The action meaning come out refers here to the film being released, rather than a person leaving a room.
- 'No idea.' The word meaning no agrees with the feminine word for idea. The shortened answer leaves out a whole sentence about not knowing.
- 'Can I have invitations? Yes, he says yes!' The first question asks for permission to have invitations. In the next sentence, the small word for he makes Gabriel the person she claims has agreed.
- 'I'll leave you to concentrate.' The first you is the person he is leaving; the second belongs with concentrate. Repeating it gives the French reflexive pattern for focusing oneself.
- 'There are horses and everything!' The phrase for there are uses a singular helper even with plural horses. The ending and everything is an informal way of adding more impressive details.
- 'Here, darling. This will do you good. Come here.' The first word offers something to the listener. Doing someone good uses the little word for you before do, marking who should feel better.
