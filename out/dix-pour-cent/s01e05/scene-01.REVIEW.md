## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Madam.' This form of address fits a servant speaking to an aristocrat. It is also the ordinary respectful way to address a woman today.
- 'No! I don't believe it!' The expression for not believing something also conveys astonishment. The small extra word points back to the situation she cannot believe.
- 'Cut! What is going on?' The first word is an instruction. The question then uses an everyday expression for something happening, rather than for a person passing by.
- 'He pushes her into the bushes.' The little word for her stands before the action. It tells us who gets pushed, rather than who does the pushing.
- 'Are you made of glass?' French uses its word for in before a material here. The question is sarcastic: he thinks she is being too delicate.
- 'No, I am an aristocrat, he is a servant.' Her character has higher social rank. Listen to how French can name a role without always using a word for a before it.
- 'He has to show respect.' The expression literally says to make proof of respect. It is a way of saying that a quality should be visible in someone's behaviour.
- 'I'm working, okay?' The French action word needs no separate helper for an activity happening now. Julie insists that her concern for the performance is part of her work.
- 'Could we have a little delicacy and elegance in this scene?' Asking whether they could have something makes a demand sound more indirect. The complaint is still very pointed.
- 'Is that aimed at me? I'm fed up.' The last expression means having had enough. Its small connecting word refers back to the thing causing the irritation.
- 'Me too!' French uses a strong form of me for this short reply. No separate action word is needed to agree with the complaint.
- 'Go to your high-society dinners!' Now JoeyStarr mocks Julie's social world. The instruction uses the same polite form that the stars have been using to address one another.
- 'Speak clearly, please. I don't understand a word.' The word for a single word makes the denial emphatic. She claims to understand nothing at all.
- 'How far behind schedule are we?' The question asks how much delay they have. French builds the thought with have, although English naturally asks how far behind they are.
- 'Five days. I don't believe it!' The number comes before the thing being counted. This time the director is the one who cannot believe what he hears.
- 'Call their agents right now!' The possessive means their, referring to the two stars. The final word makes the timing urgent.
