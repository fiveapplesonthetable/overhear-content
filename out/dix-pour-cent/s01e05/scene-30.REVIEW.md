## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Do you have a minute? I forgot something.' The familiar question asks whether Camille has a minute. The French expression for something joins the words for some and thing, leaving the forgotten matter unnamed until his next reply.
- 'What, here, in this office?' The short question repeats three possible details as she tries to understand. Here locates it in the room, rather than naming something that belongs to the office.
- 'Your birthday.' The familiar possessive your goes with a masculine word for birthday. It refers to Camille's day, even though the possessed noun is masculine and she is a woman.
- 'Oh, yes. It's no big deal. And I'm not six years old!' The negative about being serious means that it is no big problem. French uses have with the number six to give age, where English says I am six.
- 'Anyway, what would make you happy? You must have some idea.' The conditional form would make softens the question about pleasing her. The little word for to you comes before that action, while the final sentence assumes she can think of something she wants.
- 'I hurt the examiner's hand with the car door.' The completed action injured describes the result of the driving-test mishap. The connecting word of identifies whose hand it was, and with names the car door as what caused the injury.
- 'No!' The brief negative expresses disbelief. It responds to the surprising account rather than claiming that he has examined the instructor's hand himself.
- 'Yes! He had two broken fingers.' The special affirmative contradicts his disbelief. The number two counts the fingers, and the plural description broken agrees with them.
- 'I thought I'd see you at the opera. I left messages.' The familiar object you follows the basic action see. At the opera names the expected meeting place, and the completed action left explains the unanswered messages.
- 'My phone was on silent. Do you want some champagne?' The imperfect form describes the phone's earlier state. The familiar question offers some champagne, using a quantity expression for an unspecified amount of the drink.
- 'Camille helped me a lot this week. We are celebrating!' The small word for me attaches to the past helper before helped. This week gives the period of her help, and celebrating that refers to the achievement he offers as an explanation.
- 'Isn't Camille Andréa's assistant?' The negative question seeks confirmation of something she thought she knew. The feminine word assistant names Camille's job, and of connects it to Andrea.
- 'She can still help the others.' Still or nevertheless joins two small words to answer the objection. The others refers to other people in the agency whom Camille can also help.
- 'Stop! She is an assistant, I just wanted to thank her.' The familiar instruction stop addresses Catherine. The completed intention wanted to thank explains the gesture, while her replaces a second mention of Camille.
- 'Mathias... explain this to me.' The familiar command explain attaches the word for to me after the action. In this learner version Catherine makes the request while Hippolyte remains unvoiced.
- 'I have something to tell you.' Something is linked to a basic action by the connecting word to. The little word for to you comes before say, identifying the person who is about to hear the explanation.
