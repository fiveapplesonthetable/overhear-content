## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'We promised to be here.' The completed past of promise uses have as a helper. The small word linking promise to be introduces what the agents said they would do.
- 'But we didn't say when!' The final question word is left at the end of the statement. The joke is that they promised to be there without fixing exactly when.
- 'Hello? Yes, okay, I'm on my way.' The present action literally says I arrive. French uses it to say the speaker is coming immediately, even before reaching the destination.
- 'Yes, right away.' The three-word expression means immediately. Its meaning comes from the whole expression, rather than from translating every word separately.
- 'A mate in trouble. I have to go.' Mate is a familiar word for a friend. The expression for trouble describes being in a difficult situation; have to then explains why he claims to leave.
- 'Yes, of course.' The last two words work together to mean of course. The description sure keeps its masculine spelling inside this fixed expression.
- 'But can I get my phone back before you leave?' Recover means get back here, because the object is his own phone. Before introduces a noun, your departure, rather than a complete new action.
- 'The door is locked! I don't want to stay here.' The description closed is feminine to match door. The next sentence puts want before the basic action stay, explaining the wish to get out.
- 'Look, there's a window up there!' The opening is a polite instruction to look. The last expression means up there and points to a higher place than the speaker's own position.
- 'Julie! What are you doing?' The question addresses someone politely using the plural form of do. It asks about the action in progress, rather than about what has already happened.
- 'Careful, Julie!' The warning can stand alone as a noun used like an instruction. Adding her name makes clear who needs to be careful.
- 'Didier? Didier!' Repeating a person's name can call for attention or check where they are. Gabriel uses the actor's first name rather than his stage name.
- 'What's going on?' The question asks what is happening. The reflexive expression is about an event, not about physically passing another person.
