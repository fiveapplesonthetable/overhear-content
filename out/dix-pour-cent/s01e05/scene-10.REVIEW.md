## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Noémie, send flowers to Marie-Sophie Garnier, please.' The action is a polite or plural instruction. The word for some comes before the plural flowers, and to introduces their recipient.
- 'Sign the note: Dear Marie-Sophie, I am sorry.' Dear is written in the feminine form because the note addresses a woman. The apology is masculine because the supposed sender is a man.
- 'A new start. Signed Jonathan Joubert.' New takes its masculine form to match start. Signed names the supposed author of the note, even though Mathias is arranging it.
- 'Mathias, Zinedine Soualem is here.' The short place word means here. It announces that the visitor has arrived, rather than saying what he is doing.
- 'Not now!' The short negative can stand before a time word without a whole sentence. Mathias refuses the interruption immediately.
- 'No, Mathias isn't here.' The negative is built around is. The final place word points to the office where the visitor expects to find Mathias.
- 'He can call you whenever he wants. Goodbye!' Can comes before the basic action call. The words when he wants make the timing open, without promising a particular time.
- 'It's fine, he's gone.' The completed past of leave uses be as its helper. That is why the French literally builds the idea with he is gone.
- 'Don't forget the flowers for Marie-Sophie Garnier.' The negative instruction places not before forget. The polite form continues to address Noémie as one person.
- 'Of course. Interflora, please.' The first expression means of course. The following request gives the name of the flower service she wants to reach.
- 'Is it true that they're going to kill off Ryan?' The question opens with is it true that. The plural form of go announces what the producers intend to do to the fictional character.
- 'That's not possible!' Possible has the same written form for masculine or feminine things. The negative expression rejects the news as unbelievable.
- 'My mother is going to be very sad.' The possessive my follows the feminine word for mother. The form of go before be presents her sadness as an expected consequence.
- 'She watches the series every day!' The phrase for every day literally says all the days. French uses the plural even though English naturally uses a singular day.
- 'If I tell her that, she's going to organise protests!' The word for to her stands before tell. The if part describes the possible trigger, and the next part says what the mother is expected to do.
- 'My mother is going to be very surprised in her salon!' The description surprised is feminine for her mother. In the lesson's simplified version, the salon is where the news will spread among fans.
