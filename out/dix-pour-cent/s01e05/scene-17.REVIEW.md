## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Surprise! I'm checking the expense reports.' Surprise has the same spelling as in English but a French pronunciation. The expense expression literally says notes of expenses, linking the joke to her work as an auditor.
- 'How did you get into my room? They let you in?' The completed past of enter uses be as its helper. Its written ending is feminine because Colette is the person who entered.
- 'Yes, as you can see. After insisting.' As you see is an everyday way to confirm the obvious. After having insisted puts the insistence before the hotel let her in.
- 'Well, I'm working. Things to sort out for the shoot.' The casual noun for things means stuff or matters to deal with. The little word before sort out introduces the work that still needs doing.
- 'Dinner with Gabriel?' The noun dinner lets the question stay very short. Repeating the other person's words can ask for confirmation and reveal surprise at the same time.
- 'Yes, it's for work!' The description literally means professional. Here it insists that the dinner belongs to work, rather than being a personal invitation.
- 'I thought... You aren't happy to see me?' The first past form describes what she had been expecting. Happy is feminine for Andrea, and the little word for me comes before see.
- 'Fortunately, it is not the tailbone. That is very painful.' Fortunately comments on the whole situation. After Colette's fall, the painful tailbone is an injury Andrea is relieved she has avoided.
- 'You have to walk around with a cushion to sit on.' The expression for walking around includes a little word for yourself. The next action means sit down, with that same little word attached to its opening.
- 'Your pretty little backside cushioned the shock.' The French noun for backside is plural. That makes the possessive, both descriptions and the completed action plural too.
- 'Thank you, pretty little backside!' The repeated description turns the previous reassurance into a playful thank-you. Its plural ending follows the French word for backside even though English can use a singular word.
- 'It's Gabriel, are you coming?' The first expression identifies the person at the door. The question uses the present form of come to ask whether Andrea is joining him now.
- 'I'm coming. I have to go.' The first present action means I am coming. The second expression says she must go there, referring to the dinner Gabriel is waiting for.
- 'Come back soon.' The instruction uses the familiar form of come back. The final word can mean quickly, but here it asks for a return soon.
- 'I promise you. Wait for me.' The two small words before promise mean to you and it. In the following instruction, the word for me instead attaches after wait.
- 'I can't really run away!' The expression about saving oneself can mean escape or run away. The final words describe doing so by running, which her injury makes an unlikely option.
