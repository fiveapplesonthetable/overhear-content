## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hello, Julie? Where are you?' The phone greeting is followed by a contraction of the familiar you and are. French drops the vowel of you in this quick spoken form; the final word asks where she is.
- 'Can we go back to the set?' French uses the basic form of return after can. The words for to and the combine before set, identifying the place where the actors should be working.
- 'Samir says he has some good takes. We can catch up on the delay.' The first part reports what the director says, with that joining the report to its content. Catching up on the delay describes recovering lost time rather than physically catching a person.
- 'Sorry, Didier, but that's not an excuse.' Sorry is followed directly by the actor's name. The negative leaves out the small opening word often omitted in conversation, while excuse means a justification rather than an apology here.
- 'They're going to say we're irresponsible again.' Go before another action is a way French expresses a coming event. The word for again adds the idea that the same criticism has been made before.
- 'We gave it our all yesterday, didn't we?' Giving oneself fully to something is a figurative expression for making a great effort. The completed reflexive form looks back at yesterday, while the final question seeks agreement.
- 'You really must go back to the set.' Must is followed by the basic action return. Really strengthens the instruction, and the familiar you still addresses just one actor.
- 'All right.' The brief expression means agreed or all right. It accepts the request without repeating the whole plan to return to work.
- 'Didier, you're on parole! The custody of your children!' The shortened you are introduces his legal situation. The feminine word for custody names responsibility for the children, so the shouted fragment works as a warning about consequences.
- 'Was that okay? Was it not too much?' The imperfect forms ask how the completed performance felt. Too is left without a description after it, because too much is clear from the situation.
- 'No, it was wonderful.' The opening no rejects the worry that the performance went too far. The following description wonderful gives a positive judgement of the result instead.
- 'I wanted to ask you something. Are you seeing anyone at the moment?' The past form of want makes the request less abrupt. The expression for seeing someone has the relationship meaning here, and at the moment limits the question to her current life.
