## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'One wise man once spoke important words.' Indefinite temporal adverb 'once/at some point' sets up the proverb.
- 'You can sit for a long time on the quiet bank of a river.' Notice how Russian puts 'may, possible, allowed' and 'river' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'And wait for your enemy to finally lose.' Conjunction 'when' introduces the awaited outcome.
- 'But sometimes you are sitting by the wrong river altogether.' In everyday Russian, 'sometimes, at times' flows straight into 'river' without extra linking words.
- 'Vitya, I remember that I still owe you a debt.' Diminutive masculine noun 'little debt' softens the awkward moment.
- 'You are supposed to kick me two more times.' Nagiyev braces himself to pay off the rest of their bet.
- 'Well come on, kick me quickly, what are you waiting for?' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
- 'All right, Dima, just go home in peace.' Seeing his friend already defeated by the lost banquet, Barinov waves him off.
- 'What, are you not angry with me anymore?' Nagiyev pairs 'more, anymore' with 'are angry' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'I do not want to kick you anymore, pal.' Barinov chooses friendship over petty revenge.
- 'Suddenly you realize that you do not want victory over your enemy.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'After all, old friendship is much more important than any grudge.' In everyday Russian, 'old' flows straight into 'offense, resentment' without extra linking words.
