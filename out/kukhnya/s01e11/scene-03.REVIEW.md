## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hi, Nastya, why are you in a bad mood?' Russians say "without mood" to mean a bad mood, as if mood were something you can be short of. Kostya greets her warmly and offers a friendly ear.
- 'People tore live flowers for pleasure.' "Live flowers" means freshly cut flowers, which were alive before somebody picked them. Nastya looks after animals and nature, so this really upsets her. "For the sake of pleasure" is one small word plus the thing.
- 'Do not be sad, Nastya, everything will be fine.' "Don't be sad" grows from the noun "sadness". "Everything will be good" is how Russians say "all will be well". Kostya reaches for the simplest comfort there is.
- 'This is terrible, wicked people act like that.' "Act" comes from the same root as "deeds" in the chef's lecture. And "wicked" is the root of "do not anger me". Nastya has strong views and says them plainly.
- 'Let us go somewhere this weekend.' The "-nibud" tacked on to "to where" gives "to somewhere or other". A weekend is "the days off", and Kostya uses "let's go and come back". He changes the subject, hopefully.
- 'I also wanted to suggest a meeting to you.' Nastya's "wanted" ends in -a, since she's a woman. The term for "suggest" matches the TV woman's "offer". A "meeting" here also means a date. She is as keen as he is.
- 'Great! What beautiful place shall we go to?' "Into which" takes the ending for going into a place. Kostya is thinking of a restaurant, you can tell. Nastya is thinking of something quite different.
- 'We will go together to the Khimki forest.' She says "poedem", the by-vehicle "go", so they'll travel there. "Khimki" is a town on the edge of Moscow, and its forest was a famous protest site. Nastya says it as if it were a treat.
- 'And why do we need to go to the forest?' "What for" asks about purpose. And "to go" in the plain form uses the by-vehicle word again. Kostya is puzzled, since forests are not on his list of dates.
- 'There will be a big rally in defense of the forest.' "Miting" is the English word "meeting" borrowed into Russian, but it means a political rally. "In defence of" is two words, and "forest" takes the "of" ending. Nastya says it with pride.
- 'I thought we would go to the cinema.' "Kino" never changes its ending, whatever the sentence. "I thought" ends in the man's -l. Kostya's idea of a date is the classic one, a film and then dinner.
- 'Saving living nature is much more important than cinema!' She uses the rescue word for "saving". Russian puts the second item straight after "more important", with no "than". And "kino" doesn't change, even then. Nastya wins the argument.
- 'Alright, Nastya, I will gladly go.' "With pleasure" is how Russians say "gladly". Kostya is in love, so he gives in at once. The future of "go" is the by-vehicle one, because they're travelling.
