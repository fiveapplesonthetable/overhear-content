## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'This soup is really very tasty and hot.' The guest uses "really" for emphasis. He is happy with Barinov's soup, and he says so. The term for "tasty" comes from what Russian calls "taste".
- 'Kostya, tell honestly, what happened with you?' "Say honestly" is the usual way to ask for the truth. "With you" is shown in the ending. The guest uses "ty" with the bartender, as a regular does.
- 'Everything is fine, I am simply wiping this glass.' Russian has several words for "glass". This one is a small shot glass, the kind for spirits. Kostya wipes it, and the lie is obvious. His mind is elsewhere.
- 'You have been washing it for a whole half hour already.' Russian uses the present tense for something that has been going on a while: "you wash it already half an hour". "Whole" adds the exasperation. The guest notices what the bartender doesn't.
- 'An important critic is sitting at Nastya's table.' "At Nastya" is the "at" pattern, here meaning "at her table". Kostya finally admits it. Having a worry, he wants to share it.
- 'This insolent man is rudely hitting on her.' "Insolent" and "rudely" come from the same family. "Bothers her" is the pester word. Kostya's anger shows. He has the sense of a man who can't do anything.
- 'I am afraid of doing something bad for the restaurant.' "Something bad" ends in "-nibud", the "any" ending. Kostya fears that he might harm the restaurant by acting. He is torn between love and loyalty.
- 'You see, the problem is not at all in this critic.' "You understand" is the Russian "you see". Russians say "the problem is in" something. The guest is about to say something wise, and he opens with a pause.
- 'The main question here is in who you are.' "In that, who you are" is the Russian frame for "the point is who you are". The guest, a stranger, gives the bartender a mirror. It's a good line.
- 'Pass me the salt, please, young friend.' "Pass" is the same as "pass on". "Salt" is a feminine word. The guest cuts through his own wisdom with a request for salt. It is a joke, and a warm one.
- 'Whether he is a janitor or a critic—that is completely unimportant.' A janitor is a "yard-man" in Russian, from "yard". "Unimportant" is "not important" glued together. The guest says it with a dismissive wave.
- 'Do you allow someone to insult your girlfriend or not?' The question is put in a long frame: "do you allow ... or not". "Allow" and "insult" are two clear words. The guest gets to the point.
- 'The life is yours, and only you make the decision!' Russian says "life yours", with no "is". And you "accept" a decision, rather than make one. The guest finishes his sermon, and the bartender looks up.
