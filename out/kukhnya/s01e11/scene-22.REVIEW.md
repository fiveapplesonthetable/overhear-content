## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'This soup is really very tasty and hot.' "Deystvitelno" is "really", "truly". "Vkusny", "tasty", is built on "vkus", "taste". The guest is happy with Barinov's soup, and he says so.
- 'Kostya, tell honestly, what happened with you?' "Skazhi chestno" is the usual way to ask for the truth. "S toboy" is "with you". The guest uses "ty" with the bartender, as a regular does.
- 'Everything is fine, I am simply wiping this glass.' Russian has several words for "glass". "Ryumka" is a small one, the kind for spirits. "Vytirayu" is "I am wiping". His mind is elsewhere.
- 'You have been washing it for a whole half hour already.' Russian uses the present for something still going on: "moesh eyo uzhe polchasa", "you wash it already half an hour". "Tselykh", "whole", adds the exasperation.
- 'An important critic is sitting at Nastya's table.' "U Nasti za stolom" is "at Nastya, at the table", meaning "at her table". Kostya finally admits what is on his mind.
- 'This insolent man is rudely hitting on her.' To pester someone, Russian says "pristavat k" plus the person, so "pristayot k ney" is "is pestering her", often with unwanted advances. "Nagly" is "insolent", and "grubo", "rudely". Kostya's anger shows.
- 'I am afraid of doing something bad for the restaurant.' "Chto-nibud", "something", ends in -nibud, the "any" ending. "Boyus sdelat" is "I am afraid to do". Kostya is torn between love and loyalty.
- 'You see, the problem is not at all in this critic.' "Ponimaesh", "you understand", is the Russian "you see". Russians say the problem is "v", "in", something: "ne v etom kritike". The guest is about to say something wise.
- 'The main question here is in who you are.' "V tom, kto ty", "in that, who you are", is the Russian frame for "the point is who you are". The guest, a stranger, holds up a mirror to the bartender.
- 'Pass me the salt, please, young friend.' "Peredai" is "pass", hand something on. "Sol", "salt", is a feminine word. The guest breaks off his own wisdom to ask for salt.
- 'Whether he is a janitor or a critic—that is completely unimportant.' Word for word, "dvornik on ili kritik" is "janitor he or critic", with no "whether" at the start. A "dvornik" sweeps the "dvor", the yard, and "nevazhno" is "ne vazhno", "not important", run together.
- 'Do you allow someone to insult your girlfriend or not?' The verb comes first: "pozvolyaesh ty", "allow you", for a pointed question. "Obizhat" is "to insult" or "to hurt". "Ili net" is "or not".
- 'The life is yours, and only you make the decision!' "Zhizn tvoya", "life yours", has no "is". And you "accept" a decision, "prinimaesh reshenie", rather than make one. The guest finishes his sermon.
