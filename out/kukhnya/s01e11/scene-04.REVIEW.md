## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Max, why are you slacking off here?' The Russian word means "cooling yourself off", and it comes from the same root as "cold", like the refrigerator. Russians use it for someone taking it easy. Barinov is already irritated.
- 'What else am I to do, chef?' Russian says "what to me still to do". It's the question of a good worker who has been given no job. Max sounds hurt, as he often does.
- 'I was suspended from work today.' The Russian says "they moved me aside", with nobody named. That's how Russians keep the blame out of a sentence, and Max uses it well.
- 'Who suspended you? Me?' Barinov repeats the word, and the pause between "who" and "me" is the joke. He cannot bear that anyone else makes decisions in his kitchen. His voice goes up on the last word.
- 'Viktoria Sergeyevna told me to sit here.' First name plus father's name is the polite way to name the art director. And "said to sit" takes the plain form of "sit", with no "that" between. Max names his source.
- 'Since when do you listen to her?' Russian says "from which times" for "since when". And "listen" means "obey" here, as when Senya promised to watch the score. Barinov is offended on his own behalf.
- 'In this kitchen, only I am the boss!' "Main" is what you heard in "who is most main". The "only" goes in front of the "I", for emphasis. Barinov says it with a slap of the table.
- 'She promised to fire me, chef.' "Promised" ends in -la, because a woman made the promise. The firing word is the one Alisa used when she threatened Lyova. Max reports faithfully and pushes the blame up the chain.
- 'Only I can fire cooks!' Barinov states his powers like a king. The thing fired takes the object ending, and "can" is short and sharp. His kitchen is his kingdom.
- 'Now march to the kitchen!' The first little word pushes, something like "come on, now". The second is a military "march". Barinov barks in two words, and Max is almost out of the door before the second.
- 'A pile of orders is waiting for you there.' Russians use "heap" for "lots". The printed order slips are "checks". And "waits for you" has a prefix that means "waits until you come", which is just what the chef means.
- 'Thank you, chef, I am running!' Max uses the on-foot present, "I run", in the middle of the movement. He is delighted, because washing dishes is the least of his talents.
- 'Wait, where are you carrying that pan?' The prefix on "carrying" marks the moment of setting off, so Barinov catches him in the act. The pan takes the object ending. The chef never lets a pan go unwatched.
- 'To the workstation, chef!' Literally, "to the working place". The words are short and obedient. Max uses his boss's title and does not argue. He keeps hold of the pan, and the scene ends.
