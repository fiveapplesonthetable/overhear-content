## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What do you want to order with us today?' Russians say "at us" for "with us", meaning at our place. Nastya uses the polite "vy". She starts the way every waitress does, with a smile and a question.
- 'Bring me any dish to your taste.' The man uses the casual "ty" with a waitress, which is rude. "Any" means "any at all". "To your taste" is "na tvoy vkus". He takes his time.
- 'Good, I will choose the best for you.' "Choose" is what Senya used for picking his goods. "The best" is a neuter form, meaning "the best thing". Nastya stays polite, and she keeps the "vy".
- 'Why do you not take a seat nearby?' The little "by" in the question makes a polite-sounding suggestion, "why wouldn't you sit". The prefix on "sit" means "for a moment". "Nearby" is one word. It sounds friendly, and it isn't.
- 'Excuse me, but I am at work right now.' Russians are "on" work, not "in" it. "Excuse" is the polite form, not the casual one. Nastya refuses gently, which is how waitresses are trained to refuse.
- 'I am your customer and I pay for everything.' "Klient" is the French "client" in Russian letters. "I pay" is the first-person present, short and blunt. The man says it as a threat.
- 'I am obliged only to bring your dishes.' "Obliged" has the woman's -a ending. "Only" is the word that began the white-chocolate rule. Nastya sets out her duties plainly. Nothing beyond the dishes is her business.
- 'You were the first to make pretty eyes at me.' The Russian idiom is "built little eyes at me". "Little eyes" is a tender plural, and the man means it as a charge. "First" has a woman's ending. It's an accusation.
- 'I was simply smiling politely at you, guest.' "Was smiling" has the woman's -la and the -sya. "Politely" grows from the word for polite. Nastya addresses him as "guest", which is cold. It is her rudest word.
- 'Do not leave, sit with me a little.' "Don't leave" is the "away" word that you know. "Sit a while" has the "po-" for a short stay. He gets more insistent, and his tone more like an order.
- 'I do not have the right to sit here.' Here Russian uses an actual verb for "have", the formal one, which is rare in everyday speech. "Right" takes the "of" form. Nastya sounds like a lawyer, and it works.
- 'You must fulfill any requests of mine.' "Must" has the woman's form, since he's addressing her. "Requests" grows from the Russian for "ask for". The man oversteps, and everyone who has worked in a restaurant knows this man.
- 'Excuse me, I am going to the kitchen right now.' "Excuse me" again, with the polite form. "I will go" is the on-foot future. Nastya finds a way out, and her cool voice shows her relief.
