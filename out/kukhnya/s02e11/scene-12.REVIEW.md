## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Kostyan, just relax.' With the truth and apartment in play, Max keeps this short reply brisk and direct. Using the informal "you" shows how direct and unfiltered their kitchen relationship is.
- 'At least they made up.' Because at least they made up, Kostya treats the matter as settled.
- 'Kostik gave that mug to me.' Explaining that someone gave that mug lays the cards on the table for Kostya.
- 'I will buy you a new one.' Max mentions that he will buy Kostya a new one so Kostya sees what is at stake. Speaking with the close, informal "you" strips away any polite distance between them.
- 'That smells so delicious.' With the soup and peace in play, Kostya keeps this short reply brisk and direct.
- 'She did that on purpose.' Pointing out that she did that on purpose gives Kostya a sobering reality check.
- 'You saw it yourself.' Max explains that Kostya saw it themselves, keeping his voice calm and direct. Using the informal "you" shows how direct and unfiltered their kitchen relationship is.
- 'Why did you put it right next to the edge?' Asking why Max puts it right next to the edge is how Kostya puts Max on the spot.
- 'What about me?' Over the kitchen and the peace, Nastya presses Kostya for a straight answer. In Russian, she does not need helper words like "do" or "did"—rising pitch alone turns it into a question.
- 'Let us sit down and eat.' Realizing that they should sit down and eat changes how Max handles the situation. Starting with "davay" is the everyday Russian way to say "come on, let us do this".
- 'I did not even use it.' Admitting that he did not even use it shows Kostya being honest for a change.
- 'I do not want anything of yours at all.' Saying that she does not want anything of Kostya's at all is how Nastya sets the tone with Kostya. There is no "you" needed in the Russian here: one short word states the rule for everyone in the room.
- 'That is my pot.' Once that is his pot, Max can finally tell Kostya what he means. Russian drops "is" in the present, setting the two halves of the thought side by side.
