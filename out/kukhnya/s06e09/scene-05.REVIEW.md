## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Mom, hello, so where is your friend?' Lyova puts the reaction to the guest: opening with the casual greeting "privet" ("hi") signals right away that Lyova and the guest are on familiar terms.
- 'Well, here she is coming toward us.' The guest follows up candidly during the talk, refusing to let the moment slip past.
- 'Hello, I am very pleased to meet you.' Lyova anchors this direct expression on cue: "poznakomitsya" means "to meet", and it carries the main weight of what Lyova is saying.
- 'Meet her, son, this is my friend Larisa.' The guest shares this warning facing Lyova: the "-sya" ending on "poznakomsya" ("to get acquainted") turns the action inward or makes it happen between people naturally.
- 'I just did not expect to see at all.' Lyova reacts firmly to settle things, to clear the air between them once and for all.
- 'Do not be nervous, she is a very nice girl.' The guest grounds this clear expression right here: "nervnichay" means "be nervous" (from "nervnichat"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'It seems to me you are exaggerating a little right now.' Lyova emphasizes one pointed piece on cue: "preuvelichivayete" means "to exaggerate" (from "preuvelichivat"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'No, I am telling the pure truth.' The guest follows up frankly as things stand, without trying to dress up how he really feels.
- 'Mom, I would rather sit next to you.' Lyova points to one vivid choice for the guest: "luchshe" means "better", and it carries the main weight of what Lyova is saying.
- 'Larisa is the most caring nurse at the clinic.' The guest delivers the thought beside Lyova: notice how Russian needs no word for "am", "is", or "are" in the present — placing the words side by side says it all.
- 'Mom, stop praising me so loudly.' Lyova answers right away around the guest, without trying to dress up how he really feels.
- 'Well, you already know everything about Lyova.' The guest chooses one pointed piece at once: "znayesh" means "know" (from "znat"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'Let us better look at our menu for today.' Lyova frames the remark facing the guest: the word "davayte" is the polite or group version of "let's", inviting the guest into the plan right away.
