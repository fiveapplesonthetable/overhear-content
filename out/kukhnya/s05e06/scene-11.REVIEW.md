## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Fedya, did you go into the walk-in cooler today?' Senya questions earnestly to Fedya here, using "zakhodil" (from "zakhodit", "went in") alongside "segodnya" ("today") to shape the meaning.
- 'I am narrowing the circle of suspects.' Senya chooses a playful footing in the scene, anchoring the Russian line on "suzhayu" ("am narrowing") and "krug" ("circle, ring") without extra filler.
- 'I went in, but I took flounder, not stellate sturgeon.' Fedya sustains a thoughtful posture right away, joining "zakhodil" ("went in") to "kambalu" ("flounder") so every word pulls its weight.
- 'Are there witnesses?' Senya challenges frankly in their exchange, joining "svideteli" ("witnesses") to "yest" ("there is") so every word pulls its weight.
- 'For giving false testimony you will be held liable.' Senya establishes a sharp tack at once, pairing "dachu" ("dacha, country house") with "lozhnykh" ("false") to get the point across in plain spoken Russian.
- 'Alright then, we have our first suspect.' Senya shares an emphatic tone at this moment, linking "znachit" ("then, so"), "pervyy" ("first"), and "podozrevayemyy" ("suspect") in one brisk exchange.
- 'On the contrary, she is smart.' Fedya maintains a clear course at once, building the phrase around "naoborot" ("contrary") and "umnaya" ("clever, smart") for a crisp, natural delivery.
- 'She is divorcing me and marrying someone else.' Fedya displays an honest angle at this moment, anchoring the Russian line on "razvoditsya" ("is getting divorced") and "vykhodit" ("goes out") without extra filler.
- 'I am not pregnant.' Katya adopts a pointed tack toward Fedya, weaving "beremenna" ("pregnant") together with "ya" ("I") just the way native speakers do.
- 'Max, if you want, you can marry me.' Katya brings a frank rhythm at this moment, building the phrase around "khochesh" ("do you want") and "mozhesh" ("can") for a crisp, natural delivery.
- 'Easily.' Fedya chooses an honest stance beside Katya, using "legko" ("easy") by itself, a common way spoken Russian keeps fast dialogue punchy.
- 'Shall we kiss?' Katya inquires candidly without hesitation, putting all the weight on "potseluyemsya" ("kiss") so the reply lands immediately.
