## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktor Petrovich, I will be a little late tomorrow.' Lyova pairs 'tomorrow' with 'will be delayed' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Lyova, just think about this.' Still fuming about Nagiyev, Barinov barely hears Lyova and vents his own frustration.
- 'Nagiyev forbids me to participate in the contest!' Here Chef Barinov anchors the thought on 'forbids' and finishes on 'contest, competition', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'Why would I even go there at all?' In everyday Russian, 'what for, why' flows straight into 'to go' without extra linking words.
- 'In my opinion, it is a very worthy contest.' Lyova defends the competition using a parenthetical opinion word and a masculine adjective.
- 'Lyova, oh, I beg you, please!' This idiomatic exclamation expresses strong skepticism, similar to 'oh, please!' in English.
- 'Wait, where are you being delayed tomorrow?' Barinov suddenly connects the dots and asks a direct question in the present tense.
- 'Viktor Petrovich, I decided to test myself.' In everyday Russian, 'to decide; to solve' flows straight into 'to check, to test' without extra linking words.
- 'So you want to test yourself out there?' Barinov repeats Lyova's phrase in disbelief using a second-person singular verb of wanting.
- 'You are still very far from being a head chef.' Notice how Russian puts 'still, yet, more' and 'head chef' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'Lyova, do not go, do not embarrass me.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'I thought that you would support me.' Russian uses the future tense in the subordinate clause after a past verb of thinking for would.
