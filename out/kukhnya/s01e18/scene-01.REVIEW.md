## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Oh, Kristina, hello!' "Zdravstvuyte" is the long, polite hello you'd use with a boss or a stranger. "Oy" in front is just a little gasp of surprise.
- 'What's with the formal hello?' "Chto eshchyo za" means 'what kind of thing is this', said when something surprises or annoys you. Kristina treats the greeting itself as the odd object.
- 'I can't do it any other way, I'm at work.' "Na rabote", at work: "rabota" becomes "rabote" after "na". Russians are 'on' work, not 'at' it.
- 'I understand you perfectly, friend.' "Prekrasno" is the adverb from "prekrasny", 'beautiful', so she understands 'beautifully'. And she says "tebya", the casual 'you'.
- 'I worked here myself for a whole year.' "Rabotala" ends in -la because Kristina is a woman. "Tselyy god", a whole year, needs no word for 'for'.
- 'Oh, all right, hi! You look great!' "Nu ladno" is the sound of giving in. Sasha drops the polite hello and switches to "privet" and "ty".
- 'Thank you, my dear!' "Moya khoroshaya" is literally 'my good one'. Women say it warmly to a friend.
- 'When did you come back home to Moscow?' "V Moskvu", to Moscow: the city's name ends in -u when you're heading there. "Domoy" already means 'homeward', so it needs no 'to'.
- 'The day before yesterday, but I'm really bored here.' "Pozavchera" is one word, built on "vchera", 'yesterday'. "Mne skuchno", 'to me it's boring': that's 'I'm bored'.
- 'I'm thinking of flying off to Monaco soon!' "Uletet" is "letet", 'to fly', with u- in front, which adds 'away'. Rich people's boredom, in one line.
- 'I'll just check the reports and we'll go.' "Seychas" here means 'in a moment', not 'right now'. "Proveryu" looks present but means 'I will check', a job he'll finish.
- 'We'll go to the shop and buy presents!' "Poyedem" is 'we'll go' by car; on foot it would be "poydyom". "Magazin" looks like 'magazine' but means a shop.
