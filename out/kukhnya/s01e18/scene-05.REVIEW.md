## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Here you go, here's your delicious drink.' "Pozhaluysta" means please, and also 'here you go' as you hand something over. A "napitok" is a drink, from "pit", to drink.
- 'Kostya, you do a great job behind this bar.' "Za", behind, makes 'this counter' "etoy stoykoy". "Stoyka", a bar counter, comes from "stoyat", to stand.
- 'Thanks, I try to be very polite.' "Starayus", I try, belongs to "staratsya", to make an effort. After "byt", to be, polite becomes "vezhlivym".
- 'Guests always love fresh, tasty drinks.' "Lyubyat", they love. "Svezhie i vkusnye" both end in 'e' to match the plural drinks.
- 'Our bar has the very best drinks.' Literally 'in our bar the most best drinks', with no 'has' or 'is'. "Samye luchshie" doubles up for 'the very best'.
- 'People in the dining room will pay any money.' "Gotovy", ready, plus "platit", to pay. "Lyubye dengi", literally any money: whatever it costs.
- 'The main thing is to always do your job honestly.' "Glavnoe", the main thing, opens the sentence. "Svoyu rabotu": "svoy" means one's own, so it points back to whoever is working.
- 'But some bartenders know cunning tricks.' "Nekotorye", some, is "kotorye", which, with "ne" in front. "Khitrye sekrety", sly secrets.
- 'I don't want to deceive our guests.' "Khochu", I want, comes from "khotet", an irregular verb. "Obmanyvat" means to cheat, to fool.
- 'You're just too honest and kind a guy.' "Slishkom" means too, as in too much. "Paren" is a guy, a casual word between friends.
- 'But at least my conscience is clear.' "Zato" means 'but at least'. "U menya", at me, is how Russian says 'my' here; "chista", clear, is short and feminine to match "sovest".
- 'All right, keep pouring juice and wine.' "Prodolzhay", keep going, is followed by the plain "nalivat", to pour.
- 'I'll happily pour the guests some juice!' "S radostyu", literally 'with joy'. "Gostyam", to the guests, ends in "-am" because the juice goes to them.
