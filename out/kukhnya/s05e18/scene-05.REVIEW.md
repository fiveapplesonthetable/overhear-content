## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Good morning, Katya, hello to you.' Denis gently offers this morning greeting while facing Katya using "dobroye utro" ("good morning") — literally "kind morning", with the neuter ending matching "utro" ("morning").
- 'Oh, what a mess you have here.' Katya speaks at once in this moment, bringing things back to earth after the buildup.
- 'This junk should have been thrown out long ago.' Bringing up the junk during the talk, Katya reaches for the Russian word "khlam" ("junk") right at the heart of the line.
- 'Listen, I hid two tickets in there.' Talking about the tickets in front of Katya, Denis centers the thought on "bileta" ("tickets", from "bilet") without wasting a word.
- 'Those are tickets for tonight's concert.' Denis opens up frankly toward Katya, steering the talk right where he wants it.
- 'And you just threw them away.' Denis follows up vividly with Katya and leans on "vykinula" ("threw out", a form of "vykinut") to give the remark its edge.
- 'Oh, Denis, forgive me, please.' Katya pushes back openly right here and reaches for the Russian word "izvini" ("sorry, excuse me", built from "izvinit") right at the center of the line.
- 'I would have gone with great pleasure.' Katya pushes back right away facing Denis, refusing to let the moment slip past.
- 'But I have the evening shift today.' Emphasizing the shift duty to Denis, Katya chooses "vecherneye" ("evening", from the root "vecherniy") so Denis hears the exact meaning.
- 'Will you get them out of there yourself?' Answering evenly before Denis, Katya keeps normal statement word order here, turning the line into a question for Denis purely through rising pitch.
- 'Forgive me once again, alright?' Katya calmly chooses this informal apology while facing Denis using "izvini" ("sorry") — the casual singular apology you use with someone you know well.
- 'Alright, no big deal, I will get them myself.' Denis readily directs this word of agreement without hesitation using "ladno" ("alright") — built from the old Russian root for harmony and order, used every day for "okay, fine".
