## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I did not understand, what child of yours?' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'Katya, this is not your business at all.' Notice how Russian puts 'at all' and 'business' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'You promised that you would change your ways!' Here Katya anchors the thought on 'promised' and finishes on 'to ways', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'How will people look at you?' In everyday Russian, 'how / as' flows straight into 'to look, watch' without extra linking words.
- 'I am a grown man and will figure it out myself.' Chef Barinov pairs 'grown-up, adult' with 'I will sort it out' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'I know very well how you figure things out.' Notice how Russian puts 'wonderfully' and 'to understand well, to know one's stuff' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'You already abandoned two of your children!' Here Katya anchors the thought on 'already' and finishes on 'children', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'Yes, I did not live with my children.' In everyday Russian, 'lived' flows straight into 'children' without extra linking words.
- 'But I always did everything for you.' Chef Barinov pairs 'always' with 'did' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'You never lacked for anything.' A negative pronoun splits around its preposition before the plural past verb meaning lacked.
- 'After all, you studied in France itself!' He reminds her that he paid for her culinary schooling in France.
- 'I needed a father, not money.' Katya delivers the emotional core of the scene using a masculine short adjective of need in the past tense.
