## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Sasha, well, did you tell Max?' "Nu chto" is a casual 'so?'. "Skazala" has -la because Sasha is a woman, and "Maksu", 'to Max', takes the dative -u.
- 'Yes, I said I'd forgotten him.' "Skazala" and "zabyla" share the -la ending, because Sasha is talking about herself. "Chto" here means 'that', the word that joins the two halves.
- 'And how did he react to that?' "Otreagiroval" is the English 'react' with a Russian prefix and ending. Russians react 'on' something: "na eto", 'on that'.
- 'He was very surprised and upset.' "Udivilsya" and "rasstroilsya" both carry -sya, for 'got surprised' and 'got upset'. "Ochen silno", 'very strongly', together just means 'really'.
- 'I even felt a little sorry for him.' "Mne zhalko ego stalo" is literally 'to me pity him became'. The one who feels sorry is "mne", 'to me', and the one pitied is "ego".
- 'Just do not tell anyone the truth.' "Ne rasskazyvay" is 'don't tell', and Nastya means it as a plea. "Pravda" becomes "pravdu" as the thing not told.
- 'Of course, no one will find out anything.' "Nikto nichego ne uznaet" stacks three negatives, and Russian needs all of them. "Uznaet" is perfective, so it's future: 'will find out'.
- 'Thank you, you're a true, loyal friend!' "Nastoyashchiy" can be 'true' or 'real', and "vernyy" is 'loyal' or 'faithful'. Both have the masculine -iy, -yy to match "drug".
- 'Listen, maybe we should go somewhere?' A "mozhet" is a casual 'or maybe', used to suggest something. "Kuda-nibud" is 'somewhere', and the -nibud means 'some place or other'.
- 'Where exactly do you suggest going?' "Kuda" is 'where to', for movement. "Predlagaesh", 'you suggest', leads straight into "poyti", 'to go'.
- 'Let us go to the movies together.' "Kino", 'cinema', is borrowed and never changes its ending. "Davay skhodim" means 'let's go', for a trip there and back.
- 'This is needed for the believability of our plan.' "Pravdopodobnosti" joins "pravda", 'truth', and "podobnyy", 'similar', so literally 'truth-likeness'. After "dlya", 'for', it's in the genitive.
- 'Great idea, let us go tonight!' "Segodnya vecherom" is 'today in the evening', and the -om on "vecher" does the work of 'in'. "Poydyom", 'let's go', is a common way to say yes to a plan.
- 'Agreed, then let us meet after work.' "Dogovorilis", 'agreed', literally 'we've talked it into an agreement', closes the deal. After "posle", 'after', "rabota" becomes "raboty".
