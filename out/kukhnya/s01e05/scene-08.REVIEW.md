## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Who? Mom?' Two one-word questions. Mama is the word Russian children use, just like English 'mama'.
- 'And how does she know this number?' "Otkuda" is literally 'from where', and Russians use it for 'how come'. "Etot nomer" is 'this number'.
- 'Hello, Mom?' "Allo" is the hello you say when you pick up the phone. Face to face you'd say "privet" or "zdravstvuyte".
- 'Yes, it is Mom, your mom.' Barinov says "mama ya", 'mom I', with no 'am'. "Tvoya" is the feminine 'your', to go with "mama".
- 'You will cook trout with classic julienne.' "Budesh gotovit", 'you will cook', is "budu", 'will', in its 'you' form plus a plain verb. After "s", 'with', "zhyulen" becomes "zhyulenom".
- 'First, clean the fresh fish.' "Dlya nachala" is 'for a start'. "Vypotroshi" literally means 'gut it', which is exactly how you clean a fish.
- 'Then carefully remove the backbone.' "Potom" means 'then', 'after that'. "Akkuratno" looks like 'accurately' but means 'carefully' or 'neatly'.
- 'And put the fillet in the fridge.' "Kholodilnik", 'fridge', grows out of "kholod", 'cold'. "Polozhi" means 'put', for laying something down.
- 'Did you understand everything, Mommy?' "Mamulya" is an extra-tender 'mommy'. Coming from the chef, it's pure comedy.
- 'I will call you back later, I am busy.' "Perezvonyu" is "zvonit", 'to call', with "pere-", 'again'. "Nekogda" means 'there's no time', and "popozzhe" is 'a bit later'.
- 'Kisses to you, bye.' "Tseluyu tebya", 'I kiss you', is how Russians end calls with family. Then "poka", the casual 'bye'.
- 'That was Mom calling from Voronezh.' "Zvonila" ends in -la to agree with "mama". After "iz", 'from', Voronezh, a city south of Moscow, becomes "Voronezha".
