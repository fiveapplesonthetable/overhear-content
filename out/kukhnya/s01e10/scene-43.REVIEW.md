## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, pour me some more wine.' "Pour" is the polite command. "Wine" takes the "some of" ending. The guest sounds relaxed, and the chef is on show.
- 'What an incredibly delicious black caviar you have!' "Incredibly" is built on "believe", so it's "unbelievably". "At you" is Russian for "you have". The guest praises the dyed pike.
- 'Of course, this is the best caviar straight from Astrakhan.' "Straight from" is "pryamo iz". Barinov leans on Astrakhan, the guests' own favourite. His lie is a good one.
- 'We fly it here on special Boeings.' "Transport regularly" is the word, here with a plane. "Boeings" is plural and has the "by means of" ending. Barinov's lie gets wilder, and the guests are charmed.
- 'Well, in taste everything is simply excellent!' "By taste" uses the small word for "by". The guest is satisfied. He has no idea what he has tasted.
- 'In our restaurant it cannot be any other way.' "Otherwise" and "cannot be" are two short phrases. Barinov preens, and it's his best performance.
- 'Waiter, what is the score in the match now?' "Score" echoes the one in the owner's scene. "In the match" has the ending for "in". He asks a waiter in the middle of a sales pitch.
- 'The score there is three-three now, our guys tied it up!' "Played back" is how Russian says "equalised". "Ours" is a way to say "our guys", our team. The guest shares the football news.
- 'They had not tied it up like that for twenty years in a row.' "In a row" is one word. "Twenty years" uses "let", the form after twenty. The guest is amazed, and so is everyone.
- 'And today they suddenly scored three quick goals!' "Suddenly" is the "vdrug" from the cooks' scene. "Hit in" is how you score a goal. The guest recounts it with joy.
- 'Did Spartak really let victory slip away in the end again?!' "Let slip away" is a strong word, as if victory were a fish. "Really" is "neuzheli". Barinov is on the edge of tears.
- 'Football today turned out very nervous and strange.' "Turned out" stands for a result. "Nervous" is easy to spot. The guest speaks like a commentator.
- 'Indeed, they play like real slackers!' "Da uzh" is a sarcastic "yes, indeed". "Slackers" comes from "without business", people with nothing to do. Barinov says it bitterly.
