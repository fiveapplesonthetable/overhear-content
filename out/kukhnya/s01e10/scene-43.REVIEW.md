## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, pour me some more wine.' "Nalejte" is "pour", the polite form of Barinov's "nalej mne krepkogo chyornogo chaya". "Vina" is "some wine", from "vino". "Eshchyo nemnogo" is "a little more".
- 'What an incredibly delicious black caviar you have!' "Neveroyatno" is "unbelievably", built on "verit", "to believe". "U vas" is "at you", the Russian for "you have". The guest praises the dyed pike.
- 'Of course, this is the best caviar straight from Astrakhan.' "Pryamo iz Astrakhani" is "straight from Astrakhan", the city known for its caviar. "Luchshaya" is "best". Barinov sells the story.
- 'We fly it here on special Boeings.' "Vozim" is "we transport", for carrying again and again by vehicle. "Boingami" is "by Boeings", the plane name with a Russian ending. The story gets bigger.
- 'Well, everything is simply excellent!' "Nu chto" is "well then", a way to sum up. "Vsyo prosto otlichno" is "everything is simply excellent". The guest is satisfied.
- 'In our restaurant it cannot be any other way.' "Inache i byt ne mozhet" is "otherwise cannot even be", a fixed phrase. "V nashem restorane" is "in our restaurant". Barinov enjoys the praise.
- 'Waiter, what is the score in the match now?' "Schyot" is "score", from Senya's promise to watch it: "sledit za schyotom". "V matche" is "in the match".
- 'The score there is three-three now, our guys tied it up!' Fans say "nashi", "ours", for "our team". "Otygralis" is "igrat", "to play", with ot- and -sya: playing your way back level. "Schyot" means the score here, and also a bill.
- 'They had not tied it up like that for twenty years in a row.' In a row is "podryad". "Dvadtsat let" is "twenty years", with "let" after the number. The guest is amazed.
- 'And today they suddenly scored three quick goals!' "Vdrug" is "suddenly", heard in Fedya's "A vdrug shef uvidit". "Zabili gol" is "hammered in a goal", how Russians say "scored".
- 'Did Spartak really let victory slip away in the end again?!' "Neuzheli" is "can it really be", as in Max's "Neuzheli shef — tvoy blizkiy rodstvennik?". "Upustil" is "let slip", as if victory had fallen from his hands.
- 'Football today turned out very nervous and strange.' "Poluchilsya" is "turned out", as in Senya's "poluchaetsya". "Nervny" and "stranny" are "nervous" and "strange".
- 'Indeed, they play like real slackers!' "Da uzh" is a sarcastic "yes, indeed". "Bezdelniki", "slackers", is "bez" plus "delo": people "without business". Barinov says it bitterly.
