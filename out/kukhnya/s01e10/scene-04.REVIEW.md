## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hello, I am listening to you attentively.' "Ya vas vnimatelno slushayu", "I you attentively listen", is a set polite opening for a business talk, with the formal "vas". Barinov is playing the gentleman.
- 'You performed wonderfully on our culinary show.' "Vystupali" means "performed", appeared in front of an audience. "Kulinarnom" is "culinary", and "shou" is borrowed and never changes.
- 'And what did your strict producers decide?' "Prodyusery" is borrowed from English "producers". "Reshili" is "decided", with the plural -li. "Vashi strogie" is "your strict", both plural to match. He goes straight to business.
- 'We want to offer you a permanent job.' "Postoyannuyu", "permanent", grows out of "postoyanno", "constantly". "Predlozhit vam" is "offer to you", with the polite "vam". It sounds like good news, until she names the sum.
- 'At how much did you value my work?' "Vo skolko", "into how much", is Russian for "at what price". "Otsenili" is "valued" or "rated". It's a blunt way to ask about money.
- 'Ten thousand for each new broadcast.' "Peredacha", "broadcast", is something passed on, from "peredat". "Za kazhduyu" is "for each": "za" here means "in exchange for". She says it brightly, as if it were a lot.
- 'Ten thousand euros for one episode?' "Evro" never changes its ending. "Vypusk", "episode" or "issue", is something released, from "vypustit", "to let out". Barinov hopes she means euros.
- 'No, we pay ten thousand rubles.' "Platim" is "we pay", from the same family as "zaplatili", "paid", in the cake scene. After "desyat tysyach", "rubl" becomes "rubley".
- 'Then goodbye, or rather farewell!' "Do svidaniya" is "until seeing", until we meet again. "Proshchayte" is a final farewell. So Barinov means he doesn't want to see her again, ever.
- 'Wait, Viktor, let us calmly discuss everything.' "Podozhdite" and "davayte" both end in -te: the polite "wait" and "let's". "Obsudim" is "we will discuss". A television woman knows how to keep a man in the room.
- 'You propose that I work on my day off.' Hidden inside "vykhodnoy den" is "vykhodit", "to go out": a day off is a day you get out. Notice "v moy vykhodnoy", with "v" for "on" a day. Barinov is already hunting for a reason to say no.
- 'This will make your restaurant very famous.' "Izvestnym" is "famous", literally "known"; for the pastry chef it was "znamenity", which is stronger. "Sdelaet" is "will make". She changes tack, from his fee to the restaurant's name.
- 'I do not work for such ridiculous kopecks!' A hundred "kopeyki" make a ruble, so it's what English calls peanuts. "Smeshnye", "ridiculous", is the word for "funny". "Za takie" is "for such".
