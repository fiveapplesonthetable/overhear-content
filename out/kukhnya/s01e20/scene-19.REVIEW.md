## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Eleonora Andreyevna, please, have a seat.' Russian proshu literally means 'I ask', and a host uses it to invite you to sit or go first.
- 'Eat up, eat up, the owner is paying.' The word khozyain means 'owner' or 'host', the one whose place it is.
- 'Oh, look who's here. Hello.' Russian says smotri, kto prishyol, literally 'look who has come', and zdravstvuyte is the polite hello.
- 'Am I interrupting?' Russian asks ne pomeshayu, literally 'won't I get in the way?', when joining a table.
- 'Viktor Petrovich, I know what happened.' The phrase v kurse literally means 'in the course', and Russian uses it for 'in the know'.
- 'I really feel for you.' The word sochuvstvuyu literally means 'I feel together with', and it takes vam, 'to you'.
- 'You're a real professional.' Russian uses nastoyashchiy, 'real, genuine', where English might say 'true'.
- 'What are you even talking about?' Russian leaves out the verb: vy voobshche o chyom, literally 'you at all about what?'.
- 'You and I went through a rough patch.' Russian says u nas s vami, literally 'at us with you', for 'you and I had'.
- 'But thanks to you, it all worked out.' Russian says vsyo poluchilos, literally 'everything came out', for 'it worked out'.
- 'Thank you for being you.' Russian says spasibo vam za vas, literally 'thank you for you', a set phrase for toasts.
- 'Did you sell the restaurant?' Russian starts with ty chto, literally 'you what', to show shock, and prodal ends in -l for a man.
- 'Dima, how could you? We fixed everything.' The little word zhe adds 'but we did!', and kak ty mog means 'how could you'.
- 'Don't yell. It's business. Just business.' Russian borrows biznes from English, and ne ori, 'don't yell', is a blunt thing to say.
- 'Trust me, it's better to sell on the way up.' The phrase na podyome literally means 'on the rise', and luchshe means 'better'.
