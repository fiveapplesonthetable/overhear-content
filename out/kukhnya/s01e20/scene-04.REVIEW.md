## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hi. I wanted to ask about the present.' The word naschyot means 'about' or 'regarding', and podarok, 'present', becomes podarka after it.
- 'Did you like this beautiful ring?' Russian turns it round: tebe ponravilos, literally 'to you it was pleasing'. The ring is the subject.
- 'Yes, it's very beautiful.' The ring, koltso, is a neuter word, so 'it' is ono and 'beautiful' is krasivoe.
- 'And do you know how much it costs?' The word skolko means 'how much'. For 'it costs', Russian says stoit, literally 'it stands'.
- 'Do you think that's normal for a present?' Here schitaesh means 'you think' or 'you reckon'. After dlya, 'for', podarok becomes podarka.
- 'Of course. Tanya was choosing a present for herself.' The word sebya, 'oneself', is the same for every person: for herself, himself, myself.
- 'She just sent me a photo.' The word prosto means 'just' or 'simply'. The full word fotografiya ends in -ya, so after 'sent' it becomes fotografiyu.
- 'But I wanted to give you the ring myself.' The word sam means 'myself' here. And podarit, 'to give', is only for presents.
- 'She picked my present.' Max means Tanya took his idea. The word vybrala, 'picked', ends in -la because Tanya is a woman.
- 'I'll tell her to choose something else.' The phrase chto-to drugoe means 'something else'; drugoe on its own means 'other'.
- 'And you'll give me this ring.' The word podarish means 'you will give', a promise, not something happening now.
- 'What for? She's already bought it.' The little zhe, said twice, makes it sound obvious: 'she's bought it already, hasn't she?'
- 'No, she was just asking for advice.' After prosila, 'asked for', sovet, 'advice', becomes soveta.
- 'I'll call her now.' Russian rings 'to' someone: pozvonyu ey, literally 'I'll ring to her'.
