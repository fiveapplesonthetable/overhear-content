## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I'm not going to celebrate my birthday.' In ne budu otmechat, budu, 'I will be', plus the dictionary form otmechat makes a plain future. 'Birthday', den rozhdeniya, is literally 'day of birth'.
- 'And there's no need to give me anything.' The word darit means 'to give as a present', and it comes from dar, 'gift'.
- 'Maybe we'll just sit with Max.' The word posidim is 'we'll sit for a bit': the po- in front makes it a short, relaxed stretch. For Russians, sitting with someone means a quiet evening together.
- 'Max, are you awake?' Russian asks 'have you woken up?', prosnulsya, where English asks 'are you awake?'.
- 'Yes, I've only just woken up.' The word tolko usually means 'only', but before a past form like this it means 'just now'.
- 'And I've already made coffee.' The word svarila ends in -la because Vika is a woman; Max would say svaril. It literally means 'boiled', and it's the normal word for making coffee.
- 'Good morning, thanks for the coffee.' After spasibo, 'thanks', the thing you're thankful for comes after za. The borrowed word kofe never changes its ending.
- 'Have you seen my watch?' Chasy, 'watch' or 'clock', exists only in the plural, so 'my' is the plural moi. Asking with ne, 'you haven't seen...?', is a soft, everyday way to ask.
- 'Oh, you mean this watch?' Her ty pro eti is literally 'you about these', a quick way to say 'you mean these?'.
- 'Maybe you should buy a new one?' Tebe kupit, 'for you to buy', turns into a gentle suggestion once mozhet, 'maybe', comes in front.
- 'This watch barely works any more.' The word khodyat means 'they walk': a Russian watch walks when it works, and yele means 'barely'.
- 'No, it's my granddad's antique.' The word dedovsky, 'granddad's', is made from ded, 'grandfather', with a describing ending added.
