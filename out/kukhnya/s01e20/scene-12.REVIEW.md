## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Here's your coffee.' The verb "derzhi", 'hold', is how Russian hands something over: 'here, take it'.
- 'There we go, thanks, Kostyan.' Friends stretch Kostya into "Kostyan", a rough, matey form of the name.
- 'Listen, this side job has worn me out.' A "podrabotka" is extra work on the side, and "ustal", 'tired', ends in -l for a man.
- 'Could you help me drag the bags?' Russian asks a favour with "ne pomozhesh", literally 'won't you help?', which sounds softer.
- 'I will. Why the long face?' Russian calls a gloomy person "kisly", literally 'sour', like a lemon.
- 'I couldn't get out of the serious talk.' Russian says "ne poluchilos", literally 'it didn't work out', for 'I couldn't'.
- 'And what did she tell you?' The verb "skazala", 'said', ends in -la because the one who spoke is a woman.
- 'She said she's invited her parents for the weekend.' Russian says "na vykhodnye", literally 'for the days off', for 'for the weekend'.
- 'What do you think that means?' The phrase "eto znachit", 'it means', is the same "znachit" that Russians also use for 'so'.
- 'It means you're in big trouble, buddy.' Russian says "u tebya problemy", literally 'at you are problems', for 'you have problems'.
- 'They expect you to ask for her hand.' Just as in English, "poprosit ruki", 'to ask for a hand', means asking to marry her.
- 'Wow.' The phrase "nichego sebe" literally means 'nothing to oneself', but Russians say it for 'wow!'.
- 'So that means I need a ring now?' The word "poluchaetsya", literally 'it comes out', means 'so it turns out'.
- 'If that's the only problem, then yes.' Russian links the two halves with "esli... to", literally 'if... then'.
- 'So we both need rings.' The word "koltsa" is the plural of "koltso", 'ring', and "nam oboim" means 'to us both'.
- 'Yes, with just one small correction.' The ending -oy in "s odnoy malenkoy popravkoy" comes from "s", 'with'.
- 'I don't have to propose to anyone.' Russian stacks negatives: "nikomu ne nado" is literally 'to nobody not needed'.
- 'Well then, Daddy will feed everyone.' The word "papochka" is a sweet little form of "papa", here said with a big grin.
- 'We had a hard time, and now look at us.' Russian says "von kak", literally 'there's how', to mean 'look how things turned out'.
- 'We should thank our team somehow.' A "kollektiv" is what Russians call the whole staff at a workplace.
- 'Tell people that on Saturday we work till twelve.' Russian says "v subbotu", 'on Saturday', so "subbota" changes its ending to -u.
- 'And then we have a staff party.' A "korporativ" is the party a company throws for its own staff.
