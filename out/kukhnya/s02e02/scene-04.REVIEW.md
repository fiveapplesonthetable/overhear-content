## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hi.' In Russian, 'hi' leads into 'hi' in a very natural everyday phrasing.
- 'Hi, Max.' Notice the word order: 'hi' comes first, and 'Max' finishes the sentence.
- 'How are you?' Sasha puts 'how' up front so the listener immediately catches the tone, followed by 'things'.
- 'I've been better.' The key word here means 'to happen', which Max combines with 'better'.
- 'Are you still worrying?' The key word here means 'more', which Sasha combines with 'to worry'. Max answers straight away.
- 'Well, no.' Max starts with 'yes' and pairs it with 'no' to make the point clear.
- 'With Vika everything is in the past.' Max puts 'Vika' up front so the listener immediately catches the tone, followed by 'last'.
- 'Are you sure?' Russian leaves out 'am', 'is', or 'are' in the present tense, linking 'you' straight to 'sure'. Then Max replies.
- 'Of course.' The first content word means 'of course', and 'of course' rounds out the thought.
- 'Well, okay.' A compact expression built around 'oh' and 'all right'.
- 'It's time for me to work.' Listen for the word meaning 'to me' right alongside 'it's time' in this line. And Max jumps in.
- 'See you.' The reflexive ending on the verb for 'will see each other' turns the action back onto the subject.
