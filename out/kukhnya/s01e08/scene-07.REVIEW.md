## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I repeat for everyone, the order is already waiting!' You say povtoryayu, 'I repeat', when nobody reacted the first time. After dlya, 'for', vse becomes vsekh.
- 'Elena, they heard you perfectly well.' The form uslyshali means they did hear her and took it in. The polite vas, 'you', is the same vy Barinov uses for her all day.
- 'Why then is nobody working?' Russian keeps both negatives: nikto ne rabotayet, 'nobody not works'. Leaving out ne would sound wrong.
- 'In my kitchen there is always strict order.' Barinov echoes Elena's line from the studio, na moyey kukhne, swapping her idealnyy, 'perfect', for strogiy, 'strict'. Russians use strogiy poryadok about schools, armies and kitchens.
- 'You work too slowly for a restaurant.' As in English, slishkom, 'too', sits in front of medlenno, 'slowly'. After dlya, restoran gains an -a.
- 'Every good dish needs its own time.' With nuzhno, the one who needs gets a 'to' ending: kazhdomu khoroshemu blyudu, 'to every good dish'. Here svoyo, 'its own', points back to the dish.
- 'Guests do not like to wait long for food.' English waits for food, but Russian simply waits the food: zhdat yedu. As the object, yeda becomes yedu.
- 'Our guests appreciate high taste.' The word tsenyat, 'they value', shares a root with tsena, 'price'. And vkus is both the flavour of food and a person's good taste.
- 'I will show myself how one must work!' The feminine sama, 'myself', means 'I'll do it with my own hands'. To say 'how it should be done', Russian uses kak nado, 'how it's necessary', here kak nado rabotat.
- 'Great, I will go calmly rest.' In everyday speech two actions can sit side by side with no 'and': poydu otdokhnu, 'I'll go rest'. Adding spokoyno, 'calmly', makes it sound even more relaxed.
- 'Are you abandoning your kitchen to me?' Literally 'to throw', brosat also means to abandon someone or something. With na menya, 'onto me', it's dumping a job on someone.
- 'I am sure that you will manage excellently.' The short form uveren, 'sure', is a man's; Elena would say uverena. In spravites he uses the same spravitsya, 'to cope', that the whole bet is about.
- 'Return soon, otherwise I will take the restaurant!' The -tes at the end of vozvrashchaytes makes 'come back' polite, for vy. Then inache, 'otherwise', brings in the threat.
- 'We'll see who stays the winner here!' The single word posmotrim, 'we'll see', means 'time will tell'. After ostatsya, 'to remain', the role ends in -em: pobeditelem.
