## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'How did you find me, Ainura?' Barinov puts "ty" first and "kak", how, second, which makes the question sound more surprised. Then "nashla", found, has the -a because Ainura is a woman.
- 'I have been working for you for a long time.' Russian keeps the present, "rabotayu", for something that started long ago and is still true. Then "u tebya" means 'at yours': working at someone's place means working for them.
- 'I know all your secret places.' Secret, "taynye", comes from "tayna", a secret. In "vse tvoi" you get 'all your', the "ty" kind of 'your'.
- 'All right, and why did you come?' Barinov says "prishla", came, the word for arriving on foot: Russian picks the word by how you travel. And "zachem" asks 'what for', about purpose.
- 'Dear Chef, you need to return to the kitchen.' Going to the kitchen is "na kukhnyu", with -yu for a direction; being in the kitchen is "na kukhne". And "tebe nuzhno" is 'to you it is necessary'.
- 'You sound right now like my former mother-in-law.' A "tyoshcha" is specifically a wife's mother; a husband's mother is "svekrov". And "byvshaya" means former, as in an ex.
- 'Seriously, it is very hard for the cooks without you.' Ainura says "povaram", 'to the cooks', since in Russian hardship happens to someone. With "bez tebya", without you, "ty" turns into "tebya".
- 'They are not coping at all with orders.' To cope with something is "spravlyatsya s", the everyday phrase for handling work. After "s", the orders, "zakazy", become "zakazami".
- 'The new female chef will soon fall completely ill.' A "devochka" is a little girl, so "devochka-shef" is mildly dismissive. And "zaboleet", will get ill, is built on "bolet", to be ill.
- 'Are you sure you are not deceiving me?' Literally 'exactly', "tochno" works as 'for sure' in a question. To deceive, "obmanyvat", is a stronger word than "vrat", to lie.
- 'When have I ever lied to you?' Ainura's "obmanyvala" asks about any time at all, which is why it reads as 'ever'. Adding "voobshche", in general, makes it sharper.
- 'All right, let us drink to victory then.' Put "davay" before a 'we' form like "vypyem" and you have the Russian 'let's'. A toast uses "za", for: "za pobedu", to victory.
- 'Chef, I am at work after all!' It's the same line Ainura said earlier, "na rabote" with "zhe", and it gets funnier the second time. Hearing it twice is how you'll remember it.
