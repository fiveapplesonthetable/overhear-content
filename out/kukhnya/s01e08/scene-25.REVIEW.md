## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vika, look at me. What is this?' To look at someone is "posmotri na menya", with "na", at, before the person. Then "eto chto takoe" is a blunt 'what is this?', the question you ask when you already suspect the answer.
- 'Chef, this is my new little dog!' Vika's "sobachka" is "sobaka", dog, with the -chka ending that makes it small and sweet. Russians use these endings constantly with animals.
- 'I bought her this morning.' The word "utrom", in the morning, is "utro" with -om, and it needs no 'in'. Vika says "kupila", with the -a of a woman speaking.
- 'Why do you need a dog of the same breed?' Barinov's "zachem tebe" is literally 'what for to you', the short way to ask why someone needs something. Then "takoy zhe" means 'the same', and "porody" is "poroda", breed, in its 'of' form.
- 'I just really liked your Dyson.' The dog's real name is Tyson, and Vika's "Dayson" is the slip. Liking runs backwards in Russian: "mne ponravilsya", literally 'to me he was pleasing'.
- 'I could not resist and bought a puppy.' Saying "ne uderzhalas" means 'couldn't hold myself back', from "derzhat", to hold. Her "pyosik" is "pyos", dog, with the small, affectionate -ik.
- 'But they are completely identical!' You can hear "odin", one, at the root of "odinakovye", identical. The "zhe" after "no", but, adds the feeling of 'but surely'.
- 'Sorry, I still mix them up.' Literally 'up to these times', "do sikh por" means 'still' or 'to this day'. And "putayu", I mix up, goes back to "putat", to confuse.
- 'It seems this one is yours, and this one is mine.' Starting with "kazhetsya", it seems, is how Russians soften a guess. Then "vot eto vasha, a vot eta moya" points at each dog in turn, with the polite "vasha" for her boss.
- 'Vika, what is your dog's name?' Russian asks "kak zovut", literally 'how do they call', so the dog is the one being called: "tvoego psa". And "pyos" drops its o to become "psa".
- 'Mine? His name is Maxik!' The answer "ego zovut", his name is, follows the same 'they call him' pattern. The name "Maksik" is Max with a cuddly -ik, so she has named the dog after her colleague.
- 'And what breed is this dog?' Russian says the breed is "u etoy sobaki", 'at this dog', which is how you say something has a thing. After "u" the dog takes its 'of' endings.
- 'Breed sushi... Oh, Shih Tzu!' The little "oy" is the Russian 'oops'. And "shitsu" and sushi sound close enough that the mix-up is easy.
- 'Just like your dog. I will go!' Literally 'as also', "kak i" means 'just like'. Saying "ya poydu", I'll go, is a common way to excuse yourself and leave.
