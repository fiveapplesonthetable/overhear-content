## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vika, look at me. What is this?' 'Look at me' is a command plus a short phrase. 'What is this' has a small extra word, so it sounds indignant.
- 'Chef, this is my new little dog!' 'Little dog' adds the ending 'ka', which makes the word small and affectionate. 'My new' ends in 'aya', to match.
- 'I bought her this morning.' 'Bought' ends in 'a' because Vika is a woman. 'Her' goes before the action, as in a quick explanation.
- 'Why do you need a dog of the same breed?' 'The same' is one word, and 'of the breed' puts the 'of' ending on the breed. 'To you' is how Russian says 'do you need'.
- 'I just really liked your Dyson.' Vika gets the dog's name wrong, and 'Dyson' is a vacuum-cleaner brand, so it comes out as a funny slip. 'To me pleased' is the Russian way of saying 'I liked'.
- 'I could not resist and bought a puppy.' 'Could not resist' ends in 'las', and it literally means 'did not hold myself back'. 'puppy' is a diminutive of 'dog'.
- 'But they are completely identical!' 'Identical' is made from 'one' and 'kind'. 'Completely' sits before it.
- 'Sorry, I still mix them up.' 'Sorry' is the respectful command to 'excuse me'. 'Still' is literally 'until these times'.
- 'It seems this one is yours, and this one is mine.' 'This is yours' ends in 'a' to match the dog, which is one of the she-words. 'And' at the start of each half sets up the contrast.
- 'Vika, what is your dog's name?' Russian says 'how do they call your dog', with nobody named. 'Your dog' takes a changed form for a male dog.
- 'Mine? His name is Maxik!' 'His name is' is literally 'they call him'. 'Maxik' is a pet form of Max.
- 'And what breed is this dog?' 'What breed' is a pair of words, and 'at this dog' stands for 'this dog's'. 'Breed' counts as a she-word.
- 'Breed sushi... Oh, Shih Tzu!' Vika blurts out a Japanese food word instead of the dog's name. 'Oh' is a casual way to correct yourself.
- 'Just like your dog. I will go!' 'Just like' is two words, 'as and'. 'I will go' is how Russians say 'I'm off'.
