## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Dmitry, this was your stupid idea!' The -a on byla, 'was', comes from ideya, not from eto. The informal tvoya, 'your', shows these two are on ty terms.
- 'Stop shouting, it turned out a great show!' Russians stop someone with khvatit, 'enough', plus the action: khvatit krichat. The -o of vyshlo, 'it came out', matches shou.
- 'Rivals in life — rivals on screen.' Each place takes its own location ending: v zhizni, na ekrane. Nothing links the two halves except the dash.
- 'You just wanted to make me angry.' Built on zloy, 'angry, wicked', razozlit means 'to make angry'. The bare -l of khotel, 'wanted', is for a man.
- 'And it worked, you got angry perfectly!' The phrase u menya poluchilos, literally 'by me it worked out', means 'I managed'. Adding -sya turns razozlit into razozlilsya, 'you got angry yourself'.
- 'Now that woman will be in my kitchen.' Said like this, eta zhenshchina, 'that woman', sounds cold. And budet, 'will be', is the future of byt, 'to be'.
- 'She proposed the bet and will work the shift honestly.' Here predlozhila, 'she proposed', ends in -la because it's about Elena. The word chestno, 'honestly', comes from chest, 'honour'.
- 'Viktor, get your cooks in order.' The phrase privedi v poryadok, literally 'lead into order', means 'sort out'. Because cooks are people, svoi povara becomes svoikh povarov as the object.
- 'My cooks always work perfectly.' Barinov's idealno, 'perfectly', is the 'how' form of idealnyy. Elena used the other form about her own kitchen on air: idealnyy poryadok.
- 'I shouldn't feel ashamed before the guests.' Russian puts the person who feels shame in the 'to me' form: mne stydno, 'to me it's shameful'. In front of the guests is pered gostyami, with -ami after pered.
- 'Don't worry, everything will be at the highest level.' Between friends, ne perezhivay is the everyday 'don't worry'. The set phrase na vysshem urovne, 'at the highest level', means 'first-class'.
- 'Great, then I am calm about the restaurant.' The short masculine spokoyen, 'calm', is what a man says about his own state. Russians are calm or worried za something, literally 'for' it: za restoran.
- 'I will go meet our new worker.' Saying poydu, 'I'll go', plus the action is a common way to announce what you're off to do. The word rabotnik, 'worker', is built from rabota, 'work'; said of Elena, it's a dig.
- 'Good luck in the kitchen, chef!' The ending of udachi comes from an unspoken 'I wish you', which wants the 'of' ending. Between friends it's udachi tebe; to someone you call vy, it's udachi vam.
