## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, I have almost finished this cake.' Louis uses the term for "finished" that ends in the man's -l, as Max did. It's a modest, tired sentence. A pastry chef who has been up since dawn does not shout about it.
- 'Show it to me, Louis.' It's the same short command the chef used with Alisa's face. There's no "please" and no "you". Barinov is a man who has seen too many cakes go wrong, and he wants to look first.
- 'I think the cake can be served soon.' The russian for "it's possible" is the opposite of the "not allowed" word from the chocolate talk. Louis says it with caution, as if he knew something. He is hoping.
- 'Wait, what on earth is this?' The Russian is "this still what such", and the "still" adds the "on earth". It's the standard way to ask what something odd is. Barinov has seen it, and it can't be good.
- 'Oh, this is an iron clamp!' The word for iron grows from the Russian for the metal, and it takes a feminine ending to match the clamp. Louis is horrified, and rightly so. A tool in a banquet cake is a disaster.
- 'Where did it come from in the cream?' This is the third "where" word, the one that means "from where". The verb literally says "took itself", which is how Russian says that something has come to be somewhere. Barinov asks as calmly as he can.
- 'This is a celebration cake for the guests!' The term for "festive" grows from the Russian for holiday. Barinov is outraged, because a tool in the cake would ruin the whole evening, and a hundred thousand rubles with it.
- 'Probably the little girl was playing here.' Louis names the culprit without saying her name. He means Alisa, of course. The girl's past ending is a small clue, and so is the word "here", pointing at the pastry bench.
- 'Did Alisa touch your cake?' It is the same word Max used for the knife, now with a girl's ending. There is no question word, only the voice rising. Barinov's anger is already building.
- 'Chef, calm down, no need to shout.' The adverb for "calmly" works alone as "calm down". Louis is the nervous one here, and he tries to soothe. "Not needed to shout" is the "needed" pattern again.
- 'I will quickly fix everything right now.' The word for "fix" grows from the Russian for "right" or "correct", so it means "make right". Louis rolls up his sleeves. He knows how to rescue a cake, and he is calm about it.
- 'Make it pretty so the guests notice nothing.' "Make beautifully" is how Russian says it: the adverb of "beautiful". The "so that" word is back from the baking lesson. The "nothing noticed" part has the double negative.
- 'I promise, the cake will be perfect.' It's the same word Alisa used when she promised to be good. And "perfect" is the word "ideal" with a Russian ending. Louis keeps his word, and the cake saves the evening.
