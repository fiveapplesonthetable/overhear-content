## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Well, I've found your cook.' 'Vot ya i nashyol' is literally 'here I have found', and 'vot... i' gives it the sense of 'there we are'. 'Vashego povara', 'your cook', takes '-ogo' and '-a' because he is the one found.
- 'Fedya, why didn't you hide?' Barinov says 'ty' to his cooks. 'Spryatalsya', 'hid', ends in '-sya', 'oneself', so it is literally 'hid himself'.
- 'You shouldn't have lied to me, Viktor Petrovich.' 'Zrya' means 'for nothing' or 'in vain', and 'zrya vy...' is the usual way to say 'you shouldn't have'. 'Obmanyvat' means 'to deceive'.
- 'I wanted to protect my best cook.' 'Zashchitit' means 'to protect', from 'shchit', 'shield'. 'Svoego' means 'my own' here, pointing back to Barinov.
- 'You should have owned up to everything right away.' 'Nuzhno bylo', literally 'it was needed', is the usual Russian 'should have'. 'Priznatsya vo vsyom' means 'to confess to everything'.
- 'Then you would have got off with a simple warning.' 'By' after a past verb turns it into 'would': 'poluchili by', 'would have got'. 'Preduprezhdenie', 'warning', comes from 'predupredit', 'to warn'.
- 'Maybe we can work something out?' 'Dogovorimsya' means 'we'll come to an agreement', and in a moment like this everyone hears it as a hint at a bribe. 'Mozhet' here is short for 'mozhet byt', 'maybe'.
- 'No, it's too late now.' 'Pozdno' means 'late', and 'uzhe' means 'already', so it is literally 'now already late'. Russian needs no 'it is' here.
- 'Unfortunately, you'll have to pay a fine.' 'K sozhaleniyu' is literally 'to regret', the everyday 'unfortunately'. 'Vam pridyotsya', literally 'to you it will come', means 'you'll have to'.
- 'All right, we'll pay.' 'Ladno' is the grudging 'all right', a step below 'khorosho'. 'Zaplatim', 'we'll pay', is the future of 'zaplatit', 'to pay up'.
- 'But that's far from all.' Russian says 'daleko ne vsyo', literally 'far not all'. 'Eshchyo', 'still' or 'yet', adds the feeling that more is coming.
- 'The young man will have to be deported.' 'Molodoy chelovek', 'young man', is also how you address a young stranger. 'Deportirovat' is a borrowed verb, and the '-irovat' ending turns many foreign words into Russian verbs.
- 'How am I going to work without Fedya?' 'Bez', 'without', changes 'Fedya' to 'Fedi'. 'Budu rabotat' means 'I will be working', the future of a verb that describes an ongoing activity.
