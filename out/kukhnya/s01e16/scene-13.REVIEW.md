## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'The inspector is here.' Russian needs no 'is': 'inspektor zdes' is literally 'inspector here'. 'Zdes' and 'tut' both mean 'here', and 'tut' is a little more casual.
- 'I know, I saw him.' 'Znayu', 'I know', needs no 'ya' because the '-yu' ending already says 'I'. 'Videl', 'saw', ends in '-l', the past for a man.
- 'He wants to check the kitchen.' 'Khotet', 'to want', is irregular: 'ya khochu', 'I want', but 'on khochet', 'he wants'. 'Kukhnya', 'kitchen', becomes 'kukhnyu' because it is what gets checked.
- 'Let him check.' 'Pust' means 'let', and it goes before the plain 'he checks', 'proveryaet'. It's the shrug of someone who has nothing to hide, or pretends not to.
- 'He is looking for illegals.' 'Nelegaly' is built from 'ne', 'not', and 'legal', the same word as in English. 'Ishchet', 'is looking for', changes it to 'nelegalov', for people looked for.
- 'We don't have them.' 'U nas ikh net' is literally 'at us them not'. 'Net' here means 'there isn't', and 'ikh', 'them', comes in front of it.
- 'Where is Fedya?' The name is the short, friendly form of 'Fyodor'. Russian needs no 'is' in a 'gde' question: 'gde Fedya?' is literally 'where Fedya?'
- 'He is in the fridge.' After 'v', 'in', 'kholodilnik' becomes 'kholodilnike', the ending for where something is. Senya doesn't need to say more, because the fridge is the whole problem.
- 'As long as he doesn't freeze in there.' 'Tolko by' means 'if only' or 'as long as', for a worried wish. 'Zamyorz' comes from 'moroz', 'frost', so it is literally 'got frosted'.
- 'Viktor Petrovich, would you be so kind.' 'Bud'te lyubezny' is literally 'be kind', said before a polite request. It's what a very formal person says, or someone being formal on purpose.
- 'Open the fridge, please.' 'Otkroyte', 'open', ends in '-yte', the command ending for 'vy'. 'Pozhaluysta', 'please', also means 'you're welcome'.
- 'I'm waiting, Viktor Petrovich.' 'Zhdu', 'I'm waiting', comes from 'zhdat', 'to wait'. The major puts the chef's name after it, which makes the polite words sound like pressure.
