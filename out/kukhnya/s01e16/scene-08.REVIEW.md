## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Max, did you hear about the inspection?' 'Pro', 'about', is the casual partner of the more formal 'o'. After 'pro', 'proverka' becomes 'proverku'.
- 'No, I didn't.' Russian answers with the verb itself, 'ne slyshal', literally 'not heard'. There is no short 'I didn't' to lean on.
- 'There is an inspection at the restaurant next door right now.' In Russian, 'v sosednem restorane', 'in the neighboring restaurant', changes both words to say where.
- 'I have no time for that right now.' 'Mne ne do etogo' is literally 'to me not up to this', the everyday way to say you're too busy to care.
- 'They are checking all the cooks over there.' Russian drops 'they' and lets the verb carry it: 'proveryayut', 'they check'. 'Vsekh povarov' means 'all the cooks' as the ones being checked.
- 'Tell me honestly, whose pâté is better, mine or the chef's?' 'Chey' means 'whose', and it matches the word after it: 'chey pashtet', but 'chya kniga', 'whose book'.
- 'And you won't be offended?' 'Obidishsya' means 'you'll take offense'. The '-sya' on the end shows the hurt happens to you.
- 'No, tell the truth.' 'Govori', 'speak' or 'tell', is the 'ty' command, and 'pravda' becomes 'pravdu' because it's the thing told. The newspaper 'Pravda' is the same word.
- 'And you won't tell the chef?' 'Shefu', 'to the chef', is 'shef' with '-u' added for the person you tell something to.
- 'No, I won't.' Russian repeats the verb: 'ne skazhu', literally 'I won't say'. It's the natural short answer to 'ty ne skazhesh?'
- 'The chef's is better.' Kostya keeps it to three words. 'U shefa' is literally 'at the chef', so 'u shefa luchshe' means 'at the chef's place it's better'.
- 'Never mind, I'll find his secret ingredient!' 'Nichego' literally means 'nothing', but at the start of a reply it means 'never mind' or 'no matter'.
