## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, why are you smiling so cheerfully?' The ending on the last word is the polite 'you'. Russian's 'cheerfully' ends in 'o', like many words for 'how something is done'.
- 'Look at the neighboring restaurant, across the courtyard.' When you look toward a place, Russian leaves 'courtyard' bare. When something just sits in the courtyard, the word takes an 'e'.
- 'A strict inspection has just arrived over there!' The Russian for 'arrived' ends in 'a' because Russian's 'inspection' is feminine, and 'strict' ends in 'aya' for the same reason. 'Just now' is two small words, literally 'only what'.
- 'Yes, it is the migration service.' 'Service' is also the Russian for 'job', so it fits any official body. Both words have the feminine ending, just as the 'strict inspection' did.
- 'Did you call this inspection yourself?' Senya adds 'yourself' to ask whether the chef did it with his own hands. Russian's 'call' is the one for ringing a bell, so a phone call is a ring.
- 'No, it was Nagiyev who called.' The word order puts the person first and the action last. Barinov is happy to let the owner take the blame, or the credit.
- 'Now their cooks will be running around a lot.' 'Will be running' is built from 'will be' plus the dictionary form of 'run'. The Russian for 'their' never changes its ending, which is a relief.
- 'Now they are in for a workout!' The little 'po' at the front of Russian's 'run' gives it the sense of 'run around for a while'. The first word is 'right now', literally 'this hour'.
- 'Elena Pavlovna will be furious.' The second name is a patronymic, 'daughter of Pavel', which is how you politely address an adult. 'Furious' has the little 'sya' that means the anger happens to her.
- 'It serves them right.' Word for word it is 'so to-them and needed'. It's a fixed phrase, and the Russian for 'needed' is one you've met before.
- 'A small but pleasant revenge.' There is no 'is' in it, which is normal. Russian's 'revenge' is feminine, so 'small' and 'pleasant' both end in 'aya'.
- 'This is a fair lesson for rude neighbors.' The Russian for 'lesson' also means a class at school. The endings on 'rude' and 'neighbors' are both the plural 'for' form, so they go together.
- 'Then let's calmly watch out the window!' The first word is the standard way to say 'let's', here in the plural form. 'Calmly' ends in 'o' like 'cheerfully' at the top, and the window takes the direction form.
