## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'No, I do not really feel like it.' Max shares this point facing Barinov: the "-sya" ending on "khochetsya" ("want, feel like") turns the action inward or makes it happen between people naturally.
- 'I am just not in the mood.' Barinov continues earnestly in their talk, so Vika knows he is not just making small talk.
- 'I am very tired.' Vika uses one vivid touch on cue: "ustala" means "are tired, got tired" (from "ustat"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'I am going to head home.' Max delivers the reaction with ease: spoken Russian trims this down to just 3 words, dropping the extra helper words that English has to spell out.
- 'Viktoriya Sergeyevna, you are the main star of tonight's evening.' Barinov speaks sharply around Max, saying out loud what both of them already know.
- 'This whole party was arranged solely for you.' Vika uses one concrete touch toward Max: "vecherinka" means "party", and it carries the main weight of what Vika is saying.
- 'No, you are not going anywhere tonight.' Max stresses one key piece in earnest: "segodnya" means "today", and it carries the main weight of what Max is saying.
- 'Can I have a word with you for a minute? Yes?' Barinov challenges quietly why things suddenly took this turn between them.
- 'What do you think you are doing?' Vika highlights one loaded piece for Barinov: "tvorish" means "are doing, creating" (from "tvorit"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'What are you talking about?' Max puts the thought toward Vika: notice how Russian needs no word for "am", "is", or "are" in the present — placing the words side by side says it all.
- 'Keep your hands off Vika, got it?' Barinov quizzes frankly how Max expects that to work in practice.
- 'What business is it of yours?' Vika uses one classic expression right here: "delo" means "matter", and it carries the main weight of what Vika is saying.
- 'She is mine.' Max delivers this answer before Barinov: notice how Russian needs no word for "am", "is", or "are" in the present — placing the words side by side says it all.
