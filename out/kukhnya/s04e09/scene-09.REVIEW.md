## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Do whatever you want.' A plural command followed by 'what you want' leaves the method up to the crew.
- 'But the sauté pan must be with me.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Maybe it is simpler to buy a new one?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Senya's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'I need mine.' Three short masculine words make it clear that no substitute pan will do.
- 'Chef, that is impossible.' Notice how Russian puts 'chef' and 'impossible' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'German hid the sauté pan in the safe.' Here Senya anchors the thought on 'hid' and finishes on 'safe', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'So first we have to steal the keys.' The temporal adverb for 'at first' sets up the first step of the heist.
- 'You will not abandon me in a difficult moment?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Chef Barinov's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Fine, I am taking you to the banya.' Using the present tense of 'to lead' expresses a firm promise about an upcoming treat.
- 'How will we pull the keys out of his pocket?' The prefix 'vy-' on the future verb highlights pulling something out from inside.
- 'We can put him to sleep first.' The causative verb is built from the root for 'sleep' and means 'to put to sleep'.
- 'I have sleeping medicine.' This neuter adjective used as a noun literally means 'sleep-inducing stuff'.
- 'Louis, let's skip the details.' Literally 'let's without details', this is a quick everyday way to tell someone 'spare me the details'.
- 'But the thoughts are good, friends.' The plural adjective agrees with the feminine plural noun 'thoughts'.
