## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hello, please come on through.' The formal greeting is followed by the polite plural command inviting the officer inside.
- 'They stole a copper sauté pan.' In everyday Russian, 'stole' flows straight into 'sauté' without extra linking words.
- 'Nothing else disappeared.' Russian uses double negation here: the negative pronoun 'nothing' requires the negative particle before the neuter past-tense verb.
- 'I cannot find the sturgeon.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'It seems to me they stole it along with the pan.' Russian expresses feelings and needs by saying 'to me it is needed' or 'to me it seems', putting the person in the 'to me' form.
- 'You called us in over this nonsense?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Vika's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Excuse me, this is not nonsense.' German opens with the polite plural apology before flatly contradicting the claim.
- 'For a chef, this is the most important thing.' Notice how Russian puts 'cook' and 'the main thing' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'And what if someone stole a pistol from you?' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Well then, shall we have some fun?' The particle for 'let's' combines with the first-person plural future verb to suggest an activity together.
- 'No, we must draw on paper.' Vika pairs 'need to, must' with 'paper; official document' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Have you already started having fun without me?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Vika's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
