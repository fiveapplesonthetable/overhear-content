## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'This is no theft at all.' The emphatic negative determiner at the front intensifies the denial: 'no kind of theft whatsoever'.
- 'I took back my own sauté pan.' Chef Barinov pairs 'retrieved' with 'sauté' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'I bought this pan in France.' Notice how Russian puts 'frying pan, skillet' and 'France' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'Now I am the head chef at the restaurant.' German asserts his authority in the present tense, omitting the verb 'am'.
- 'Everything in the kitchen is mine now.' In everyday Russian, 'kitchen' flows straight into 'now, nowadays' without extra linking words.
- 'I want to get it back.' German pairs 'want' with 'to return' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'The conversation is over.' Notice how Russian puts 'matter' and 'is finished, over' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'In your place, I would not argue.' Here German anchors the thought on 'place' and finishes on 'to argue, to dispute', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'You do not need problems with the police.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'Hello, Max, hi, how are you doing there?' The telephone greeting is distinct from an in-person 'hello' and is used only when answering or making a call.
- 'How is Styopa, is everything good?' Short verbless questions are the most natural way to check in on family over the phone.
- 'Styopa is fine, he fell asleep.' The phrase 'in order' is the everyday Russian equivalent of 'fine' or 'okay'.
- 'Strange, he usually does not sleep right now.' An adverb of frequency sits right before the negated present-tense verb to describe a routine.
- 'Above all, do not worry.' Inserted as a parenthetical word, the neuter adjective for 'main' means 'most importantly' or 'above all'.
