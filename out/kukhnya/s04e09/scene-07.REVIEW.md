## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'This is no theft at all.' The emphatic negative determiner at the front intensifies the denial: 'no kind of theft whatsoever'.
- 'I took back my own sauté pan.' The reflexive possessive pronoun stresses that the pan belongs to the subject of the sentence himself.
- 'I bought this pan in France.' Country names ending in '-iya' take the ending '-ii' in the prepositional case after 'in'.
- 'Now I am the head chef at the restaurant.' German asserts his authority in the present tense, omitting the verb 'am'.
- 'Everything in the kitchen is mine now.' Because the pronoun for 'everything' is neuter singular in Russian, the possessive pronoun 'mine' also takes the neuter form.
- 'I want to get it back.' The conjugated verb 'I want' is followed by the object pronoun and the perfective infinitive 'to return'.
- 'The conversation is over.' A masculine short-form past passive participle creates a firm, final statement meaning 'is finished'.
- 'In your place, I would not argue.' The idiom 'in your place' uses the preposition 'on' with the prepositional case before a conditional verb.
- 'You do not need problems with the police.' Because 'problems' is plural, the short-form predicate for 'needed' takes the plural ending '-y'.
- 'Hello, Max, hi, how are you doing there?' The telephone greeting is distinct from an in-person 'hello' and is used only when answering or making a call.
- 'How is Styopa, is everything good?' Short verbless questions are the most natural way to check in on family over the phone.
- 'Styopa is fine, he fell asleep.' The phrase 'in order' is the everyday Russian equivalent of 'fine' or 'okay'.
- 'Strange, he usually does not sleep right now.' An adverb of frequency sits right before the negated present-tense verb to describe a routine.
- 'Above all, do not worry.' Inserted as a parenthetical word, the neuter adjective for 'main' means 'most importantly' or 'above all'.
