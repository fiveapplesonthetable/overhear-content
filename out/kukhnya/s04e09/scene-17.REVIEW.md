## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Well, did we settle things like men?' The plural reflexive past tense asks if they have now settled their score with each other.
- 'I simply slipped.' Sitting on the ground, Barinov saves face with the reflexive past verb for 'slipped'.
- 'Right, a fight in a public place!' The officer uses official police phrasing with the prepositional case after 'in'.
- 'Oh come on, what fight?' The exclamation 'what are you saying!' dismisses the officer's accusation with warm surprise.
- 'We are colleagues, we work at the restaurant.' Barinov stresses their shared workplace using the first-person plural present tense.
- 'We have no time for the gym.' Negating 'time' requires the genitive singular form 'of time' before 'there is not'.
- 'So we box on our break.' Borrowed sports verbs in '-irovat' drop that suffix and take '-iruem' in the 'we' form.
- 'Yes, of course, we spar quite often.' German backs up the lie using the same '-uem' first-person plural verb pattern.
- 'I am actually a Master of Sport in boxing.' Specialties and subjects in Russian take the preposition 'po' plus the dative case.
- 'And my buddy helps me.' The verb 'to help' always governs the dative case ('to me').
- 'And what happened to the car?' The officer points to the crushed roof using the instrumental case after 'with'.
- 'Nothing serious, I have it insured.' The feminine short-form past participle agrees with the feminine noun 'car'.
- 'You need to hold sparring matches in the ring.' In Russian, boxing happens 'on the ring' rather than 'in the ring'.
- 'All right then, all the best.' Literally '(I wish you) of everything good', this genitive phrase is a polite formal goodbye.
