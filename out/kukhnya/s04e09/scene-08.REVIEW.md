## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'And your sauté pan is fine.' The prepositional phrase meaning 'in order' works equally well for people and objects.
- 'Does it not seem to you that this is overkill?' The noun at the end literally means taking too many cards or going too far — 'overkill'.
- 'He steals a defenseless sauté pan!' The adjective combines the prefix for 'without' and the root for 'defense' to mean 'defenseless'.
- 'And these are security measures.' In Russian, 'security measures' is a noun followed by the genitive singular of 'safety'.
- 'Excuse me, what safe is this?' The polite imperative 'forgive me' introduces a question about an unfamiliar object in the office.
- 'It was left over from the old owners.' The preposition 'from' takes the genitive plural of both the adjective 'old' and the irregular noun 'owners'.
- 'I do not use it.' The reflexive verb for 'to use' always requires its object to be in the instrumental case, here 'by it'.
- 'Well, I will use it, give me the keys.' Adding the prefix 'po-' to the verb gives the future sense of using something for a while.
- 'Now that is another matter.' This common idiom literally means 'here is another matter' and expresses satisfaction with a new arrangement.
- 'Do you think Viktor will try again?' The adverb for 'again' precedes the perfective future verb meaning 'will try'.
- 'You can expect anything at all from him.' The particle after 'of what' creates the indefinite pronoun phrase meaning 'anything whatsoever'.
- 'I will take the spare keys to the safe after all.' In Russian, keys are 'from' a lock or safe rather than 'to' it, using the preposition 'ot' plus the genitive.
- 'You do not use it, do you?' German repeats Vika's own words back to her with the emphatic particle to question why she wants the spare key.
- 'He will not bother with such nonsense.' Like 'to use', the verb for 'to engage in' governs the instrumental case on 'such nonsense'.
