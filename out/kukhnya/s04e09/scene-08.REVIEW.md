## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'And your sauté pan is fine.' Vika pairs 'fine' with 'order' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Does it not seem to you that this is overkill?' The noun at the end literally means taking too many cards or going too far — 'overkill'.
- 'He steals a defenseless sauté pan!' The adjective combines the prefix for 'without' and the root for 'defense' to mean 'defenseless'.
- 'And these are security measures.' In everyday Russian, 'measures' flows straight into 'safety, security' without extra linking words.
- 'Excuse me, what safe is this?' German uses a polite everyday Russian courtesy expression that sets the tone of the conversation right away.
- 'It was left over from the old owners.' Notice how Russian puts 'remained' and 'owner, proprietor, host' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'I do not use it.' Vika centers this short reply on 'I am taking advantage', letting a few direct words carry the whole meaning.
- 'Well, I will use it, give me the keys.' Adding the prefix 'po-' to the verb gives the future sense of using something for a while.
- 'Now that is another matter.' This common idiom literally means 'here is another matter' and expresses satisfaction with a new arrangement.
- 'Do you think Viktor will try again?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Vika's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'You can expect anything at all from him.' The particle after 'of what' creates the indefinite pronoun phrase meaning 'anything whatsoever'.
- 'I will take the spare keys to the safe after all.' In everyday Russian, 'will take' flows straight into 'little key' without extra linking words.
- 'You do not use it, do you?' German repeats Vika's own words back to her with the emphatic particle to question why she wants the spare key.
- 'He will not bother with such nonsense.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
