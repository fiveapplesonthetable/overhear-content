## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'All right, guys, let's do it differently.' The adverb 'differently/otherwise' proposes a less painful plan.
- 'Just tie me up tightly.' Here Senya anchors the thought on 'simply, just' and finishes on 'to tie up, to bind', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'What if I do not turn into one after all?' At the start of a question, the word for 'suddenly' means 'what if?'.
- 'Or scientists will invent a vaccine soon.' Senya pairs 'scientist, scholar' with 'to think up, to come up with' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'And you will always have time to shoot me.' Notice how Russian puts 'to time' and 'to make it, to be in time' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'Hold him tighter, guys!' Here Fedya anchors the thought on 'are holding' and finishes on 'guys, team', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'The zombies are already breaking down the doors!' The plural present-tense verb describes the attackers breaking through.
- 'Everyone get into the van quickly!' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
- 'I will drive you to the safe zone.' The prefixed transport verb means 'I will drive/deliver you there'.
- 'Does everyone have eight hundred rubles each?' Even during the end of the world, businessman Timur uses the distributive preposition 'po' to charge a fare per person.
- 'Timur, are you bargaining even now?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Fedya's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Gasoline is very expensive right now, brother.' Timur justifies his fare with a simple present-tense statement.
