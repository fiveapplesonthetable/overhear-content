## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Good afternoon, gentlemen and ladies.' The villain opens with overly polite formal plurals to contrast with the stick-up.
- 'I will allow myself to correct you.' Notice how Russian puts 'will allow' and 'to straighten, fix, adjust' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'We are not terrorists, we are businessmen.' Two parallel verbless clauses contrast the two plural nouns directly.
- 'And now I ask everyone into the dining room.' In everyday Russian, 'right now' flows straight into 'hall, large room; courtroom' without extra linking words.
- 'Have you heard about Stockholm syndrome?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Kostya's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Victims feel sympathy toward their captors.' The word for 'sympathy' is a classic false friend: in Russian it means 'liking' or 'attraction', not pity.
- 'Excuse me, please, may I ask a question?' Tied to a chair, Vika still addresses the kidnappers with impeccable manners.
- 'Could you tie the bartender to me instead?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Vika's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'According to the list, one cook is missing.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'His name is Arseny Chuganin.' In his own daydream, Senya makes sure the villain announces his full name dramatically.
- 'Where is this dangerous cook?' Here Nagiyev anchors the thought on 'where' and finishes on 'cook', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'He is in the kitchen today.' Locations like 'kitchen' use the preposition 'on' rather than 'in' in Russian.
