## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktor Petrovich, are you not sleeping?' Russian forms this yes-or-no question without a question word, relying on rising pitch on 'to sleep'.
- 'I heard everything, Lyova.' Chef Barinov delivers a punchy one-word reaction built around the Russian word meaning 'heard'.
- 'Did you hear my words?' Lyova turns a statement into a question purely through rising intonation on the word meaning 'did you hear?', followed by 'words'.
- 'I almost had a stroke.' The verb for 'to be enough' is in the masculine past tense, marked by the '-l' ending.
- 'Your speech was very fiery.' Chef Viktor uses the informal singular 'you' here with 'fiery', as friends and close colleagues do.
- 'I spoke from a pure heart.' The verb for 'said' is in the masculine past tense, marked by the '-l' ending.
- 'This is already the second heart attack today.' Russian omits the present-tense verb 'to be' here, linking 'already' directly with 'second', combining 'already' with 'second' and 'heart attack'.
- 'So, you are not angry at me?' Lyova uses the imperative command form of the verb meaning 'to be angry'. Then Chef Viktor responds.
- 'No, I am not angry at you.' The reflexive ending on the verb for 'to be angry' turns the action back onto the subject.
- 'Will you stay to live with us?' Lyova uses the classic Russian possession phrasing — literally 'at us' rather than a verb meaning 'to have', combining 'to remain' with 'to live'.
- 'Yes, I will stay here.' Notice the reflexive ending on the verb for 'stay', which directs the action back onto the subject.
- 'I am very glad to hear this.' Lyova expresses this naturally by combining the word for 'very much' with 'glad' and 'to hear'.
- 'And now let me sleep.' Chef Barinov leads with the direct command form meaning 'give', pairing it with 'now' and 'get some sleep'.
