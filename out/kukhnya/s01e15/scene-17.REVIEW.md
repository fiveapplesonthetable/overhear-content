## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hi, Max.' Russian leaves out 'am', 'is', or 'are' in the present tense, linking 'hi' straight to 'Max'.
- 'Hi.' A crisp, everyday conversational reply meaning 'hi' that you will hear constantly in Russian dialogue.
- 'I want to move to your place.' Max uses the informal singular 'you' here with 'for you', as friends and close colleagues do.
- 'Do you have nowhere to live?' Vika turns a statement into a question purely through rising intonation on the word meaning 'nowhere', followed by 'to live'.
- 'No, I have a million options.' Max uses the classic Russian possession phrasing — literally 'at me' rather than a verb meaning 'to have', combining 'a million' with 'options'.
- 'Kostya is calling me back.' — Notice the proper name meaning 'Kostya' paired with the verb for 'calls'.
- 'Leva is also inviting me to live.' — Notice the proper name meaning 'Lyova' paired with the adverb for 'too'.
- 'But I am ready to live with you.' Max uses the informal singular 'you' here with 'you', as friends and close colleagues do.
- 'What do you want to say?' The question opens with the interrogative word for 'what' and builds toward 'tell'.
- 'I want to live with you.' Max chooses the imperfective verb meaning 'want' for the ongoing action, followed by 'to live'.
- 'I do not want to be without you.' Max chooses the imperfective verb meaning 'want' for the ongoing action, followed by 'be'.
- 'That is very pleasant to hear.' Vika expresses this naturally by combining the word for 'very much' with 'pleasant' and 'to hear'.
- 'Really?' Max asks this with a single word meaning 'honestly', using rising intonation to turn it into a quick question.
- 'Yes, I believe you.' Vika delivers a punchy one-word reaction built around the Russian word meaning 'believe'.
