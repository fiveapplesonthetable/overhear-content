## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'One hundred sixty over ninety.' 'Sto' is a hundred, 'shestdesyat' is 'six tens', and 'devyanosto' is the odd one, ninety. 'Na' here means 'over', as in a fraction.
- 'Viktor Petrovich, this is not a joke.' Russians say 'these are not jokes' in the plural. A single joke would be 'shutka'.
- 'Such pressure is very dangerous.' The dash stands for 'is'. 'Davlenie' comes from 'davit', to press, so blood pressure is a pressing.
- 'Yes, everything will be fine.' Barinov waves it off with 'will be well'. Russian uses the adverb for 'well' where English says 'fine', and there is no subject.
- 'Any stress and it will be bad.' 'Lyuboy' means 'any'. The little 'i' means 'and then', so the sentence says 'any stress, and then it will be bad'.
- 'Hi, Mom.' Lyova uses the full word for mum, not the short form a child would call out. It sounds a little more grown-up.
- 'How was your workday?' Days 'pass', 'proshyol', in Russian, and 'proshyol' is a man's form because 'den' is masculine. It's the usual way to ask how someone's day went.
- 'Fine, they fired me.' 'Menya uvolili' is 'me they fired', and nobody is named as the one who did it. 'Normalno' means 'fine'.
- 'Not fired, but demoted.' 'Ne ... a ...' is the pair for 'not X but Y'. 'Ponizili' means 'lowered', and 'nizky' is 'low', so a demotion is a lowering.
- 'Mom, I am not guilty of anything.' 'In nothing not' is the double negative. 'Guilty' has no ending, because Lyova is a man.
- 'You must know how to answer for your actions.' 'Umet'' is 'know how to', and 'otvechat za' is 'answer for', which is how Russians say 'be responsible'. 'Postupki' are deeds.
- 'Thank you for the support, Mom.' 'Thanks to you' makes the thanks personal. The supported thing follows 'for'.
