## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vika, why did you ban relationships at work?' 'Zapretila' ends in 'a' because Vika did the banning, and it grows out of 'zapret', ban. Russian says 'on work' where English says 'at work'.
- 'Because work requires order and discipline.' After 'requires', the things required change their endings, so 'poryadok' turns into 'poryadka' and 'distsiplina' into 'distsipliny'. 'Rabota' is work, and you can hear it in 'rabotay' below.
- 'Did you make this strange decision because of Sasha?' Russian says 'accept a decision' where English says 'make one'. 'Iz-za Sashi' is 'because of Sasha', and Sasha picks up an 'i' ending.
- 'Stop asking these foolish personal questions.' Russian 'sets' a question rather than asking it: zadavat vopros, from the same root as zadacha, 'task'. 'Perestan' is 'stop'.
- 'I just want to understand your real feelings.' 'Chuvstva', feelings, is a close cousin of 'chuvstvovat', to feel. 'Nastoyashchie', real, is the word Vika put in front of 'idiot' earlier in the episode.
- 'I have no feelings towards you!' 'U menya net' is literally 'at me there is not', and that's how Russian says 'I don't have'. After net, the missing thing takes an 'of' shape, so chuvstva loses its ending: chuvstv. 'Nikakikh' means 'none at all'.
- 'But you will forget this rule yourself!' 'Sama' is 'yourself', and it points the finger at her. The little 'zhe' adds 'after all'.
- 'I always strictly observe all my rules.' 'Strogo' means 'strictly', and 'soblyudayu' is 'I keep', as in keeping a rule. 'Svoi' means 'my own', which Russians use whenever the owner is the one doing the action.
- 'If you fall in love, will you fire yourself?' 'Yesli' means 'if'. 'Uvolish' is 'you will fire', and 'sebya' is 'yourself'. 'Samu sebya' is 'your very self': sama in its object form, sitting in front of sebya for emphasis.
- 'Max, go to the kitchen and work quietly.' Two orders in a row, 'idi' and 'rabotay', both in the friendly form. 'Molcha' means 'silently', so Vika is telling him to keep his mouth shut.
- 'I will go, but you will still remember me.' 'No' looks like the English word, but in Russian it means 'but'. 'Yeshchyo' means 'still' or 'yet', so he says she'll remember him yet. 'Vspomnish' is 'you will remember'.
- 'Go already, the cooks have long been waiting for your help.' 'Uzhe' after an order means 'go on, then', with the impatience of 'already'. Russians say 'have been waiting for a long time' with the plain present, 'zhdut'.
