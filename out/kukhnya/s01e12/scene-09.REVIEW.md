## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vika, what is happening in my restaurant?' This is the grown-up, slightly official way to ask 'what's going on'. The 'in my restaurant' part adds an 'e' to the end of 'restaurant', which is what 'in' does.
- 'Actually, this important question is for you!' 'Voobshche-to' means 'in fact', and the little 'to' makes it sound pointed. 'K tebe' is 'to you', so she hands the question back to him.
- 'Why did you remove the kitchen wall?' He uses the polite 'you' form, 'vy', even though he's cross, which shows how formal the argument is. 'Ubrali' is 'removed', and 'stenu' is the wall as the thing removed.
- 'Guests want to see the work of the best cooks.' 'Khotyat' is 'they want' and goes with 'videt', 'to see', as in English. 'Luchshikh' is 'of the best', and 'povarov' is 'of cooks'.
- 'This is not a kitchen, but a foolish circus!' A tiny 'a' carries the 'but' in 'not this, but that'. 'Foolish' is made from 'fool', so it's fool-ish, nearly letter for letter.
- 'All our guests love this beautiful hall.' 'Vse' is 'all', 'nashi' is 'our', and 'lyubyat' is 'they love'. 'Krasivy' means 'beautiful', the same word as 'handsome' for people.
- 'They look at me like at a fish!' 'Kak na rybu' is literally 'like at a fish', and Russian keeps the 'at' in front of the fish. 'Rybu' is how 'ryba' looks when it's the thing being looked at.
- 'The guests are still in shock from shouting.' 'Do sikh por' is literally 'until these times', which is how Russians say 'still'. 'Ot krika' is 'from the shout', and Vika is pointing at him.
- 'I am a great chef, not a fish!' 'Velikiy' means 'great' in the sense of 'grand', not just 'big'. 'A ne' is 'and not'.
- 'Then behave calmly in front of people.' 'Vedi sebya' is literally 'lead yourself', and it means 'behave'. 'Pered' is 'in front of', and 'lyudmi' is how 'people' changes after it.
- 'I will not tolerate this new circus!' 'Ne poterplyu' is the future, 'I will not put up with it'. It's the standard way to say 'I won't stand for it' in Russian.
- 'Get used to it, this is a modern and beautiful restaurant.' You'd say this order to a friend, and it's the same as 'get used to it'. 'Restaurant' is masculine in Russian, so the two describing words end in the bare masculine ending.
