## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Kostya, what happened here?' Max raises the pitch on 'happened' to turn the phrase into a direct spoken question.
- 'Did you have a bad bartender show?' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'I will find out who was juggling here at night.' The grammar packs 'will find out' and 'juggled' into a tight, idiomatic kitchen exchange.
- 'Ainura, could you clean up faster?' Notice how Russian expresses 'Ainura' and 'faster' with crisp inflection rather than helper words. Ainura answers right away.
- 'Listen, Kostya, do not bother me.' Here the Russian phrasing highlights 'listen' before landing on 'to me'.
- 'Let me breathe in the expensive cognac a bit.' Ainura brings out the contrast between 'give' and 'to me' in a few short words.
- 'They smashed three bottles last night.' The plural past ending '-li' on the verb for 'smashed' covers a group or the polite 'you'. Max answers right away.
- 'Maybe only two bottles?' Max brings out the contrast between 'maybe' and 'be' in a few short words.
- 'No, not two, three.' The meaning hinges on 'no' working together with 'three' in spoken Russian.
- 'They forgot to lock up, and someone broke in at night.' Spoken Russian keeps this brisk, leaning on 'forgot' and finishing on 'snuck in'. Max answers right away.
- 'Who could have done that?' Max anchors the line on 'who', while 'to do' completes the thought.
- 'Well of course, not me.' Notice how Russian expresses 'come on' and 'but' with crisp inflection rather than helper words.
- 'And what are you doing here?' Ainura raises the pitch on 'are doing' to turn the phrase into a direct spoken question.
