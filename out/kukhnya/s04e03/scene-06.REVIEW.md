## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'How is the new head chef?' Here Senya anchors the thought on 'how / as' and finishes on 'chef', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'He is sitting in the office sorting through papers.' In everyday Russian, 'is sitting' flows straight into 'papers' without extra linking words.
- 'He is not touching anything.' Lyova centers this short reply on 'to touch', letting a few direct words carry the whole meaning.
- 'Colleagues! A moment of your attention.' Notice how Russian puts 'colleagues' and 'attention' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'You and I have known each other for one day.' Here German anchors the thought on 'acquainted' and finishes on 'day', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'And it would be worthwhile for us to get to know each other closer.' The past-tense verb ending marks the grammatical gender and number of the subject directly.
- 'We could go to a sauna together.' Senya pairs 'may, possible, allowed' with 'to go' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'That is good too. But I have a better idea.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Let's hold a skills evaluation today.' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
- 'Believe me, nothing brings people closer like that.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'Are there any objections? Well, that is wonderful.' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and German's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'I jinxed it.' Spoken Russian keeps this brief and expressive, built right around 'to jinxed' without extra helper words.
