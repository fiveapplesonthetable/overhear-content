## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Wait, guy! Where are you carrying the expensive cognac?' "Stoy" is literally "stand", so "stop!". The question word is "kuda", where to, because the bottle is moving.
- 'What cognac? I took nothing.' "Ya nichego ne bral" is "I nothing not took", another double negative. "Bral" is the same plain "took" Nastya used to deny taking the knife.
- 'The bottle from the bar! It costs twenty thousand.' "Stoit" means "costs" again. In "dvadtsat' tysyach", twenty thousand, "tysyacha" shrinks to "tysyach" after the number.
- 'Wow, twenty thousand rubles? What a price!' "Ogo" is "wow". "Nichego sebe", "nothing to oneself", means "well, I never!". "Tsena" is the price.
- 'You seriously got into trouble, bartender.' "Popal v bedu" is "fell into trouble", like English "landed in". "Beda", trouble, becomes "bedu" after "v".
- 'No, you are the one in trouble!' "Eto ty" is "it's you", which puts the stress on "you". Kostya turns the man's own sentence back on him.
- 'Return this expensive bottle to me immediately.' "Verni", give back, is the friendly command, and "mne" is "to me". "Etu doroguyu butylku" all end in -u as the thing returned.
- 'Listen here, guy, you are asking for it now.' "Slushay syuda" is literally "listen here". "Naryvayesh'sya" means "you're asking for it", a threat.
- 'A big wardrobe always falls very loudly.' "Bol'shoy shkaf gromko padayet" is a real Russian saying, like "the bigger they are, the harder they fall". "Shkaf" is a wardrobe and "padayet" is "falls".
- 'Have you heard such a clever folk rule?' "Umnyy", clever, comes from "um", mind. "Narodnyy", folk, comes from "narod", people, and "slyshal" is the past for a man.
- 'I seriously practiced boxing for many years.' After "mnogo", "god", year, turns into "let", an odd form. "Zanimalsya boksom" is "busied myself with boxing", so the sport takes -om.
- 'Hand over the cognac, otherwise I will hit you!' "Otday" is "give it over", and "inache" is "otherwise". "Udaryu", I'll hit, is one word.
- 'Okay, okay, no need to fight right away!' "Ladno, ladno" is "OK, OK". "Ne nado" is "no need", and "drat'sya", to fight, ends in "-sya".
