## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Kostya, why did you arrive so early?' "Prishyol", 'came', is for arriving on foot, with pri- meaning arrival. "Tak rano" is 'so early'.
- 'It is already eight o'clock in the morning, Max.' Russian counts the clock in hours: vosem' chasov, 'eight of hours'. "Utra", 'of the morning', is how you say a.m.
- 'I thought that the restaurant was still closed.' "Dumal" ends in -l, the past tense for a man; a woman would say dumala. "Yeshchyo" means 'still' here.
- 'Why haven't you put on your uniform yet?' "Yeshchyo ne", 'still not', is how Russian says 'not yet'. "Nadet'" is the verb for putting on clothes, and forma becomes formu as the thing being put on.
- 'My locker key is lying here.' A key in Russian 'lies', lezhit, when it is flat on a surface. And it's a key ot shkafa, 'from the locker', where English says 'to' or 'of'.
- 'Then go faster to the staff locker room.' "Idi" is the command 'go', and bystreye, 'faster', is how you tell someone to hurry. "Razdevalka", the changing room, comes from razdevat'sya, to get undressed.
- 'Do not boss me around, I will manage everything in time.' "Komandovat'", to command, takes mnoy, the 'by me' form of 'me'. "Uspeyu" is the future of uspet', to make it in time, and you'll hear it whenever someone is running late.
- 'What were you doing here at night at all?' "Vobshche", 'at all', gives the question a suspicious edge. "Noch'yu", 'at night', is the word for night with an ending that turns it into a time.
- 'Honestly speaking, I live right here.' "Chestno govorya" is a fixed phrase, like 'to be honest'. "Pryamo zdes'" is 'straight here', meaning right here.
- 'You spend the night in Chef Barinov's restaurant?!' "Nochevat'", to spend the night, is built on noch', night. "Shefa Barinova", 'of Chef Barinov', puts an -a on both words to show whose restaurant it is.
- 'Yes, I sleep on the sofa in the dining room.' "Splyu", 'I sleep', has an extra l that only the I form gets; 'you sleep' is spish. "Divan" and zal both take -e after na and v: na divane, v zale.
- 'If the chef finds out, he will kill you!' Russian puts uznayet, 'will find out', in the future, where English says 'finds out'. "Ub'yot", 'will kill', is the future too.
- 'Please do not tell anyone my secret.' "Nikomu" means 'to nobody', and the ne in front of rasskazyvay stays: 'don't tell to nobody'. Ne rasskazyvay is the usual way to ask someone to keep quiet.
