## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I have been living in Moscow for two weeks already.' Russian has no 'have been living': it just says 'I live in Moscow already two weeks', zhivu, plain present. Notice dve, the form of 'two' used with feminine words like nedelya, a week.
- 'Have you already gotten used to life in Moscow?' "Privyk", 'got used', has no l at the end, which is unusual for a man's past. It takes k, 'towards', so you get used 'to' life: k zhizni.
- 'I came here from Voronezh for a dream.' "Priekhal" means he came by transport, train or bus, not on foot. And za mechtoy, 'after a dream', is the same pattern as going to the shop za khlebom, for bread.
- 'And now you work in the best restaurant.' After v, 'in', the restaurant becomes restorane and 'best' becomes luchshem. Both endings mark the place where something happens.
- 'Claude Monet restaurant is the most famous in the city.' Put samyy, 'most', in front of a describing word and you have a superlative: samyy izvestnyy, the most famous. The dash in writing stands in for 'is', which Russian leaves out.
- 'Here there are very strict rules and an expensive menu.' "Menyu" is borrowed from French and never changes its ending. It counts as neuter, so 'expensive' takes the neuter -oe: dorogoe menyu.
- 'But for me this is a wonderful experience.' "Dlya menya", 'for me', is worth keeping whole. "Opyt" is the everyday word for experience, as in work experience on a CV.
- 'And where did you find yourself housing?' "Sebe", 'for yourself', is often slipped in like this when you get something for your own use. "Zhilyo", housing, is related to zhit', to live.
- 'I live right in the center of the city.' "Samyy" shows up again, this time as samom, and here it means 'the very'. V samom tsentre is literally 'in the very center'.
- 'Apartments in the center cost huge money.' Here stoyat means 'they cost', not 'they stand'. Money, dengi, only exists in the plural, and here it is in the 'of' form, deneg.
- 'And I do not pay a single kopeck!' "Ni odnoy kopeyki" is 'not even one kopeck', and Russian keeps the ne of 'I don't pay' as well. Two negatives in one sentence are normal here.
- 'That sounds very strange and suspicious.' "Zvuchit", 'sounds', is built on zvuk, a sound. Kostya isn't buying it.
- 'I have one secret plan.' U menya est', 'at me there is', is the basic Russian 'I have'. "Sekretnyy" is the English 'secret' with a Russian adjective ending.
