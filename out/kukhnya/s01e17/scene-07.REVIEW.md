## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Listen, this is a nice bar.' "Slushay", listen, is the casual way friends open a thought. The little "a" just eases into it.
- 'I could work here.' "By" turns "porabotal", worked, into "would work". "Ya by" is how Russian starts a daydream.
- 'Here? What about our restaurant?' "A kak zhe" is literally "and how then", the set way to say "what about...?".
- 'I mean, if we didn't have our restaurant.' "V smysle", literally "in the sense", is how Russians say "I mean". "Esli by ne bylo" is "if there weren't".
- 'Then I'd work here.' "Tut" and "zdes" both mean "here", and Kostya has now used one of each. "By" keeps it a wish, not a plan.
- 'Fine, I believe you.' "Veryu" means "I believe", from "verit". "Ladno", fine, sounds a bit like she's humouring him.
- 'Well, here comes the fun.' Max uses the same arrival phrase as Nastya did: "a vot i", and here is. "Vesele", fun, comes from "vesyolyy", cheerful.
- 'Grab your glasses.' "Razbirayte" is literally "take apart": everyone helps themselves to one. "Bokaly" is the plural of "bokal", a wine glass.
- 'Thanks, Max.' Plain "spasibo" and a name: in Russian the name usually goes at the end like this.
- 'Well?' "Nu chto?" is literally "well, what?". Here it's a nudge: somebody say the toast.
- 'Let's drink to meeting up.' "Za" is the toast word, and "vstrecha", meeting, turns into "vstrechu" after it. "Davayte" makes it "let's" for a group.
- 'To meeting up!' Kostya repeats the toast, as everyone round a Russian table does.
