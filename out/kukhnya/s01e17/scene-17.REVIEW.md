## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hold it right there.' "Stoyat" is the dictionary form of "to stand", barked as an order, like a guard's "freeze!".
- 'What's the matter?' "V chyom delo?" is literally "in what is the matter?", a fixed way to ask what's wrong.
- 'You stole our dish.' "Ukrali" is "stole" for "vy". "Blyudo", dish, is a word you'll see on every menu.
- 'I knew it.' "Ya tak i znal", literally "I so and knew", is the set phrase for "I knew it!".
- 'You can't do anything yourselves.' "Sami", yourselves, comes last for weight, and "nichego ne" doubles the no.
- 'We've had this dish since the day we opened.' "So dnya otkrytiya" is "since the day of opening": "s" grows to "so" before "dnya".
- 'Give me the menu, please.' "Dayte" is the polite "give", and "mne" means "to me".
- 'Look at this date.' "Posmotrite na" is "look at", and Russian needs that "na". "Eta data" becomes "etu datu" after it.
- 'Well, well.' "Nu" said twice and slowly is a doubtful "we'll see about that".
- 'Can you see clearly?' "Vy khorosho vidite?" is literally "do you see well?", and from Elena it's a jab.
- 'Yes, fine.' "Normalno", literally "normally", is the everyday answer for "fine" or "okay".
- 'Sorry.' "Izvinite" is the polite "excuse me", for "vy". It's a word you rarely hear from Barinov.
