## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Here are my tasty chops for the chef.' "Kotlety", from "kotleta", usually means a patty in Russian, but here it's lamb chops. "Vot" points: here are.
- 'The chef already picked my meat dish.' "Myasnoe", meat, describes the dish and comes from "myaso". "Vybral" is "chose".
- 'He didn't even taste my food!' "Dazhe ne" is "not even". "Eda", food, becomes "edu" as the thing being tasted.
- 'Well, I got here first.' Russian "comes as first": "pervym", with an "-ym" ending you'll hear after "came" and "was".
- 'I cooked these chops all morning.' "Vsyo utro", all morning, needs no "for" in front of it.
- 'Don't get so upset, friend.' "Ne nado", no need, is a soft way to say "don't". "Rasstraivatsya" means to get upset.
- 'But these are lamb chops with asparagus!' "Baranyi", lamb, comes from "baran", a ram. "S" grows to "so" before the "sp" of "sparzhey".
- 'Next time you'll definitely win.' "V sleduyushchiy raz" is literally "in the next time". "Tochno" here means "for sure".
- 'Want to try my new chops?' "Khochesh poprobovat?" is a plain statement asked with a rising voice; Russian needs nothing more to make a question.
- 'Go on, I'll gladly try them.' "S udovolstviem", literally "with pleasure", is the standard way to say "gladly".
- 'So how does it taste, honestly?' "Kak tebe", how is it to you, is the usual way to ask for an opinion. "Chestno" means honestly.
- 'Listen, it's really very tasty!' "Deystvitelno" means "really" or "truly", and it's a little stronger than "ochen", very.
- 'Then we'll eat it ourselves for lunch.' "Sami" means "ourselves". "Na obed", for lunch, uses "na", onto, as with "na pitstsu".
