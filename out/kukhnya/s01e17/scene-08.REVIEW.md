## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Why didn't you tell me they'd be here?' "Tut budut" is literally "will be here": Russian keeps the future where English shifts to "would".
- 'Vik, they're my friends.' "Vik" is Vika with the last sound dropped, which Russians often do when calling someone. "Eto" covers "they are" here too.
- 'We spend a lot of time together. Get used to it.' "Provodit vremya" is "to spend time", and "vremya" turns into "vremeni" after "mnogo". "Privykay" means "get used to it".
- 'Maybe I should bring my mom here, then?' "A mozhet" opens a sarcastic suggestion. "Mama" becomes "mamu" as the one being brought, and "svoyu" means "my own".
- 'I want to spend a lot of time with them, too.' "S nimi" means "with them". She throws Max's own words back at him: "mnogo vremeni provodit".
- 'It's fine, you'll get along.' "Podruzhites" is built on "drug", friend: you'll become friends with each other.
- 'Kostya, tell the story about the strawberry mojito.' "Rasskazhi" is "tell" to one friend. "Klubnichnyy", strawberry, comes from "klubnika".
- 'And the one about that bald guy.' "Pro" is the chatty word for "about". "Lysyy" means bald, and "togo lysogo" is "that bald one".
- 'That never happened.' "Takogo ne bylo nikogda" is literally "of such there wasn't never". The double "no" is ordinary Russian.
- 'For some reason, I'm not surprised.' "Pochemu-to" is "why" with "-to" added: "for some reason". "Udivlena" ends in "-a" because a woman is speaking.
- 'Anyway, you stay a while, and we'll probably go.' "Posidite" is literally "sit a bit", the usual way to tell people to stay on. "A my" means "and as for us".
- 'It's time for us to go home.' "Nam pora" is literally "to us, it's time". "Domoy" means "homewards", unlike "doma", at home.
- 'Since at work it's forbidden for us.' "A to" here means "because otherwise". "Zapreshcheno", forbidden, is Vika's own rule from the morning.
- 'Guys, what's got into you?' "Rebyata" is "guys" for any group of friends. "Vy chego?" is a casual "what's up with you?".
- 'No, you stay here and have fun, and I'll go.' "Ostavaytes" and "veselites" are both commands for the group, and both end in "-tes".
- 'And why are you telling us what to do?' "Ukazyvat" is to give orders, and it comes from "ukazat", to point. Nastya still says "vy" to her boss.
- 'You're not at work now. You stay, and we're leaving.' "Ne na rabote" means "not at work". "Ukhodim" is "we're leaving", in the present, as in English.
- 'Kostya, hey, where are you going?' "Ty kuda?" is literally "you where to?", with no word for "go" at all. "Nu" makes it pleading.
