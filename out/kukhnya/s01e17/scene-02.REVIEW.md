## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Well, there I agree with you.' "Soglasna" ends in "-a" because Elena is a woman; Barinov would say "soglasen". "S vami" is "with you", the polite form she keeps for her rival.
- 'Yes, five days. Just right for your restaurant.' "Kak raz" means "exactly right" or "just the thing", and here it is pure sarcasm: old fish suits her restaurant.
- 'You think so? Then I'll cook it for you on Tuesday.' "Vo vtornik" is "on Tuesday": days of the week take "v", which turns into "vo" before "vt". "Eyo" here is "her", meaning the fish.
- 'See you then.' "Do vstrechi" is literally "until the meeting". It is the goodbye you use when the next meeting is already planned.
- 'See me when?' Barinov throws her own word back: "kakoy eshchyo vstrechi?", what meeting is that? "Kakoy eshchyo" before a noun is an indignant "what on earth".
- 'You're paying us a return visit, aren't you?' "Otvetny vizit" is a return visit, from "otvet", answer. "Vy zhe" with "zhe" means "surely you are", a reminder rather than a question.
- 'That's what I was told, anyway.' "Mne tak skazali" is "to me so they said", with no "they" at all. The plural ending hides who did the telling.
- 'So, see you at Arcobaleno.' "Tak chto" means "so" when it draws a conclusion. Foreign names ending in "-o", like Arcobaleno, never change their ending in Russian.
- 'Damn, as if I had nothing better to do.' "Delat mne bolshe nechego" is literally "to do, to me, nothing more". Russians use it to grumble about a chore they did not ask for.
- 'Actually, chef, it's not bad at all there.' "Nichego" usually means "nothing", but "ochen dazhe nichego" means "really rather good". Context decides which one you hear.
- 'And how would you know? What, have you been to Arcobaleno?' "Otkuda" is "from where"; "otkuda ty znaesh" asks where your knowledge comes from. "Byl" is the past of "to be", and its bare ending means a man is speaking.
- 'Me? No, of course not.' "Konechno", of course, is said "koneshno": the "ch" sounds like "sh" in this word. Senya says it a little too fast.
- 'A friend just told me about it.' "Rasskazyval" is the long, unfinished kind of past: the friend talked about it, maybe more than once. "Mne" is "to me".
- 'I even stopped talking to him after that.' "Perestal" means "stopped", and the next verb stays in its dictionary form, "obshchatsya", to talk to each other.
- 'Oh, relax, I was just asking.' "Da ladno" is a shrug, roughly "come on" or "never mind". "Prosto" means "simply", here "just".
