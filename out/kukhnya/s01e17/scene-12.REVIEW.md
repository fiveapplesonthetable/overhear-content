## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Who are you? What is this?' "Ty kto takoy?" is a rude "who do you think you are?". The owner says "ty", as a boss may to staff.
- 'This is the chef's new seasonal dish.' "Ot shef-povara", from the head chef, is the usual menu phrase for a chef's special.
- 'Veal rolls with thin slices of ham.' "Ruletiki" are little "rulety", rolls: the "-ik" ending makes things small and cute. "Iz telyatiny" means "made from veal".
- 'I came up with it, and the chef approved it.' "Eto ya pridumal" puts "ya" before the action to stress "it was me".
- 'A guy came up with a dish instead of the chef.' "Chelovek", person, is how he refers to Senya, a bit like "this guy". "Vmesto" means "instead of".
- 'Not bad, kid. Right?' "Malysh", little one, is a teasing way to address a young man. "Neplokho", not bad, is real praise in Russian.
- 'Really not bad. Thanks.' Senya repeats the praise back, "ochen neplokho", and takes it before he makes his request.
- 'We also need a slicer in the kitchen for this dish.' "Nuzhen", needed, puts the slicer in charge of the sentence. "Slayser" is borrowed straight from English.
- 'What's that? I hope it's legal?' "Zakonnoe", legal, comes from "zakon", law. The new word sounds suspicious to him.
- 'It's a gadget for slicing ham really thin.' "Shtuka" is a casual "thing" or "gadget", handy when you don't know a word. "Narezka" means slicing.
- 'And there I was thinking: buy him this slicer.' "Ya-to dumal" with "-to" means "and I thought", with a shrug. "Kupi", buy, is said to himself; "emu", to him, means Senya.
- 'Let the man cut his slices.' "Puskay" means "let", as in "let him". "Lomtikami" is "in slices", from "lomtik", a slice.
- 'After all, somebody here has to come up with dishes!' "V kontse kontsov" is literally "at the end of ends". "Kto-to", somebody, is "kto", who, with "-to".
- 'Off you go, chef impersonator.' "Idi otsyuda" is literally "go from here", a blunt "get out". "Imitator" is the same word as in English.
- 'Goodbye.' "Do svidaniya" is literally "until seeing again", the polite goodbye to someone you call "vy".
