## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, I have information for you.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Spill it quickly, what is it about?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Chef Barinov's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'This is important news regarding your Katya.' Here Senya anchors the thought on 'important' and finishes on 'to katya', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'She did not tell you about this.' Spoken Russian keeps this brief and expressive, built right around 'told' without extra helper words.
- 'And what will I get for this?' Senya centers this short reply on 'will be', letting a few direct words carry the whole meaning.
- 'Senya, do not drag it out, speak up already!' Barinov loses patience and commands him with two imperatives.
- 'All right, only you will not like this.' Senya centers this short reply on 'is liked, pleases', letting a few direct words carry the whole meaning.
- 'Are you making fun of me or what?' In everyday Russian, 'are kidding, mocking' flows straight into 'need to, must' without extra linking words.
- 'She is now under his protection.' Senya pairs 'now, nowadays' with 'protection' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Your Katya got a job working at Monet.' Notice how Russian puts 'to get a job, settle' and 'to work' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'Well, how could she do that?' Barinov expresses outrage with a feminine past modal verb.
- 'Chef, where are you going right now?' Senya panics as Barinov marches toward the door.
- 'Just do not say that I told you!' Senya pairs 'speak' with 'told' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
