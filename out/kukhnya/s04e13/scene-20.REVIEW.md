## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'All right, Viktoriya Sergeyevna, if you ask.' German yields to Vika's request using a conditional clause in the present tense.
- 'For your sake I am ready to bring Katya back.' Notice how Russian puts 'ready' and 'back' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'I can even bring her father back.' Here German anchors the thought on 'can, am able to' and finishes on 'father', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'No, thank you, there is no need to bring her father back.' Vika uses a polite everyday Russian courtesy expression that sets the tone of the conversation right away.
- 'Then I have a request in return.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Will you drink a cup of coffee with me today?' He invites her out with a polite future verb and an affectionate diminutive for cup.
- 'Forgive me, German Mikhailovich, I cannot today.' Vika politely turns down the date using a first-person modal negation.
- 'It is hard to always remain a happy person.' In everyday Russian, 'hard, with difficulty' flows straight into 'person' without extra linking words.
- 'Vika, just know that life goes on!' Kostya pairs 'simply, just' with 'to continue, go on, last' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Everything will definitely be fine for you.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Yes, thank you very much, I know that.' Vika smiles genuinely for the first time all day.
- 'Let's agree not to lie to each other anymore.' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
