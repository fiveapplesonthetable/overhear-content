## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Nastya, did something happen to you?' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'No, Kostya, nothing is wrong with me.' Nastya replies briefly with a negative pronoun in an elliptical possession phrase.
- 'Viktoriya Sergeyevna, how do you feel?' Nastya pairs 'how / as' with 'feel' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Hang in there, please, everything will pass.' Nastya uses a polite everyday Russian courtesy expression that sets the tone of the conversation right away.
- 'Thank you, Nastya, everything is quite normal.' Vika tries to sound composed using an adverb of degree and a predicative adverb.
- 'Do you want me to bring you some water?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Nastya's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Yes, thank you very much.' Vika accepts the glass of water with a warm expression of gratitude.
- 'Denis, can you play something more cheerful?' Notice how Russian puts 'may, possible, allowed' and 'cheerful' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'This music makes one want to cry.' Here Nastya anchors the thought on 'music' and finishes on 'to cry', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'Kostya, bring the report to the office.' In everyday Russian, 'bring' flows straight into 'office' without extra linking words.
- 'All right, I will bring everything right now.' Kostya pairs 'good, well, all right' with 'will bring' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Poor Vika, I feel sorry for her.' Notice how Russian puts 'poor' and 'feel sorry for; it's a pity' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
