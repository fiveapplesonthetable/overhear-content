## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktoriya Sergeyevna, your flowers ended up with me.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Are these really my flowers, German Mikhailovich?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Vika's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'They bloomed in order to lift your mood.' In everyday Russian, 'to order' flows straight into 'mood' without extra linking words.
- 'Thank you, of course, for the kind words.' Vika responds politely while keeping her emotional guard up.
- 'Believe me, I have nothing to do with it at all.' Notice how Russian puts 'believe' and 'completely' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'These lovely flowers chose you themselves.' The plural emphatic pronoun reinforces the personified subject.
- 'I really appreciate your kind attention.' In everyday Russian, 'very, really' flows straight into 'attention' without extra linking words.
- 'But I will not take this bouquet.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'Excuse me, it is already time for me to go.' Vika uses a polite everyday Russian courtesy expression that sets the tone of the conversation right away.
- 'Ainura, take these, please, this is for you now.' German uses a polite everyday Russian courtesy expression that sets the tone of the conversation right away.
- 'Oh, thank you very much, German Mikhailovich!' Ainura blushes and accepts the flowers with delight.
- 'What do you need here, go to work!' Embarrassed that the cooks saw him get rejected, German barks at them to get busy.
