## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Max, maybe you will come work for me?' Chef Barinov offers Max a spot in his new restaurant right away.
- 'Oh no, thank you very much, chef.' Colloquial 'da net' means a gentle 'no'.
- 'I will go to St. Petersburg to a friend.' St. Petersburg is affectionately called 'Piter' by everyone in Russia.
- 'Apparently, it will be better for everyone this way.' Here Max anchors the thought on 'apparently, evidently' and finishes on 'better', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'We went for an X-ray, my leg is fine!' Kostya and Nastya sprint onto the platform just before departure.
- 'Passengers, board the car, the train is departing.' Kostya pairs 'passenger' with 'to departing' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Well goodbye, pal, take care of yourself there!' The idiom 'beregi sebya' ('guard yourself') is the warmest way to say 'take care of yourself'.
- 'In my youth I thought that I would still have everything.' Barinov pulls Max aside for a fatherly word before he boards.
- 'So I did not truly treasure anything in particular.' Putting the short word for 'not' right in front of the action word is all Russian needs to make a sentence negative, with no extra helper words like 'do' or 'did'.
- 'And now I have very little left.' Having lost both his kitchen and his family, Barinov speaks from the heart.
- 'You still have time, do not waste it in vain.' Barinov's advice hits Max deeply.
- 'Thank you for everything, dear chef.' Max hugs his mentor and steps onto the train.
- 'But instead of faith, only emptiness remained inside.' As Vika's phone says 'unavailable', Max reflects on what happens when someone loses faith in you.
