- 'Viktoriya Sergeyevna, may I have a minute with you?' Barinov combines formal address by name and patronymic with a familiar singular pronoun and time phrase.
- 'Vika, be more careful with this German.' In everyday Russian, 'were he' flows straight into 'german' without extra linking words.
- 'He is a psychopath and very aggressive.' Chef Barinov pairs 'aggressive' with 'very, really' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Vitya, why are you talking like that?' Vika pushes back against his accusation using his diminutive name and a present-tense verb.
- 'I have one terrible video.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'In it he brutally beats a person.' In everyday Russian, 'cruelly' flows straight into 'person' without extra linking words.
- 'Vitya, just accept it already.' Vika centers this short reply on 'already', letting a few direct words carry the whole meaning.
- 'German is not to blame for anything.' Spoken Russian keeps this brief and expressive, built right around 'at fault, guilty' without extra helper words.
- 'You lost your place yourself.' An emphatic pronoun highlights that Barinov himself lost his job as head chef.
- 'Viktoriya Sergeyevna, are you having some problems?' German steps over and asks politely using an indefinite plural modifier with a feminine plural noun.
- 'I knew that they would bring this up someday.' An indefinite temporal adverb meaning someday precedes an impersonal third-person plural future verb.
- 'Come along, I will tell you everything.' Notice how Russian puts 'come' and 'will tell' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
