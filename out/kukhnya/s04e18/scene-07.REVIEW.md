## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'We need to introduce a new menu.' Russian expresses feelings and needs by saying 'to me it is needed' or 'to me it seems', putting the person in the 'to me' form.
- 'I have seen it, it works wonderfully.' A masculine past verb of perception pairs with an adverb meaning wonderfully and a present-tense verb.
- 'Dima, let's step aside for a minute.' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
- 'What are you holding there in your hands?' Here Nagiyev anchors the thought on 'hands' and finishes on 'to hold', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'Vitya, are you playing games?' In spoken Russian, the words stay in their normal statement order and Nagiyev's rising voice at the end turns the line into a question.
- 'Speak, I have no secrets from colleagues.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Dima, we have a big problem.' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'You have warmed a snake on your chest.' This classic Russian idiom describes welcoming a treacherous person who will later betray you.
- 'My ex-wife is far away right now.' Nagiyev jokes that the only snake he knows is his ex-wife Kristina.
- 'I am talking about your German.' Chef Barinov pairs 'speak' with 'german' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Take a look at this yourself.' Spoken Russian keeps this brief and expressive, built right around 'look' without extra helper words.
- 'Well go on, show me the video.' Starting with the everyday word for 'let us' is how Russian speakers warmly invite someone to do something together.
