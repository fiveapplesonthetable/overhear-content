## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Max, look at the guests in the dining room.' "Posmotri" is the friendly 'look', the one you'd say to a mate. Russian looks 'on' things, "na gostey", and the hall, "zal", becomes "v zale" when it's where something is.
- 'They are ordering our specialty dishes.' "Firmennye" comes from "firma", a company, so "firmennoe blyudo" is the house's own dish. You'll see that phrase on Russian menus.
- 'People come here every evening.' "Lyudi", people, has nothing to do with the word for one person, chelovek. And "kazhdyy vecher", every evening, needs no 'on' or 'in' in front of it.
- 'Everyone chooses their favorite dish.' "Lyubimoe", favourite, grows from "lyubit", to love, so it's literally 'the loved dish'. "Svoyo" means 'their own' and always points back to whoever is doing the choosing.
- 'They do not think about the food's journey.' "Eda", food, turns into "edy" to mean 'of food', so "o puti edy" is 'about the journey of food' with no word for 'of' anywhere.
- 'Which dish travels a long way?' "Kakoe", which, takes the same -oe ending as "blyudo". "Prokhodit" is 'goes through', from pro-, through, and "khodit", to walk.
- 'Every dish begins its journey at the market.' "Kazhdoe" ends in -oe here because "blyudo" does; a line earlier it was "kazhdyy vecher". And a market is something you're 'on', not 'in': "na rynke".
- 'Then the cooks prepare the order in the kitchen.' One cook is "povar", several are "povara", with a stressed -a instead of the usual -y. The kitchen, like the market, takes "na": "na kukhne".
- 'The whole kitchen works for one plate.' "Radi" means 'for the sake of', and it changes what follows: "odna tarelka", one plate, becomes "radi odnoy tarelki".
- 'The cooks rush very much and get nervous.' "Nervnichat", to be nervous, is built on "nerv", nerve, so it's a word you can almost guess. Both verbs end in the 'they' ending, -at and -ayut.
- 'And guests only see the delicious result.' "Vkusnyy", delicious, comes from "vkus", taste, which you'll hear again in the next line. "Rezultat" is just 'result' said the Russian way.
- 'For them this is a real celebration of taste.' "Dlya nikh" is 'for them'; after a little word like "dlya", 'them' picks up an extra n at the front. "Prazdnik" is also what Russians call a public holiday.
- 'Soon our usual working evening will begin.' "Nachnyotsya", will begin, is a single verb with the future built in. "Rabochiy vecher", a working evening, takes "rabochiy" from "rabota", work.
