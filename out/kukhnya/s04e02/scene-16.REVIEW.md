## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Forgive me for what happened yesterday.' With the conversation and apology in play, Barinov keeps this short reply brisk and direct.
- 'And forgive me for everything else too.' Over the conversation and the apology, Barinov answers Lyova without a second thought.
- 'Oh my goodness, what a speech. Do you like that speech, huh?' Over the moment and the scandal, Nagiyev presses Barinov for a straight answer. Using the informal "you" shows how direct and unfiltered their kitchen relationship is.
- 'He looks kind of strange today. Sober.' Since he looks kind of strange today, Lyova does not waste words on excuses.
- 'I am serious. Take me back, Dima.' Noting that he is serious keeps Barinov's side of the story simple and clear.
- 'That is the problem. For five years I have been Dima to you.' Saying that that is the problem is how Nagiyev sets the tone with Barinov. Speaking with the close, informal "you" strips away any polite distance between them.
- 'When in fact I am Dmitry Vladimirovich.' Because when in fact he is Dmitry Vladimirovich, Nagiyev treats the matter as settled.
- 'After all, I was your boss.' Explaining that after all he was Barinov's boss lays the cards on the table for Barinov. Speaking with the close, informal "you" strips away any polite distance between them.
- 'And what did you think, pal?' By wondering what Barinov thinks pal, Nagiyev presses Barinov for a straight answer. Using the informal "you" shows how direct and unfiltered their kitchen relationship is.
- 'That I am your buddy, right? Your pal.' Nagiyev asks whether that he is Barinov's buddy right, refusing to let the point slide.
- 'That is why you took advantage of me.' Pointing out that that is why Barinov took advantage of him gives Barinov a sobering reality check. Using the informal "you" shows how direct and unfiltered their kitchen relationship is.
- 'Go on, march home.' Going on march home moves the conversation forward without extra drama. Starting with "davay" is the everyday Russian way to say "come on, let us do this".
