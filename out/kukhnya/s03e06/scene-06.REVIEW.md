## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Come on, get up quickly and get dressed.' Vika fires back quietly around Max, without trying to dress up how she really feels.
- 'Do you have a meeting with the boss in the morning?' Touching on the management in this beat, Max puts the weight on "nachalstvom" ("management", from "nachalstvo") as the exchange unfolds.
- 'Please, just disappear from here quickly.' Vika politely sends this courtesy word without hesitation using "pozhaluysta" ("please") — the all-purpose Russian politeness word that covers both "please" and "you're welcome".
- 'I do not want him to see you here in this state.' Vika speaks up frankly hearing Max, steering the talk right where she wants it.
- 'Good morning to you.' Max simply voices this morning greeting without hesitation using "dobroye utro" ("good morning") — literally "kind morning", with the neuter ending matching "utro" ("morning").
- 'Quiet, that is Dima calling right now.' Vika opens up candidly with Max and puts the emphasis on "zvonit" ("is calling") in the middle of the talk.
- 'Hello, dear Dmitry Vladimirovich.' Vika directly voices this formal greeting at this moment using "zdravstvuyte" ("hello") — the polite Russian greeting that literally wishes the other person good health.
- 'Well then, dear Victoria Sergeyevna.' Nagiyev steps in decisively right here and reaches for the Russian word "dorogaya" ("expensive", built from "dorogoy") right at the center of the line.
- 'You and I had an agreement, right?' Opening up openly on the spot, the "-sya" ending on "dogovarivalis" ("had an agreement") marks an action that unfolds mutually or turns back on the speaker.
- 'Hello, Dima.' Vika quietly gives this familiar greeting in front of Nagiyev using "zdravstvuy" ("hello") — the singular form of the classic greeting that literally means "be healthy".
- 'I decided to pick up my colleague and give her a ride to work.' Underscoring his colleague in this beat, Nagiyev brings in "kollegoy" ("colleague", from "kollega") as the key Russian word here.
- 'Someone is ringing the doorbell there.' Talking about the door to Nagiyev, Max centers the remark on "dver" ("door") without any extra fuss.
