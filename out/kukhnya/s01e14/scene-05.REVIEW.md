## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Chef, why did you get so drunk?' 'Napilsya' is built from 'pit', to drink, with 'na' for 'fill up' and 'sya' for 'oneself'. So 'drank himself full'. 'Zachem' is 'what for'.
- 'I am not drunk, but drank a little aperitif.' 'Ne ... a ...' is the Russian 'not ... but ...'. 'Vypil' is 'drank up', and 'nemnogo aperitiva' is 'a little of aperitif', with an 'a' on the end.
- 'You can barely stand on your feet.' 'Ele' means 'barely', and 'stoish' is 'you stand'. 'Na svoikh nogakh' is 'on your own feet'.
- 'The face of the restaurant arrived, I am ready to welcome guests!' 'Litso' is 'face', and the same word is used in 'the face of the company'. 'Pribylo' is a formal 'arrived', used for trains and delegations. 'Gotov' is 'ready'.
- 'In this state you will not go out to the guests!' Vika says 'in such a state' with a long, heavy word for 'state'. The going-out word starts with 'vy', which means 'out', so it is the same as English 'go out'.
- 'And you will never get married.' It's the same word as Vika's, 'vyydesh', but 'vyyti zamuzh' means 'to marry', and only a woman does it. 'Nikogda ne' is 'never not', with the extra 'not' that Russian needs.
- 'Stop grabbing me so roughly.' The words for 'enough' and 'grab hold of' sound almost the same, because they grow from one root. It sounds like a pun, and Russians enjoy that.
- 'I must personally check our restaurant.' The word for 'personally' is a handy one to remember, since you can put it before any action. Barinov uses it to mean 'I, nobody else'.
- 'Do something with this drunken monster!' The 'nibud' on the end of 'something' means 'any'. The word for 'monster' grows from the word for 'miracle', because a monster is something odd and amazing.
- 'I had a very good idea.' Russian says 'at me appeared an idea', 'u menya poyavilas ideya'. Ideas turn up, they're not had.
- 'What other idea can there be now?' 'Kakaya eshchyo' is 'what else'. 'Mozhet byt' is 'can be'.
- 'Let us all together inspect the bar!' 'Poydyomte' is 'let's go', with the 'te' ending, which makes it feel like an invitation to a group. 'Proinspektiruem' is built from the English word 'inspect', plus a Russian beginning.
