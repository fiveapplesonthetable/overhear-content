## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vitya, where are you? The guests are already arriving.' 'Ty gde?' is 'you where?', with no 'are'. 'Sobirayutsya' is 'gather themselves', the usual verb for guests turning up.
- 'Hello, Dima, I am driving in the car.' 'Allo' is only for the phone. 'Dima' is the short form of Dmitry, and Russians travel 'na mashine', literally 'on the car'.
- 'Why are you so very late today?' 'Tak silno' is literally 'so strongly'; 'silno' is the everyday word for 'a lot' or 'badly'. 'Opazdyvaesh' is the 'you' form of 'opazdyvat', to be late.
- 'There were terrible traffic jams on the road.' 'Probka' is a cork, and also a traffic jam, a road corked up with cars. 'Byli', were, is plural to match 'probki'.
- 'Our important client is already waiting for you.' 'Zhdyot tebya', literally 'waits you': Russian 'wait' needs no word for 'for', the person waited for simply changes from 'ty' to 'tebya'.
- 'Tell them to drink wine.' Literally, 'say to them, so that they drank wine'. After 'chtoby', so that, the verb goes into the past form, 'pili', even though nobody has drunk anything yet.
- 'I already poured them the best cognac.' 'Im', to them, is the whole of 'for them' here, no extra word. 'Nalil', poured, is the man's past form; a woman would say 'nalila'.
- 'That is excellent, let them rest for now.' 'Pust' is 'let', and the verb after it is the plain 'they' form, 'pust otdykhayut', 'let they rest'. 'Vot i otlichno' is the stock 'well, that's great then'.
- 'You must be here in five minutes!' 'Dolzhen', must, has the man's form; for a woman it's 'dolzhna'. 'Cherez pyat minut' is literally 'through five minutes', which is how Russians say 'in five minutes'.
- 'Do not yell at me, I am almost there.' You shout 'na' someone, onto them: 'krichat na menya'. 'Podyezzhayu' is 'I'm driving up', with 'pod', under or near, as the prefix for getting close.
- 'I am waiting for you at the main entrance.' 'U', at, takes the 'of' form, so 'glavny vkhod' becomes 'glavnogo vkhoda'. You'll hear 'u vkhoda', at the entrance, in any building.
- 'Coming, coming, I am almost there already.' 'Idu, idu' is the impatient 'coming, coming!' Russians say it even when they're in a car. 'Na meste', literally 'in place', means 'there' or 'arrived'.
