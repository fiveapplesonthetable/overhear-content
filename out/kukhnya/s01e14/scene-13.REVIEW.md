## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vitya, wake up!' 'Vitya' is the short form of Viktor, Barinov's first name. 'Prosypaytes' is the polite order 'wake up!', with 'te' for 'vy' and the 's' that means 'yourself'.
- 'He will not wake up.' 'Prosnyotsya' looks like a present, but it's the kind of verb whose present form means the future: 'he'll wake up'. With 'ne', 'he won't'.
- 'It is useless to wake him.' 'Bespolezno' is 'bez', without, plus 'polza', use. 'Budit' is waking someone else; waking up yourself is the 'sya' verb from the line before.
- 'Maximilian, there you are.' 'Vot vy gde' is literally 'here you where', the stock 'so there you are'. Diana uses the full, grand 'Maksimilian' instead of 'Maks'.
- 'Yes, I am here.' 'Ya zdes', 'I here', with no 'am'. 'Zdes' is 'here' for where something is, not where it's going.
- 'You are hiding from us.' 'Pryachetes', you hide yourselves, ends in 'tes': the 'vy' ending 'te' plus the short 's' of 'sya'. 'Ot nas' is 'from us'.
- 'I am not hiding from anyone.' 'Nikto', nobody, splits around 'ot', from: 'ni ot kogo', literally 'not from whom'. And the 'ne' before the verb stays.
- 'Prepare a little masterpiece for us.' 'Prigotovte' is the polite order, ending in 'te'. 'Nam', to us, is all Russian needs for 'for us' here.
- 'We want classic French cuisine.' 'Kukhnya' is both 'kitchen' and 'cuisine', as in the show's title. It and its two describing words end in 'yu' or 'uyu' as the thing wanted.
- 'A chef needs to rest.' Literally, 'to the chef it's needed to rest'. The person who needs something takes the 'to' form, 'shef-povar' becoming 'shef-povaru'.
- 'We have a production meeting.' 'U nas' is 'at us', so 'we have'. 'Proizvodstvennoe soveshchanie' is office jargon, and 'soveshchanie' shares its root with 'sovet', advice or council.
- 'Maximilian, I am waiting for you.' 'Ya vas zhdu', literally 'I you wait', with no 'for'. 'Vas' is the polite 'you' as the one waited for.
- 'I will come to you now.' There's no 'will' anywhere, yet 'pridu' already means 'I'll come', and 'pri-' is the part that says 'arrive'. 'K vam' is 'to you', using the polite or plural 'you'.
