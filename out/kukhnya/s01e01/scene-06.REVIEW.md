## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Why is the kitchen so quiet?' Russian says "na kukhne", "on the kitchen", where English says "in", and it's what everyone says about a restaurant kitchen. "Tikho" is "quiet", for a voice or a room.
- 'Chef is coming. Angry again.' Russian covers "is coming" with "idyot", the on-foot kind. The second sentence has no "is" at all: just "again angry". "Shef" is borrowed from French "chef", meaning chief.
- 'What happened? Football again?' "Chto sluchilos'" is "what happened", a question you'll need constantly. "Futbol" is borrowed whole, and "opyat'", "again", is the word Senya used a moment ago.
- 'Yes, yesterday Spartak played.' "Vchera" is "yesterday", a handy single word. Russian puts the news first, so "igral", "played", comes before the team. Spartak is a Moscow football club, and the chef takes it personally.
- 'And how much did he lose?' "Proigral" is "igral", played, with "pro-" in front, and that prefix turns "played" into "lost". "Skol'ko", "how much", is the same word you'd use to ask a price.
- 'A hundred grand.' "Sto shtuk" is slang: "sto" is a hundred, and "shtuka" means "a piece", which is street slang for a thousand roubles. So it's literally "a hundred pieces".
- 'Unbelievable! A hundred thousand rubles?' "Nichego sebe", "nothing to oneself", is an exclamation of surprise like "wow". After a number like a hundred thousand, "rubl'" turns into "rubley".
- 'Yes, exactly one hundred thousand.' "Rovno" means "exactly", and it also means "even" or "level". Senya adds it because the amount is the whole point.
- 'They always lose before his shift.' "Proigryvayut" is "proigrat'" stretched out with "-yva-" in the middle, which makes it a habit: "they keep losing". "Pered smenoy" is "before the shift".
- 'And what do we do now?' There's no "we" in Russian. "Chto delat'" is "what to do", the plain "to do" with nothing else, and that's how people ask for advice.
- 'Stay silent and just work.' "Molchi" and "rabotay" are both commands, and both are short. "Prosto" is the shrug from earlier: just work, don't make it complicated.
- 'Do not anger the chef today.' "Ne zli" has "zloy", angry, inside it, which Senya used a moment ago: "don't make him angry". "Shefa" has an -a on the end because the chef is the one being angered. And "segodnya" is spelled with a g but said "sevodnya".
- 'Understood. I will be very careful.' "Ponyatno" does the job of "understood". Nastya is a woman, so "careful" ends in "-oy", "ostorozhnoy": the "as a" ending, in the feminine.
- 'Good job. To work!' "Vot i molodets" is "there you go, well done". "Molodets" is the praise word from the soup scene. "Za rabotu", "for work", is how you say "let's get going".
