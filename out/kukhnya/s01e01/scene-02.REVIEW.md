## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What cocktail will you drink, Vika?' "Budesh' pit'" is "you will drink", built just like English: "budesh'", "you will", and then the plain "pit'", "to drink". Max is on the friendly "ty" with her now.
- 'I like dry white wine.' "Lyublyu" is "I love", and Russians use it for liking things too, like wine. "Beloye" and "sukhoye" end in -oye to match "vino", a neuter word, so all three chime together.
- 'Bartender, bring us a glass of white wine!' "Prinesite", "bring", ends in "-ite", the polite form you'd use with a bartender. For "a glass of white wine", "beloye vino" turns into "belogo vina", and that change of ending is the "of".
- 'And what will you drink yourself?' "Sam" means "yourself", and it turns the question back at Max: what about you? The little "a" at the start isn't "and" exactly. It means something like "and as for you".
- 'I will drink a little good juice.' "Vypyu" is "I'll drink", a future with no "will" word in it at all. After "nemnogo", "a little", "sok" becomes "soka", as if he said "a little of good juice".
- 'Do you not drink strong drinks at all?' "Sovsem ne" is "not at all", and it comes before the verb. "Krepkiye napitki", "strong drinks", is the polite way to talk about spirits.
- 'Tomorrow morning I need a clear head.' There's no "I need" in the Russian. It says "mne nuzhna", "to me is needed", and then the thing. A "fresh head" is just how Russians say a clear one.
- 'You take your work very seriously.' "Ser'yozno" is "seriously": take "ser'yozny", serious, and swap the ending for -o, and you get the how-word. Almost any describing word can do this.
- 'I dream of becoming the very best chef!' "Mechtayu", "I dream", comes straight from "mechta", a dream. And "shefom" has the "-om" that means "as", like "povarom" when he first said he wanted to be a cook.
- 'I like your big dream very much.' This is the other way to like something: "mne nravitsya", "to me is pleasing". The dream is the one doing the pleasing, so the verb follows the dream and not the "I".
- 'You look simply stunning tonight.' "Potryasayushche" comes from a verb meaning "to shake", so something stunning is something that shakes you. "Prosto", "simply", makes the compliment sound easy and unforced.
- 'Thank you, Max, I'm very pleased.' "Mne priyatno" is "to me it's pleasant". It's the standard polite reply to a compliment, and it's the same "to me" pattern again.
- 'Let's go dance to this beautiful music!' "Poydyom" is "let's go", then "tantsevat'", "to dance". "Pod muzyku" means "to the music", but "pod" literally means "under", so Russians dance under the music.
