## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What cocktail will you drink, Vika?' "Budesh' pit'" is "you will drink", built like English: a word for "will" and then the plain "to drink". Max ends with her name, and a name needs no change to be called out.
- 'I like dry white wine.' "Lyublyu" is "I love", and Russians use it for liking things too, like wine. "Beloye", "sukhoye" and "vino" all end in the same sound, and that echo is how Russian shows that the describing words belong to "wine".
- 'Bartender, bring us a glass of white wine!' "Prinesite" is a polite order: "bring". The "-ite" on the end addresses a formal "you". "A glass of white wine" makes both "white" and "wine" change their endings, and that change is the "of".
- 'And what will you drink yourself?' "Sam" means "yourself", and it turns the question back at Max: what about you? The little "a" at the start isn't "and" exactly. It means something like "and as for you".
- 'I will drink a little good juice.' "Vypyu" has the little prefix "vy-", which means to drink something up, a serving, rather than just "be drinking". "Nemnogo", "a little", is useful in any sentence.
- 'Do you not drink strong drinks at all?' "Sovsem ne" is "not at all", and notice how Russian puts "at all" first. "Krepkiye napitki" are "strong drinks", the usual way to talk about alcohol.
- 'Tomorrow morning I need a clear head.' There's no "I need" in the Russian. It says "mne nuzhna", "to me is needed", and then the thing. A "fresh head" is just how Russians say a clear one.
- 'You take your work very seriously.' "Ser'yozno" is "seriously": take "ser'yozny", serious, and swap the ending for -o, and you get the how-word. Almost any describing word can do this.
- 'I dream of becoming the very best chef!' "Mechtayu" comes from "mechta", a dream, so you can see where it grows from. "Shefom" has the "-om" ending that means "as a", the same as "povarom" earlier.
- 'I like your big dream very much.' This is the other way to like something: "mne nravitsya", "to me is pleasing". The dream is the one doing the pleasing, so the verb follows the dream and not the "I".
- 'You look simply stunning tonight.' "Potryasayushche" comes from a word meaning "to shake", so something stunning is something that shakes you. "Prosto", "simply", is the shrug that makes the compliment sound easy.
- 'Thank you, Max, I'm very pleased.' "Mne priyatno" is "to me it's pleasant". It's the standard polite reply to a compliment, and it's the same "to me" pattern again.
- 'Let's go dance to this beautiful music!' "Poydyom" is "let's go", then "tantsevat'", "to dance". "Pod muzyku" means "to the music", but "pod" literally means "under", so Russians dance under the music.
