## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktor, and how will this serum get to the bookmaker?' Mikhail asks skeptically as Barinov tries to explain the situation.
- 'Chef, a guest arrived there for a free lunch from the chef.' Nikita centers one concrete touch at once: "besplatnyy" means "free", and it carries the main weight of what Nikita is saying.
- 'Thank you, Nikita, consider the serum already delivered!' Barinov offers that answer frankly here: the everyday word "spasibo" ("thank you") grew out of an old blessing meaning "save you", and it anchors the whole line here.
- 'Well what, Misha, are you betting with me today?' Barinov wonders eagerly why things suddenly took this turn between them.
- 'No, I pass, I have no money right now.' Mikhail picks this vivid choice in earnest: "seychas" means "now", and it carries the main weight of what Mikhail is saying.
- 'Chefs, tell me, who set such a strong mode on the oven?' Barinov brings this update toward Nikita: putting the question word "kto" right at the front of the line lets Nikita hear the point of the question on the very first beat.
- 'Senya, admit it, was it you who put the meat in the oven today?' Barinov wonders briskly how Mikhail expects that to work in practice.
- 'No, chef, I did not even go near the oven today.' Nikita picks this vivid choice in earnest: "voobshche" means "at all", and it carries the main weight of what Nikita is saying.
- 'Then tell me immediately, who did this today?' Barinov directs this statement briskly now: putting the question word "kto" right at the front of the line lets Mikhail hear the point of the question on the very first beat.
- 'Where is Lyova, did he really ruin the whole dish again?' Barinov questions plainly before Nikita can change the subject.
- 'Alright, Viktor, then I will go about my business.' Mikhail grounds one familiar word toward Barinov: "delam" means "business" (from "delo"), giving the line its exact edge.
- 'Go ahead, go, and meanwhile I will prepare soup for the guest!' Barinov shares the thought without pause: starting with "davay" is the classic everyday Russian way to say "come on, let's do this" between people on close terms.
