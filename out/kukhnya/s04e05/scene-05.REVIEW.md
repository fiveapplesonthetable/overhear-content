## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Dear colleagues, I ask for a minute of your attention!' Notice how Russian puts 'respected, esteemed' and 'attention' together in a short, direct phrase where word endings show how the ideas connect.
- 'I want to introduce our new sous-chef to you.' Here German anchors the thought on 'want' and finishes on 'sous-chef', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'This is cook Lavrov Maksim Leonidovich.' Full official Russian style places the surname before the given name and patronymic.
- 'I ask everyone to welcome and respect him.' A classic Russian idiom pairs two infinitives to ask for a warm reception.
- 'Greetings to you, dear kitchen colleagues!' Max enjoys his new status with a formal first-person greeting verb.
- 'Cooks, bow low to our new sous-chef!' Here Fedya anchors the thought on 'cook' and finishes on 'sous-chef', keeping the spoken rhythm brisk and natural.
- 'Max, tell us honestly, what is going on here?' Fedya switches back to informal speech to ask how Max got promoted.
- 'How are you addressing the sous-chef right now?' Max pairs 'how / as' with 'right now' directly, since spoken Russian skips English filler words like 'the', 'a', and 'is'.
- 'Oh, forgive us, respected Maksim Leonidovich!' Fedya mocks his formality by using Max's full name and patronymic.
- 'You have now risen to become one of our bosses!' Russians say 'by me' or 'with us' where English says 'I have' or 'we have', without needing a separate word for 'have'.
- 'Yes, now I am your new boss.' In everyday Russian, 'now, nowadays' flows straight into 'boss' without extra linking words.
- 'Let's all quickly return to work.' A first-person plural exhortation brings the joking brigade back to their stations.
