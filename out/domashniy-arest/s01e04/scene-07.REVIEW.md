## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Samsonov!' Just his surname, in official style. Samson hurries up to the desk and hands over his papers.
- 'Yes, here. Hello. Passport, work book.' In Russia, every employee has an official work book that records every job they have ever held. The clerk looks at her computer.
- 'Hello. I can offer you two vacancies.' After the number 'two', the feminine word 'vacancy' takes the singular 'of' ending. She reads out the first one.
- 'The first one is a loader in a shop.' Because the word 'vacancy' is feminine, 'first' has the feminine ending too. A loader carries heavy boxes, so Nina steps in immediately.
- 'No loader.' Literally, 'loader not needed', blunt and practical.
- 'He's not allowed, he has a bad back.' Literally, 'to him it is forbidden, at him is a back'. In Russian, just saying 'he has a back' or 'he has a heart' means that part of the body gives him trouble. The clerk checks the second option.
- 'Well, then the second one is a promoter.' Another English word borrowed into Russian for someone who hands out leaflets on the street. Nina likes the sound of that.
- 'Promoter is fine. And what does he have to do?' Literally, 'what to do is necessary?'. The clerk reads out the job description without blinking.
- 'Call customers into a children's café.' Because 'customers' are people, the object-form plural looks just like the 'of' form.
- 'In a waffle-cone costume.' Literally, 'in a costume of a waffle horn'. Picture big, burly Samson dressed as an ice-cream cone. Samson is outraged.
- 'Nina, I'm not going to be a waffle!' After 'will not be', the noun 'waffle' goes into the 'with/as' form. Nina doesn't care about his pride; she wants to know the pay.
- 'And how much do they pay?' Third person plural with no subject: 'how much do they pay?'.
- 'On weekdays seven hundred roubles.' Russian omits the present-tense verb 'to be', connecting 'on' directly with 'roubles'.
- 'On weekends, fifteen hundred.' Literally, 'one and a half thousands'. That settles it for Nina.
- 'We agree, do the paperwork!' The plural short form 'agreed' covers both of them, whether Samson likes it or not. As the clerk writes the slip, she looks closely at Samson's face.
- 'I'll write out a referral right now.' The inspector puts 'right now' up front and finishes with 'referral slip' to make the point unmistakable.
- 'Where could I have seen you?' Remember the viral video on the morning news: half the country has already seen Samson throwing the mayor out the window. Samson just stares at her.
- 'What?' Samson asks this with a single word meaning 'what', using rising intonation to turn it into a quick question.
- 'Well, I must have mistaken you for someone else. That's all.' The feminine past verb ends in 'a' before the self-action verb form because the clerk is a woman.
