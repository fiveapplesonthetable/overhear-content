## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Oh, Samson.' Just a little sound of surprise and the name. Then a short, rather cold question.
- 'What have you come for?' The first word asks 'what for'. The verb means 'came', and it is in the masculine past form, because Samson is a man. Samson answers with just two words.
- 'Just once.' The first word is the number one. He means he'll do it, but only one time. Arkady pretends not to understand.
- 'What do you mean, once?' He simply repeats Samson's words with 'what' in front, which makes Samson spell it out.
- 'Well, the thing you said.' He means the humiliating kiss Arkady demanded as the price of the job. The verb 'said' is past tense again, with 'you' in front. Arkady shrugs and calls someone in.
- 'Well, as you like.' The verb means 'you want'. It's a handy phrase whenever you leave a choice to someone else. Then he calls his secretary.
- 'Lidia Alekseyevna, come in, please!' This is the polite command form, the one you use with a boss, a stranger, or an older colleague. Samson doesn't like the idea of an audience.
- 'What's that for?' The same 'what for' as before, plus a little word for 'that'. Arkady has his answer ready.
- 'So that there are witnesses, Samson.' The first word means 'so that'. He wants proof that the deal is done. And so it happens, off stage. Afterwards, Arkady turns to his secretary.
- 'Lidia Alekseyevna, did you see everything?' He uses the polite form of 'you' with her, as he always does. She answers like a perfect secretary.
- 'Yes, Arkady Borisovich. I saw everything.' She calls her boss by his first name and his father's name, which is the respectful way. And her verb ends in the feminine form, because she is a woman. Arkady is pleased.
- 'Now we're at peace.' The last word means both 'peace' and 'the world'. Samson echoes it straight back.
- 'Peace, peace.' He's not really interested in peace, though. He has only one question on his mind.
- 'So when do I start work?' Literally just 'and when to work?'. Russian often drops the verb when the meaning is clear. Now Arkady has something to tell him.
- 'Listen, Samson, I've been thinking.' The verb for 'thought' is in the masculine past tense, marked by the '-l' ending.
- 'Your boss is such an experienced specialist.' There's no word for 'is' in it: Russian simply leaves it out in the present tense. This is the very boss Samson phoned and insulted the day before.
- 'We can't fire him. It's not nice.' The first sentence is 'we cannot him fire'. Short words like 'him' often move to the middle. Samson finally sees where this is going.
- 'Then what am I going to be?' The word for 'what' here takes a special ending, because it is about a job or a role you will have. Arkady has a suggestion.
- 'You go to the job centre. There's work there.' The first verb is a simple command, 'go'. It's a cruel joke: the mayor sends his old enemy to look for work like anyone else. Samson can't believe it.
- 'But you gave your word!' The small word after 'you' adds force, something like 'but you did'. And here comes Arkady's answer.
- 'And what kind of mayor would I be if I kept my word?' He turns the normal idea of an honest politician upside down, on purpose. So the promise was revenge, not a job offer, just as he swore as a boy.
