## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Oh, Vanya! You did everything, and there's no job.' In response, Vanya is the everyday form of Ivan, Samson's real name; Samson is only a nickname. The little word in the middle means 'and' with a hint of 'but': it sets up the contrast. Samson doesn't like her tone.
- 'Why are you shouting?' Literally, 'and what are you shouting?' In complaints like this, 'what' often does the job of 'why'. Nina throws the question straight back.
- 'Why am I shouting?' The same verb, now with 'I'. Then she gives the real reason.
- 'I want to live a normal life!' Word for word, 'I to live normally want'. Word order is loose in Russian: you can move words around for emphasis and the meaning stays the same. Samson tries to defend himself.
- 'And what could I do?' 'Could' is in the past tense, in the short form a man uses; a woman would add a syllable at the end. Nina knows exactly what he should have done.
- 'You should have got a receipt from him.' The first two words together mean 'it was necessary', a handy way to say 'you should have'. A receipt here is a written note that proves a promise was made. Samson can hardly believe his ears.
- 'A receipt? From the mayor?' After the little word for 'from', the mayor gets a new ending, the one that means 'of'. Nina has no doubts at all.
- 'Yes! Even a child knows.' Their teenage daughter Yulia is listening in, and she clearly agrees. So Samson sends her off.
- 'Yulia, go and do your homework!' Literally, 'go learn your lessons'. Two verbs side by side with no 'and' between them, very natural in speech. Then Nina notices the television.
- 'Oh, look! The news.' The word for news is plural, and it always talks about several pieces of news at once. And here comes the big story.
- 'The town's mayor, Arkady Anikeev, has been detained.' This is television language: the verb comes first, in a short formal form meaning 'detained'. It's the man who cheated Samson. Samson can't believe it.
- 'What? Our Arkasha?' Right then, Arkasha, and this cheekier form of it, are what everyone called Arkady when he was a little boy in this very flat. 'Our' makes him one of the family again. The newsreader goes on.
- 'The court has decided: house arrest at his registered address.' In Russia every person is officially registered at one address, and that phrase at the end means exactly that. Nina works it out first.
- 'At his registered address? But that's here, at our place!' The short word after 'that' adds 'but, you know', and the last two words mean 'at our home'. Arkady never moved his registration out of the communal flat. And there's one more piece of news.
- 'The new mayor is Anikeev's deputy.' There's no 'is' in the present tense: a short dash does the job. The deputy is Pasha, the ambitious man we met at the town hall. Samson sums it up.
- 'Well, that's news!' Said with feeling, it means 'what a surprise'. And Nina has the last word.
- 'No job, and no mayor either.' Word for word, 'and of work there's none, and of mayor there's none'. After 'there is no', the missing thing takes the ending for 'of'. The double 'and' works like 'neither, nor'.
