## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Arkady Borisovich, I'm sorry, there's a man here to see you.' Literally, 'here, to you, a man'. No verb at all, and it's perfectly natural. She explains that she tried to stop him.
- 'I told him you're not seeing anyone today.' The verb 'told' ends in 'a' because the secretary is a woman. And she uses the polite 'you' with her boss. But Arkady waves it away.
- 'It's all right, Lidia Alekseyevna.' Literally 'everything is normal', a phrase you'll hear all the time. Then he looks at his visitor.
- 'Well, hello, Samson.' Notice the greeting: it's the casual form of hello, for someone you know well. Samson is surprised.
- 'You recognised me.' Just one word, a past-tense verb, and the 'you' is understood. Then he says thank you.
- 'Thanks for seeing me.' Literally, 'thank you that you received me'. A useful pattern: thank you, then 'that', then what the person did. He admits he was worried.
- 'I thought maybe you wouldn't see me.' After all, he once pushed Arkady's head into a toilet. Arkady acts as if it never happened.
- 'Oh, come on! I'm very glad to see you.' The word for 'glad' is masculine here, because Arkady is a man. A woman would add an 'a'. Then he asks for news.
- 'So, tell me, how are you doing?' The first verb is a friendly command, 'tell me', and the last two words are the everyday 'how are things'. Samson answers simply.
- 'Everything's fine. I work on a building site.' Here's the grammar point: 'on a building site' uses the location ending, an 'e' on the end. You'll meet it after 'in' and 'on' whenever you say where something is. Then Samson gets to the point.
- 'Arkash, I wanted to ask you something.' The name is cut short, the friendly way to call someone. And the verb comes last, which sounds relaxed and a bit shy. So what does he want?
- 'Excavator driver isn't my level. Maybe you could help?' A long word for his job, then 'not my level'. The last verb is in the future, 'will you help?'. Arkady doesn't hesitate.
- 'Of course I'll help. Who's your boss now?' The question begins with 'who', and the word for 'boss' comes last. Samson names him.
- 'Well, it's Golovamzyuk.' That's the boss's surname. Arkady has a bold idea.
- 'Let's fire him and put you in his place.' 'Let's' plus 'we will': a very common way to suggest doing something together. And 'him' and 'you' sit before the verbs. Samson can't believe his luck.
- 'No way, what, seriously?' The first two words are a very common way to say 'you're kidding'. Arkady pushes him on.
- 'Of course. Call him.' A short, casual command. So Samson dials the number and speaks to his boss, using the boss's first name and his father's name.
- 'Nikolai Filippovich, I'm in the mayor's office right now.' Literally 'in the office at the mayor's'. And then the big news.
- 'Now I'm the boss of our building site. You're fired!' No word for 'am' again: 'now I boss'. And to his old boss he suddenly uses the casual, rude 'you'. Arkady watches with a smile.
