## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktor Stepanovich, greetings.' A very formal hello, the kind you use with someone important. Vika can hear every word. The caller is far less formal.
- 'Hi, Arkady Borisovich.' A casual 'hi', but with his full name and his father's name. Then a simple question.
- 'What are you doing?' This is the casual 'you': the ending of the verb says it, so no pronoun is needed. Arkady answers like a politician.
- 'Working. Making people's lives better.' Again, no 'I': the verb endings already mean 'I work', 'I make'. The caller has a better idea.
- 'And you don't want to make my life better?' Quite a request from a government official. Arkady keeps up the act.
- 'Viktor Stepanovich, I really can't today.' The word in front of 'can't' means 'in no way', so it's a strong no. And he gives a reason.
- 'There's a lot of work.' Literally 'of work, very much'. After words for 'a lot', the noun takes the 'of' form. The caller doesn't give up.
- 'Well, come in the evening.' The verb means to come by car or transport, and it's a casual command. Arkady tries another excuse.
- 'But I promised to be with my wife this evening.' The word for 'promised' ends in a consonant because Arkady is a man. Now the caller sulks.
- 'Well, if you don't want to, suit yourself.' Word for word: 'you don't want, as you want'. A handy phrase when someone says no to you. And then a little threat.
- 'I'll have a good time on my own.' Listen to the word for 'myself' or 'on my own': it ends in 'a', which is the feminine form. So our Viktor Stepanovich is a woman, and Arkady gives in fast.
- 'There's no need to shout. All right, I'll be there tonight.' 'I will be' is the future of 'to be'. Then he hangs up like a businessman.
- 'Right, cheers then.' Word for word it says 'I shake your hand', a firm goodbye between men in business, which is funny when the caller is a woman. Vika is curious.
- 'And?' Two little words: 'and what?'. Arkady has his story ready.
- 'He's called an emergency meeting.' The verb is masculine past, so 'he', the official, called it. It's the official story for his wife. Vika gets the message.
- 'So I shouldn't wait for you tonight?' Literally, 'so, not to wait for you today?' Russians often ask with a bare verb like this. Arkady says it very sweetly.
- 'No, darling, don't wait.' The last word is a command, 'don't wait'. And he sighs, as if it's a sacrifice.
- 'I need to do a bit of work.' 'Need' comes at the end here, which makes it sound tired and casual.
