## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'The court has considered the new circumstances of the case.' The verb literally means 'took into account'. And what are those new circumstances?
- 'Conflicts with the neighbours threaten the defendant's health.' After the verb 'threaten', 'health' takes the dative case: literally, 'threaten to the health'. That is how Berg justifies letting Arkady leave the communal flat.
- 'The court has ruled to grant Anikeev's appeal.' In Russian legal style, the infinitive 'to satisfy' comes right at the end of the sentence.
- 'To replace house arrest with a travel ban.' With the verb 'replace', the new thing is introduced by 'onto': replace house arrest onto a travel ban. Arkady can't contain his joy.
- 'Yes! At last! I'm free!' He uses the short masculine form for 'free'. Judge Berg bangs his gavel.
- 'I demand silence! Respect the court!' After 'I demand', 'silence' takes the genitive ending. Just as Arkady prepares to walk out a free man, the prosecutor rises.
- 'Your Honour, we have an important statement.' In a Russian courtroom, the judge is addressed as 'Your Honour'. Then comes the bombshell.
- 'The prosecution asks to add new material to the case.' Literally, 'to attach new material'. Arkady's smile vanishes.
- 'What new material? What is this?' He echoes the prosecutor's word in the genitive case: 'of what material besides?' Judge Berg has no choice in open court.
- 'Very well. Call your witness.' Literally, that means 'invite your witness into the room'.
- 'Please enter the courtroom.' Literally, 'I ask into the hall of the court'. The door opens, and Arkady stares at the man who walks in.
- 'Who is that? You?!' It is his own stuttering public defender, Danila Golovkin, whom Arkady casually bragged to about handing out flats through connections.
