## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Hi, Samson. What did you want?' In Russian, asking 'what did you want?' in the past tense is a natural, casual way to ask 'what can I do for you?'. Samson comes straight out with his news.
- 'Here's the thing.' Literally, 'such a matter', a classic way to open an awkward request.
- 'They're going to give us a flat.' The verb is third person plural with no subject: 'they will give to us a flat'. Arkady pretends to be delighted for him.
- 'Great, congratulations.' Literally, 'great, I congratulate'. Then Samson explains the catch.
- 'We just need you to hand over the money, Arkasha.' After 'in order that', Russian always uses the past-tense form of the verb, so literally 'that you handed over the money'. Arkady plays innocent.
- 'What money?' Because the word for 'money' is always plural in Russian, the question word 'what kind of' is plural too.
- 'Yours. The people's money.' Both words are plural to match 'money'. Samson means the public money Arkady stole from the town. Arkady turns the phrase into a joke.
- 'Ah, well for the people's money, go to the people.' No verb at all: just 'for the people's, to the people'. Samson drops all pretence.
- 'Seriously, tell me, where's the money?' After asking if Arkady is joking, he uses the informal command 'tell'.
- 'They hired me.' Samson blurts out that someone put him up to this, and Arkady guesses who.
- 'Was it Osipenko who hired you?' Osipenko is the surname of Pasha, Arkady's treacherous deputy. The two little words in the middle mean 'or what, is it really?'. Then Arkady calls Samson's bluff.
- 'And if I don't tell you, then what?' Again, the single-action form 'I will tell' points to the future.
- 'Will you cut my ear off?' In Russian, body parts take 'to me' instead of 'my': 'will you cut off an ear to me?'. Samson tries to sound menacing.
- 'Well, maybe.' Literally, 'well, let's suppose'. Arkady just smiles and flatters him.
- 'Samson, you're a kind, good person.' The little particle after 'you' means 'after all, as we both know'. Samson is baffled and holds up Arkady's bandaged hand.
- 'What? I broke your finger. Did you forget?' Back in the first episode, Samson slammed the door on Arkady's finger. Arkady waves that away.
- 'I tricked you, and you were right.' All masculine forms, because both men are speaking about themselves.
- 'Now everything has changed, Samson.' And he lays it on even thicker.
- 'I've grown fond of you. You're my friend.' The prefix on the verb 'loved' turns it into 'came to love, grew fond of'. Then comes Arkady's new lie about the money.
- 'There is no money, Samson.' With 'there is no', the plural word 'money' drops its ending altogether.
- 'I lost it on the stock exchange.' Because 'money' is plural, he says 'I lost them'. Samson loses his temper.
- 'Don't you get it, four-eyes?' He falls back on his old schoolyard nickname for Arkady.
- 'I'm going to have to torture you.' Literally, 'to me it will fall to torture you'. Arkady bursts out laughing.
- 'Torture? What childish words. Go on then, torture me, Samson!' First the basic verb form 'to torture', and then the command 'torture!'. Samson has no idea what to do next and walks out.
