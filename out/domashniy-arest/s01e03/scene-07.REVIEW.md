## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Is there no way to cancel this hearing?' Literally, 'is it impossible in no way to cancel?'. Russian loves double negatives for emphasis. The judge shakes his head.
- 'Well, how can I cancel it?' Literally, 'how will I cancel it?'. The pronoun 'him' refers to the hearing.
- 'It's the prosecutor's decision.' Before Pasha can answer, the little dog spots something in the grass.
- 'Lyutik, Lyutik! Drop it! What are you doing there? Lyutik, stop!' The dog's name means 'Buttercup'. The short command after his name is what Russian dog owners say for 'drop it, leave it alone!'. Pasha is losing patience.
- 'Ilya Olegovich, please! Can you listen to me for two minutes?' Notice that Pasha starts with the polite 'you' and the judge's name and father's name. After the number 'two', the feminine word 'minute' takes the singular 'of' ending.
- 'Yes, of course. Wait, just now, one little second.' The last word is a soft, affectionate form of 'second'. And he is off after the dog again.
- 'Lyutik, where do you find this stuff? What did he eat again?' First he talks to the dog as 'you', and then to Pasha about the dog as 'he'. Pasha has had enough of the walk.
- 'Ilya Olegovich! I can't walk around here all day.' 'All day' is literally 'a whole day', in the object form for how long something lasts. The judge apologises.
- 'Sorry, it's my daughter's dog.' Literally, 'dog of daughter'.
- 'I'm walking her for now.' Even though Buttercup is a boy dog, the Russian word for 'dog' is feminine, so the judge says 'with her'. At that, Pasha drops the polite 'you' altogether.
- 'Oh, wait a minute with your dog!' Now it's the informal command 'wait' and the informal 'you': Pasha is showing who is really the boss here. And he gives the judge his order.
- 'You just keep Anikeev under house arrest for me.' Literally, 'you to me Anikeev leave better under house arrest'. The title of the show appears right here in the 'with/as' form after 'under'. The judge looks worried.
- 'Pavel Yuryevich, I would keep him.' Adding the little particle 'would' to a past-tense verb makes the conditional: 'I would leave him'.
- 'But that Mishkin simply flattened us.' Literally, 'rolled us out flat', like pastry dough. Mishkin is the expensive Moscow lawyer from Scene 1.
- 'There are so many mistakes there! After all, we did everything in a hurry.' After 'so many', the word 'mistakes' takes the plural 'of' form. Pasha tells him not to worry about the Moscow lawyer.
- 'Don't you worry about that Mishkin. We're working on him.' Literally, 'we are working with him', an ominous way to say they are already dealing with the lawyer.
- 'The main thing is, you keep him at home.' The same command 'leave, keep' once again.
