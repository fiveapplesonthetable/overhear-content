## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'In short, girls.' The first word literally means 'shorter', and Russians use it all the time to mean 'long story short'.
- 'You can't take anyone at their word.' Literally, 'to believe on word is impossible to nobody'. Because Russian uses double negation, 'nobody' goes in the dative case alongside 'impossible'.
- 'Got that? You need a stamp in the passport.' The first word is plural past tense: 'did you understand?' In Russia, an official marriage is recorded with a stamp inside your passport. Or else, she adds:
- 'Or a bank account. Because they are all cheats.' Both 'in the passport' and 'in the bank' use the prepositional case ending in '-e' for location. Then she gets personal.
- 'I lost two years like that. I loved him so much!' Both verbs end in '-la', the feminine past-tense ending, because Lyolya is a woman. Notice 'two years': after the number two, 'year' takes the genitive singular.
- 'And I only have a flat left. And they locked him up!' Literally, 'at me only a flat remained'. The verb in the second sentence is third person plural with no 'they', which Russian uses for an impersonal 'they locked him up'. Slavik sees his cover story falling apart and tries to stop her.
- 'Babe, maybe you've had enough already?' Literally, 'maybe to you it will suffice already'. Lyolya turns on him and laughs in front of the whole room.
- 'You're my boyfriend? That will never happen in your life!' With negative 'will not be', the thing that won't happen takes the genitive form.
- 'You'll never have a girl like me!' Again, because the sentence is negative — 'at you there will not be' — 'girl' stands in the genitive singular.
- 'Do you understand me? You're still just a boy!' She asks 'did you understand?' in the masculine singular past tense, because she is speaking to Slavik. Dimon cannot believe his ears.
- 'Then why did you come with him?' The verb 'came' is feminine past tense, and after 'with' the pronoun 'he' becomes 'with him'. Lyolya casually gives away the whole secret.
- 'My ex simply asked me to play along for him.' The word for 'ex' is an adjective meaning 'former', used on its own as a noun. After 'asked so that', Russian always uses the past tense form of the verb, even for a request.
- 'Oh, I've had a bit to drink now. I feel so sad all of a sudden.' Literally, 'to me now so sad it became', using the dative 'to me' and neuter past tense. Dimon grins at poor Slavik.
- 'Well, well! Slavik, what a cheat you are!' He turns Lyolya's own word for a cheat back onto Slavik.
