## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What are you doing in here?' Literally, 'and what is this you here?' Samson quickly covers for himself and Nina.
- 'Nothing, son. Everything's fine.' The word for 'son' is an affectionate diminutive, 'little son'. Then Samson spots the fluffy white bundle in the boy's arms.
- 'And what is that you brought into the flat?' After 'into', the word for 'flat' takes the object ending because something was carried inside. Slavik answers simply.
- 'Uncle Arkasha asked me to.' Russian children call adult neighbours and family friends 'Uncle' or 'Aunt' plus their short first name. Samson grins at Arkady.
- 'See, Arkasha, he brought you a dog.' The verb is past tense masculine, matching the boy. Arkady immediately sees his chance and wants the room cleared.
- 'Listen, I need to be left alone.' He uses the plural command 'listen' because he is speaking to both Samson and Nina. Literally, 'to me it is needed alone to remain'.
- 'I need to concentrate.' The self-action verb form at the end of the long verb means gathering yourself together: focusing your own mind. Samson doesn't argue.
- 'All right, come on, let's go.' He leads Nina out of the room. Before Slavik follows, Arkady calls for the dog.
- 'Slavik, hand over the dog.' Here the everyday word for 'come on' means 'give me'. Slavik holds on for a second to check their bargain.
- 'What about Lyolya? You promised.' He uses the plural past tense for 'promised', which is how Russian shows polite respect to one grown-up.
- 'You'll get your Lyolya, I said. Go on.' Literally, 'your Lyolya will be'. Once the door shuts, Arkady is alone with the judge's tiny dog and puts on a tough voice.
- 'What is it, little guy? Are you scared?' The verb for 'got scared' is masculine past tense, because the little dog is a boy.
- 'I'm getting out of here right now.' Russian uses the future-tense form with 'right now' for something about to happen any minute.
- 'Look here. You just sit quietly for a bit.' The prefix on 'sit' means 'for a little while', and the long adverb at the end is a soft diminutive for 'quietly'.
