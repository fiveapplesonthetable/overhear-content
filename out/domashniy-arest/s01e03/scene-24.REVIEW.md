## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Anatoly Ivanovich, there's pizza delivery here.' Zavarzin uses the major's first name and father's name, even when they are alone in a cold room.
- 'They write: we'll deliver in ten minutes.' The preposition before 'ten minutes' is how Russian says within a set time: 'in ten minutes'. And there's a bonus.
- 'If they're late, the pizza is free.' Literally 'if they will be late, pizza free of charge'.
- 'Maybe we should order?' Literally 'maybe we will order?'. Lavrov is a strict rule-follower.
- 'Not allowed. It's against regulations.' Literally 'not according to the instruction'. Zavarzin reminds him of an old mission.
- 'What about that time in Omsk? That wasn't by the book either.' Omsk is a big city in Siberia, where the two of them clearly broke the rules once. Lavrov shuts that down.
- 'Back then it was my mistake.' The verb 'was' is feminine to match 'mistake'.
- 'And we were drunk.' Plural past tense, plus a plural adjective. He reminds Zavarzin of their promise.
- 'You and I made a deal back then. First and last time.' Notice how Russian says 'you and I': literally 'we with you'. Just then, someone knocks and walks right into the secret post—it's young Lieutenant Lyova Sokolov, the local chief's nephew.
- 'Good health to you, sir! How are things here?' The first two words are the formal military greeting to a superior officer, literally 'I wish you health'. And Lyova hasn't come empty-handed.
- 'I've brought you some tea and bread rings.' Notice the ending on 'tea': instead of the usual object ending, Russian often uses the '-u' ending for 'some tea'. Hungry Zavarzin is happy to see it.
- 'Oh, thank you.' At least it's better than nothing. Lyova looks around the bare room and grins at their bright orange janitor vests.
- 'It's nice in here, roomy.' Literally 'normal by you here, spacious'.
- 'And good disguises—you look like janitors.' Literally 'similar to courtyard-sweepers'. Lavrov is not amused.
- 'Lieutenant, don't you know that we're on a mission?' Being 'on a mission' uses the preposition 'on'.
- 'You mustn't just walk in on us.' The two little words in the middle mean 'just like that', without calling first. Lyova explains why he came.
- 'I'm here on business.' A very useful three-word phrase.
- 'I want to join your group.' He is tired of sitting in his uncle's archive and wants to be a Moscow agent. Lavrov refuses at once.
- 'No, that's impossible.' Plain and final.
- 'We're from different departments.' After 'from', both words take the genitive plural ending. Lyova takes offence.
- 'I see. So you don't need me.' Literally 'to you I am not needed', with the short masculine form of 'needed'. He turns to stomp out, and Lavrov stops him at the door.
- 'Freeze! All clear, now go.' Using the infinitive 'to stand!' is how an officer barks 'halt!'. Lavrov checks the corridor outside first so Lyova doesn't blow their cover.
