## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'You're so funny, Tuzik!' In the flat, old Klavdia has given the judge's little dog a classic Russian dog name, Tuzik, instead of Lyutik. Both adjectives take the masculine ending for a male dog.
- 'Who's so sweet?' The word for 'sweet' is used just like in English when talking to a pet or a baby.
- 'Come on, Tuzik, let's get up!' The word for 'come on' is followed by 'we get up' in the present tense, which is how Russian says 'let's get up'. Meanwhile, little Lyosha proudly shows off his new electric guitar from the dean. At the kitchen table, Arkady has a headache after court.
- 'Please be quiet.' Literally, 'be silent for a little while', in the polite plural command form.
- 'Can't you see someone isn't feeling well?' In Russian, 'a person feels bad' puts 'person' in the 'to' case: literally, 'to a person it is bad'. Marina spots the bottle of the dean's Chilean wine in front of him, and here we hear her scolding in Nina's voice.
- 'So you just take other people's things without asking, yes?' The word for 'other people's' is a neuter adjective used as a noun: 'that which belongs to others'. Arkady isn't ashamed at all.
- 'It's good wine. I feel very sad right now.' Again, feelings use the 'to' form of the pronoun: literally, 'to me now it is very sad'. Meanwhile, poor Slavik comes home heart-broken after the party, and he is told to eat.
- 'Slavik, sit down. Eat.' Two short informal commands to a child. In the next room, Nina is staring at the photos of the three-room flat that Pasha promised them.
- 'Three rooms, a kitchen, renovated.' After the numbers two, three, and four, Russian nouns take the singular 'of' ending, so 'three of a room'. And the last word means a fresh renovation.
- 'What a flat it was!' The verb 'was' has the feminine ending '-a' to match the feminine word for flat. Samson tries to defend himself.
- 'Nina, well what could we have done?' The verb 'could' is in the plural past tense, 'we could', followed by the completed-action verb 'to do'. Nina turns on him.
- 'What do you mean, we?' She repeats his 'we' in annoyance: it wasn't 'we' who backed down.
- 'You should have acted like a man!' Literally, 'to you it was needed like a tough guy'. The word she uses for a man is the colloquial word for a real, tough man. Samson bristles.
- 'Funny you should say that.' Literally, 'so interesting', in the feminine form: an everyday sarcastic reply to a woman who asks for the impossible.
- 'I'm no gangster for you.' In the show he says this much more crudely, telling her to go marry a gangster with a soldering iron. Nina is still upset.
- 'And where are we going to live now?' Here the future tense is made with 'we will' plus the ongoing-action form 'to live', because living somewhere is an ongoing state. Samson has had enough of the argument.
- 'All right, calm down already. Calm down.' The command ends in a self-action verb form, literally 'calm yourself'. Just then, someone knocks hard at the front door.
- 'Quiet! Who is knocking there?' Even when asking about several people outside, the word 'who' always takes a singular verb in Russian. Arkady gets up.
- 'I'm going to open it now.' Again, the compound future: 'I will' plus 'open'. He has no idea who is waiting on the other side of that door.
