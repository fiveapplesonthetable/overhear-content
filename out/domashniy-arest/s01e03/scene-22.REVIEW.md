## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Marina Sergeyevna, have a seat.' He uses her first name and father's name, with a polite plural command that literally means 'sit down for a moment'. Then he points to the table.
- 'Try the tea with bread rings, they're very tasty.' Bread rings are small, crunchy ring-shaped rolls that Russians love with tea. After 'with', the noun takes the instrumental plural ending. Marina replies politely.
- 'Yes, thank you, Mikhail Mikhailovich.' Both of them stay on formal terms. Now the chief gets to the news.
- 'Well, everything worked itself out.' Literally 'everything resolved itself on its own'. Remember, earlier Marina couldn't plant the bug because Arkady came back to his room too soon.
- 'Anikeev is going to court today.' The verb for 'is going' specifically means going by vehicle, not on foot. And that gives Marina plenty of time.
- 'He'll be gone for about two hours.' Literally 'of him there will not be hours two'. In Russian, putting the noun 'hours' before the number 'two' turns it into an estimate: 'about two hours'.
- 'You'll do everything calmly.' A perfective future verb: one clean job, finished while Arkady is away. Marina is still uneasy.
- 'And then what?' Literally 'and further?'. She isn't a secret agent, and this whole business scares her.
- 'I'm very nervous, I'm not cut out for this.' The last word has the feminine short ending because Marina is a woman: literally 'not adapted to this'. Then the chief reaches into his desk.
- 'Oh, by the way, I almost forgot.' The little word before 'not' means 'nearly' or 'almost', so 'almost didn't forget' is the Russian idiom for 'almost forgot'. He puts an envelope on the table.
- 'What's this?' Two tiny words. The chief explains.
- 'This is your advance.' Cash up front for helping the security service. And like any Russian office, there is paperwork.
- 'Here, you need to sign the payment sheet.' Literally 'in the register to sign it is necessary'. Marina looks at the money with indignation.
- 'What, are you buying me or something?' The two little words at the end add an indignant 'or what?'. Remember, she only agreed to help because of her son and her job. The chief doesn't blink.
- 'Nobody is buying anybody.' Notice the double negative: 'nobody' in the subject form, 'nobody' in the object form, plus 'not' before the verb. In Russian, all three go together.
- 'It's built into the operation budget.' To the chief, it's just normal bookkeeping. He hands her the pen.
- 'Please, go ahead.' Literally 'I ask', a formal one-word way to invite someone to sign or step through a door. Marina signs the sheet and takes the envelope, but she can't resist a parting shot.
- 'Fine.' She gives in, then looks at the cash.
- 'So this is where taxpayers' money goes.' That long last word is 'of taxpayers', built from the words for 'tax' and 'payer'. Then she gets up to leave.
- 'Right, I'm off. Goodbye.' She uses the feminine past form, 'I set off', which is how Russians say 'I'm heading out'. In the show, she even tries to walk out through the archive door by mistake. Once she is gone, the chief sighs.
- 'We should have taken the old lady.' Literally 'it was necessary to take the grandmother'—meaning old Klavdia Viktorovna, whom he wanted to recruit in the first place.
