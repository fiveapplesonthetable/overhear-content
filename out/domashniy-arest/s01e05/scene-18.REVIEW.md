## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Vanya, let me give you some cabbage.' She uses an affectionate diminutive for 'cabbage'.
- 'Sorry, Vanya, I was wrong.' Because Nina is a woman, both the verb 'was' and the short adjective 'wrong' end in '-a'. Samson puffs out his chest.
- 'A leader!' Samson is delighted with the word and repeats it proudly.
- 'I've felt it since childhood, actually.' Then he sums up what he felt.
- 'I am a special person.' Putting the adjective after the noun gives it extra weight: 'a person who is special'. Nina looks at him admiringly.
- 'Consider yourself, Vanya, like my chosen one.' Just then, Samson spots teenage Yulia smirking at her smartphone across the table.
- 'And why are you sitting there smiling?' He glares across the table at his daughter.
- 'Why are you poking at your phone?' The verb means to poke or jab at a screen.
- 'Dad is cleaning the city of criminals!' Samson still believes Danila's lie that he rammed a getaway car full of robbers. Afraid that Yulia will read the real news about Samson's drunken crash into the Society of the Blind, Nina quickly steps in.
- 'Yulia, put the phone aside.' Here, Yulia clicks her tongue in annoyance, and Nina snaps at her.
- 'How are you talking to your mother?' After 'with', the irregular noun 'mother' takes the 'with/as' ending.
- 'Why are you even here?' Nina realises it is the middle of a school day.
- 'Why aren't you at school?' In Russian, 'at school' uses the preposition 'in' with the location form.
- 'My maths teacher fell ill.' Notice how Russian says 'teacher by mathematics' using the preposition with the 'to/for' form. When Nina asks about her other subjects, Yulia shrugs.
- 'And I've learned everything.' The prefix on the verb shows she has learned it all thoroughly.
- 'I do well at school.' Literally 'I study well'. Nina is not having it.
- 'Yulia, get up and go wash the dishes!' Notice how Russian parents often use past-tense forms—literally 'stood up and went!'—as an immediate command. Before Yulia can leave with her phone, Samson holds out his hand.
- 'Hold on, Nina.' Samson decides to take charge of the situation himself.
- 'Hand the little phone over here!' Literally 'give hither'.
- 'Here you go.' The same word means both 'please' and 'here you are' when handing something over.
- 'It's still bought with my money!' The line opens with 'for now' and lands on 'bought' at the end for emphasis.
- 'Get up and wash the dishes, forward march!' He finishes with the army drill command 'forward march!'.
