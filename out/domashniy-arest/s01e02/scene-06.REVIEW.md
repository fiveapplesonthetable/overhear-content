## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Little ones can hold on, but grown-ups can't, got it?' The verb that follows 'little ones' means 'can', in the sense of 'know how to'. At the end he checks the boy has understood, with a masculine past form, because he's talking to a boy. The boy protests.
- 'That's not fair.' Notice that 'not' is glued to the word for 'fair' here, and it makes one word: 'unfair'. Arkady pretends to be friendly.
- 'What's your name, boy?' Literally, 'how do they call you?'. It's the normal way to ask a name, and you'll use it all the time. The answer is just one word.
- 'Lyokha.' A casual, boyish form of Alexei. Elsewhere you'll also hear him called Lyosha. Arkady then uses the name against him.
- 'It's all fair, Lyokha.' The same word for 'fair', now without the 'not'. Then comes his reason.
- 'Grown-ups can't wait.' The same 'know how to' verb as before, now with 'not', and then the verb 'to wait'. That's a neat pattern: 'know how' plus a verb in its dictionary form. Lyosha calls for help.
- 'Mum!' Exactly what you'd expect.
- 'He's jumping the queue!' Word for word: 'he goes without queue'. It's the classic complaint in any line in Russia. Soon someone is knocking on the toilet door.
- 'Listen, what's the matter, woman?' He starts with the polite command form of 'listen'. But calling a stranger simply 'woman' sounds rude and old-fashioned.
- 'It's occupied.' Literally 'here is taken'. It's the word you'll hear through any locked toilet door. The woman outside is not impressed.
- 'You're a bribe-taker!' She uses the polite 'you', but there's nothing polite about the rest. Remember, Arkady was arrested for taking a bribe.
- 'You're pushing in ahead of a sick child.' The verb literally means 'climb', and it's the word for barging in. 'Ahead of' is followed by a special form of the words for 'sick' and 'child', the form that means 'of'. Now Arkady takes a proper look at her.
- 'I think I've seen you somewhere.' The verb is past, masculine, because Arkady is a man. The little word at the end means 'it seems'. She reminds him who she is.
- 'I'm your adviser on historical heritage.' She is Marina Sergeyevna, the lecturer who led the protest to save the old monastery wall, and Arkady invited her onto a council about it. And she lives right here, in this flat.
- 'Have you forgotten?' One word does the whole job: it's the past tense in the polite 'you' form.
- 'Yes, yes. But the toilet isn't historical heritage yet.' The word in the middle means 'for now', or 'yet'. He's mocking her, from behind a locked door.
- 'That's it, excuse me.' The polite 'excuse me', but here it really means 'go away'. Marina has a parting shot.
- 'Remember: evil always comes back to the ruler.' It's a polite command, 'remember', then a little proverb. The last words mean 'to the ruler'. Arkady has an answer ready.
- 'Tell that to Stalin.' The name has an ending that means 'to'. In other words: some rulers did very well, thank you. Lyosha still doesn't know who the rude man is.
- 'Mum, who is that?' A small word for 'and' or 'but' in front of 'who' makes it a curious, natural question. His mother tells him.
- 'That's our mayor, Lyosha.' Just 'this is our mayor', with no word for 'is'. A moment later Arkady's own former bodyguards burst in while he's still in there; they apologise, then drag him away 'with frightening rudeness', exactly as ordered.
