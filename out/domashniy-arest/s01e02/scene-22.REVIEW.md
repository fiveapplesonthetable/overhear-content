## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Our town is very old.' Word for word, 'our-with-you town', a warm way for a teacher to say 'our town, yours and mine'. There's no word for 'is': in the present tense Russian simply leaves it out.
- 'For almost four centuries there were raids here.' Nomad horsemen kept attacking the land where the town now stands. Then Marina notices a student who isn't taking notes.
- 'And why aren't you writing?' She uses the casual 'you' with her student. The student has a modern excuse.
- 'Well, my voice recorder is on.' Literally, 'at me the recorder switched itself on': the device does it all by itself. Marina is not impressed.
- 'And will the voice recorder take the exam for you?' The future here is built with the verb 'will be' plus a verb in its basic form, a very common pattern. Then Marina is called out of the room.
- 'Right, no noise. I'll be back soon.' Teachers often give orders with 'we': literally, 'we don't make noise'. In the dean's office she gets news she didn't expect.
- 'You're firing me? Are you serious?' She uses the polite 'you' with her boss, even now. The dean doesn't argue.
- 'Yes. I'm sorry.' The second word is the casual command 'forgive me'. He says it to her with the familiar 'you': in the show they were once a couple.
- 'For my conduct? Are you laughing at me?' The official reason sounds grand: conduct unworthy of a teacher. After 'at', the word for 'me' changes its form. And then the dean says he is the one who suffers.
- 'I feel bitter, I feel awful. I have a bad heart.' Russian says 'to me it's bitter, to me it's bad' for how you feel. Marina has a real problem.
- 'I have a sick child. Where will I find work?' To say 'I have', Russian says 'at me there is'. Her son Lyosha walks on crutches. The dean has an answer ready.
- 'I've already thought about that. Here.' He hands her a sheet with job offers. She reads it out.
- 'A sales assistant in a phone shop?' The last two words mean a shop that sells phones and contracts. For a history lecturer it's an insult.
- 'Why not?' Literally, 'and why no?' Then he even makes it sound like a promotion.
- 'A sales assistant earns more than a lecturer.' The word for 'more' and then 'than', just as in English. Sadly, in the town that is probably true. Then comes the strangest part.
- 'But that's only work. Marina, I love you.' He fires her and declares his love in the same breath. Marina can only laugh.
- 'How stupid and ridiculous.' Two short adverbs, both ending in the same sound. He takes it badly.
- 'You find it funny, but I'm very sad.' Literally, 'to you it's funny, and to me it's very sad'. The same pattern as before: the person who feels something takes the form for 'to'. Then his phone rings.
- 'Sorry, I have to answer this. Yes, Allochka!' The word for 'have to' is in the masculine form, because the dean is a man. Allochka is a fond form of the name Alla, a colleague, and Marina is forgotten at once.
