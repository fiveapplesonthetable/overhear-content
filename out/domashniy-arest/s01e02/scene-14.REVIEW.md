## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What have you come for again?' Literally, 'well, what have you come again?' The verb is in the past tense, in the form for a man. And the chief adds a second question.
- 'Can't you see I'm working?' Word for word, 'you don't see, I am working'. Lyova apologises, and then asks for what he really wants.
- 'Sorry.' This is the friendly form, for someone you call by first name. Then comes the request.
- 'Uncle Misha, move me to the field team, would you?' Uncle Misha is the chief, Mikhail Mikhailovich. In everyday speech people clip names when they call someone, so uncle and Misha both lose their last vowel. The little sound at the end makes the request softer, like 'would you?'. The field team are the agents who go out on real operations.
- 'I'm tired of sitting in this archive.' The first word means 'it has got boring', and the person who is bored is 'to me', not 'I'. Russian often puts feelings this way round. The chief won't hear of it.
- 'What field team?' Literally, 'what kind of field team for you?' It means 'forget it'. And here's his reason.
- 'Your mother will tear my head off.' The verb is a single-action verb, so this present-looking form talks about the future. He means if anything happens to Lyova. So Lyova teases him.
- 'You're the head of the department, and you're afraid of a woman.' The little word after 'you' adds 'after all', and the word for 'and' here has a 'but' in it. That's too much for the chief.
- 'That's enough talk, Lieutenant Sokolov!' The first word is a little word for 'chatter', used in the army to say 'stop talking'. Switching to rank and surname puts the nephew back in his place.
- 'Back to your desk, quickly.' Literally, 'quickly to your work place'. There's no verb at all, and you don't need one. Then there's a knock at the door.
- 'May we come in? Good day, sir.' Both are formal military phrases. The second one literally wishes the other person good health. These are the two agents from Moscow, Major Lavrov and Captain Zavarzin. The chief is not impressed.
- 'Oh, the James Bonds have turned up.' He makes the famous spy's name plural, the way you would with any noun. Then he tells them exactly what he thinks.
- 'In Moscow they give you money for expensive clothes, but they don't give you brains.' 'They give' has no 'they' in front of it: the verb alone does the job, like 'people give'. The agents stay calm.
- 'General Kruglov is waiting for a call.' After the verb 'wait', the thing you wait for often takes the ending for 'of'. Then a very polite phrase.
- 'If you'd be so kind.' Literally, 'be kind'. Here it's cool and formal: please set up the call. The chief turns to his nephew.
- 'Just look at these Moscow types, Lieutenant. And they're the ones who mess up operations.' The word for 'themselves' points the finger: they are the ones who failed. Their operation at the airport was called off at the last minute. Still, the chief picks up the phone.
- 'Dima, put me through to Moscow. To General Kruglov.' The word for 'with' comes before both Moscow and the general, and both get the same ending that goes with it. And now Lyova sees his chance.
- 'Uncle Misha, can I stay?' The word for 'may I' is followed by 'I stay', in the future form. And the uncle gives in.
- 'All right, sit down. Just keep quiet.' The verb is the one for 'sit', a friendly command. So the young man from the archive gets a seat at a secret meeting.
