## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Rise and shine!' It's the army wake-up call, literally 'getting up'. Samson served in the army, and it shows.
- 'Daddy's already had his cold dip.' He calls himself 'Daddy', the way a father does with his kids. The noun means toughening yourself up with cold water, and the verb is a solemn word for 'did'. Nina isn't impressed.
- 'Oh, get away! You're crazy!' The first phrase pushes someone away, playfully. The second word literally means 'sick', but here it means 'you're mad'. Samson has a request.
- 'Nina, pour your husband a drink.' It's an informal command, 'pour'. 'Husband' has an ending that means 'to' or 'for': pour for the husband. Then comes his reason.
- 'We've got something to celebrate!' Literally 'there is a holiday'. Nina wants to know more.
- 'What's the occasion?' 'What kind of' is two little words here: 'what' and 'for'. It's a very useful pattern. Samson doesn't really answer; he just beams.
- 'Yes, everything's going to be fine.' The middle word makes the future: 'will be'. Nina has her doubts.
- 'I can't see it somehow.' The first word adds a vague 'somehow'. So Samson comes out with it.
- 'So, I'm running for mayor.' Literally 'I'm going into mayors'. Russians say it with the plural, the way you join a group: into the mayors, into the presidents. Nina can't believe her ears.
- 'Running for what?' Her word really means 'where to', just as if he'd said he was going somewhere. He spells it out.
- 'For mayor of our town.' 'Of our town' uses the form that means 'of'.
- 'Arkasha suggested it.' A past form for a man: Arkady. Samson has worked out a plan with him.
- 'I'll be mayor instead of him, so I can get him out later.' 'Instead of him' is one handy little phrase. The idea is that a friendly mayor could free Arkady. Then Samson lists his strengths.
- 'And I've got every chance.' That's the usual way to say 'I have': literally 'at me there are'.
- 'A wife, two kids, I served in the army.' 'Didn't sit' means 'never did time'. In the series he also boasts that he's Orthodox, and that he was in the paratroopers. Nina says nothing.
- 'Why aren't you saying anything?' One verb means 'keep silent'. Finally she speaks.
- 'Nothing. And maybe president after that?' The same plural trick as before: into the presidents. And it's pure sarcasm. Samson is hurt.
- 'Oh, why do you have to start?' Word for word: 'well, what are you starting?'. You say it when someone begins the usual argument. Nina has a more practical idea.
- 'Go to your boss and ask him to forgive you.' Two informal commands: 'go' and 'ask'. Samson insulted his boss on the phone, sure he was getting a better job. But he doesn't want to grovel.
- 'Nina, have a heart!' Literally 'have a conscience'. It means: be fair to me.
- 'Yesterday I did everything for you.' The phrase in the middle means 'for your sake'. He means the humiliating kiss he gave Arkady for a job he never got. Nina just mocks him.
- 'So now you're a hero? And all you have to do is drink?' Literally 'and you may only drink'. That stings. Samson answers with a promise.
- 'I'm going to be mayor, understood?' The verb is a single-action verb, so it's a firm future: 'I will become'. And notice the ending on 'mayor': after 'become', a job or role takes a special ending, the one that means 'as'.
