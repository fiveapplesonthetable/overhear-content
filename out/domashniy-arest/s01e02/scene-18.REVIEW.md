## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Viktor Stepanovich, it's Anikeev calling.' He uses the governor's first name and father's name, the polite Russian way to address a boss. The verb literally means 'is disturbing you', the humble way to introduce yourself on the phone. The governor sounds almost cheerful.
- 'Well, look who it is!' Word for word, 'oh, what people!', said when someone important, or someone unexpected, turns up.
- 'So, Arkasha, how are things?' He uses the childish form of Arkady's name, a little mocking. Arkady doesn't pretend.
- 'Bad. Very bad, Viktor Stepanovich.' Notice he repeats the full polite name, again and again, all through the call. The governor isn't surprised.
- 'I know. So why are you calling me?' The governor says 'you' in the familiar form, while Arkady keeps to the polite one. That tells you who is the boss here.
- 'I really need your help.' Literally, 'to me your help is very needed'. The word for 'needed' is feminine, to match 'help'. And now the governor shows what he knows.
- 'How interesting!' Said coldly, it means quite the opposite. Then comes the accusation.
- 'And in Moscow you go around saying nasty things about me.' After 'in', Moscow takes the ending for places. And there's worse.
- 'I know what I'm saved as in your phone. Pig.' So word has reached him about that contact name. Arkady denies everything.
- 'That's not true! I have you saved as Dad.' The word he uses is a warm, folksy way to say 'dad', and also 'the big boss'. 'I have' is said as 'at me', the usual Russian pattern. The governor has had enough.
- 'I haven't got time.' Literally, 'to me, there's no time'. And he hangs up, back to his judo. Arkady rings again.
- 'Don't hang up, I'm begging you!' Literally, 'don't throw down the receiver'. A polite negative command, for someone you call 'you' formally.
- 'I'm on my knees in front of you.' And in that small room, he really is kneeling. Then flattery.
- 'Forgive me. You're the most effective of all!' He even calls himself a young fool, and worse. The word for 'the most' makes any adjective into the very best, here the governor's favourite quality. It works, a little.
- 'All right, that's enough. What do you want?' The first two words cut someone off politely, or not so politely. Arkady lists his troubles: he slept in his clothes on a bare bed frame, and pipe water poured all over him.
- 'My wife has left me. Two of my fingers are broken.' In the first sentence, 'me' comes first and 'wife' comes after: the word order is free, the endings show who did what. The governor's sympathy is limited.
- 'Serves you right.' The word usually means 'correct' or 'right'. Here it's harsh. Arkady swallows it and asks straight out.
- 'Help me! I'll pay you back, I promise.' The second sentence literally says 'I won't stay in debt', a fixed phrase for 'I'll return the favour'. But there's a problem.
- 'You're under Tretyakov's personal control.' Tretyakov is the fearsome official in Moscow. Unless he gives the go-ahead, the governor won't lift a finger. So Arkady has one more idea.
- 'But maybe you could call him yourself?' The word for 'yourself' here is a plural form, to go with the polite 'you'. The governor nearly drops the phone.
- 'Call Tretyakov? What, have I lost my mind?' Literally, 'have I gone off my mind'. Even the governor is afraid of the man in Moscow.
