## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Well? Is there a job?' The second sentence is only two words, 'work' and 'there is'; the voice going up turns it into a question. Samson doesn't make a long speech of it.
- 'Long story short, no.' The first word is what people say when they want to skip the details, like 'basically'. Then he explains.
- 'The job didn't work out.' Literally, 'with the job it didn't turn out'. This verb is the everyday way to say something did or didn't work. And there's more bad news.
- 'And the same with Crimea.' His boss had given him a holiday by the sea there for two, and that's off as well. Nina is not surprised.
- 'Nobody ever doubted it.' The word for 'nobody' comes with a 'not' before the verb as well: Russian happily uses two negatives in one sentence. Samson isn't in the mood.
- 'Nin, come on, don't start.' Nin is a short, affectionate way of calling Nina. 'Don't' is just 'not' in front of the command. Just then, Arkady comes in, and little Lyosha hurries over to ask about the games console he was promised for his piggy bank.
- 'Lyosha, listen to me.' A simple command to someone you call by their first name. What Arkady says next is hard to hear.
- 'There won't be any console.' Another double negative: 'no console will not be'. After 'there won't be', the missing thing takes the ending for 'of'. And he doesn't soften it.
- 'You've been fooled, got it?' Literally, 'they fooled you'. Russian often uses 'they' with no subject at all when it doesn't matter who did it. Of course, the one who fooled Lyosha is Arkady himself. Samson is shocked.
- 'Arkady, what's got into you? He's just a kid.' The first question is literally 'you what?', a common way to say 'have you lost your mind?'. Lyosha's mother, Marina, leads him away and tells him not to be upset: they'll buy one themselves. But Arkady has already turned to Samson's son.
- 'Slavik, give that here.' Arkady puts 'give' up front and finishes with 'here' to make the point unmistakable.
- 'That's my iPhone.' Arkady swapped it with Slavik for an old phone a few days ago, and now he wants it back. Nina points that out.
- 'What do you mean, yours?' She repeats his word as a question. Then she reminds him of the deal.
- 'You two swapped!' The little word after 'you' means 'but you know'. Nina uses the plural 'you' because she means both of them. Arkady's answer is one word.
- 'Dream on.' It's a command, 'dream', said with a sneer. And then he sends the boy away.
- 'That's it, off you go!' The first word, 'all', is what Russians say to end a conversation. Samson can only stare.
- 'What was that?' Three short words, and they work exactly like the English. Nina has the last word.
- 'Well, there you go. No job, no Crimea, and no iPhone.' The little word repeated three times means 'neither, nor', and each thing after it takes the ending for 'of'. A grim little list for the Samsonovs.
