## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'You look at me as if you want to kill me.' The phrase in the middle means 'as if'. The verb 'look' takes a little word meaning 'at', just like in English. Samson plays innocent.
- 'I'm looking at you normally.' The first word is the handy everyday word for 'normally', 'fine'. Then two short words back at him.
- 'What's up with you?' It's very casual, something friends or rivals say when the other one is acting oddly. Nikolai won't let it go.
- 'Say it to my face.' Literally 'say to me into the eyes'. Then the real question.
- 'Why do you hate me so much?' The first two words together ask 'for what', meaning what have I done to deserve it. Samson decides to be honest.
- 'All right. Look, Nikolai.' He just uses the boss's first name, with the casual 'you'. These two clearly go back a long way. Now comes the evidence.
- 'Right now you're pouring me vodka, aren't you?' The little 'yes' at the end turns it into a question, like 'aren't you?'. Nikolai can't deny it.
- 'Yes.' Just one word, but he has walked into the trap.
- 'And you're drinking French cognac yourself.' The word in the middle means 'yourself': the boss keeps the good stuff for himself. Nikolai shrugs it off.
- 'Well, what difference does it make to you what you drink?' The middle part, 'what difference', is a phrase you'll hear all the time. But then the boss gets to the point himself.
- 'What, you think I'm stingy?' The adjective at the end means 'greedy' or 'stingy'. It's a masculine form, because Nikolai is talking about himself. Samson doesn't soften it.
- 'I think you're stingy.' He just repeats the boss's own two words, without the question. Nikolai protests.
- 'I'm not stingy. I'm sensible.' Notice there's no word for 'am': in the present tense Russian simply leaves it out. And to prove it, he pulls something out.
- 'Here, this is for you.' The first word is the one for handing things over, 'here you are'.
- 'What's this?' The simplest question there is, with 'this' at the end.
- 'Holiday vouchers for two, to Crimea.' The first word is a voucher for a trip, the kind a workers' union used to hand out. The words 'for two' are very useful, for tables and tickets too. And the date sounds lovely, at first.
- 'March, the start of the season.' The month comes first, and then 'the beginning of the season'. Samson isn't fooled.
- 'Hold on, it's cold there in March!' The first word is the casual way to say 'wait'. The little word after 'there' means 'surely, you know'. Just then a foreman comes in with news.
- 'They've delivered bad concrete.' There's no 'they' in it: the verb ends in the plural, and that's enough. And he turns to Samson.
- 'Samson, you carried those radiators for nothing.' The same word for 'in vain' as earlier. With bad concrete, all that showing off may have been wasted effort. And the foreman has one more piece of news.
- 'And another thing. Nobody on the crew wants to go to Crimea in March.' So the boss's generous present is something nobody else wanted. Nikolai has a two-word answer.
- 'They're lying. They're lying.' The verb means 'to lie', 'to tell lies', and it's in the plural: they all lie. Samson has his vouchers now, whether he likes it or not.
