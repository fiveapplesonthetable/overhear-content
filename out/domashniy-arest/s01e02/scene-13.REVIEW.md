## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Nikolai Filippovich, greetings.' A first name and patronymic, the respectful way to address a boss, and a slightly formal hello. Then a careful question.
- 'Am I interrupting?' Literally, 'did I not disturb you?' The verb is in the past tense, in its masculine form, because Samson is a man. The boss answers with the very same word.
- 'You are. Go away.' He just repeats the verb without the 'not', which is a very rude way of saying 'yes'. Samson tries again.
- 'Oh, come on, Filippovich.' Old colleagues sometimes call each other by the patronymic alone, which is friendly and familiar. Then his main argument.
- 'I said sorry, didn't I?' The verb for 'to apologise' ends in a little self-action verb form, and the word before it adds 'but surely'. The boss has a surprise.
- 'And I've already forgiven you.' Sounds good, so far.
- 'You're fired.' A short past form, the same kind of form as 'blocked' or 'detained'. Samson is shocked.
- 'Filippovich! How can you?' The last part is a common cry of protest, literally 'how so?'. The boss explains what still hurts.
- 'You called me a bad word. Me, your boss!' After the verb 'to call' something, the name you call someone goes into the 'with/as' form, so the words for 'bad word' change their endings. When Samson quit, he was very rude to him. Samson suggests a simple solution.
- 'So, shall we forget it?' The verb is in the past tense and the plural, as if it were already done: 'forgot it, right?' The boss isn't sure he can.
- 'How can I forget that?' The verb 'can' is followed by a basic action verb, just like in English.
- 'Well, let's try.' 'Let's' plus a verb in the 'we' form is the standard way to suggest doing something together. Samson cheers him on.
- 'Go on. We're forgetting!' It's like a countdown at the start of a race. But it doesn't work.
- 'I just can't forget it.' Literally, 'it doesn't work out for me to forget it'. A very common way to say that you try but can't. And then a polite goodbye with a sting in it.
- 'Good luck to you in your new job.' Literally 'at the new place of work'. The word for luck is in the 'of' form, because it really means 'I wish you some luck'. At that moment a worker rushes in.
- 'Filippych, the mixer truck can't get through. The radiators are in the way.' At this point, Filippych is an even shorter, chummier form of the boss's patronymic. The mixer is the concrete lorry, and a stack of heavy radiators blocks its path. The boss sees no problem.
- 'So what's the problem? Pick them up yourselves and move them.' Both verbs are commands in the polite or plural form, because he's talking to the whole crew. But there is no crew.
- 'There's nobody. The police have taken everyone.' After 'there is no', the word for 'nobody' is in the 'of' form. The police came to check the workers' documents and took them all away. The boss is stuck.
- 'Well, where am I going to find movers right now?' The verb is a single-action verb, so this present-looking form is really a future: 'will I find'. And Samson sees his chance.
- 'Well, me. It's fine, I can do it.' The word for 'nothing' here means 'no problem'. The boss can hardly believe it.
- 'Samsonov, have you seen those radiators?' Here, Samsonov is Samson's real surname; the boss uses it like a sergeant. Samson isn't worried at all.
- 'Oh, I worked as a mover for three years.' The job, 'mover', is in the 'with/as' form after 'worked': you work 'as' something. Then a promise.
- 'This is fifteen minutes' work.' Literally, 'here there's work for fifteen minutes'.
