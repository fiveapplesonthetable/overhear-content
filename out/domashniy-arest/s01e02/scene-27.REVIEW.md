## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Well, has everyone liked it?' Listen for the word meaning 'liked', which leads straight into 'liked'.
- 'There's a little heart right here, see?' The word for 'heart' has a small, cute ending: a little heart. Then a clear instruction.
- 'Press it.' Literally 'press on it'. It's the polite command, because the official is older. The governor wants numbers.
- 'How many is it?' The first word means 'how many' or 'how much'. Ksenia checks her phone.
- 'A hundred and seventeen likes.' After a number like that, the noun goes into the plural form you hear at the end. The governor is not pleased.
- 'And why so few?' The first word asks 'why', and the last one means 'few' or 'little'. Ksenia has an excuse.
- 'But we're only just starting.' The little word after 'we' adds 'but, you see'. And she makes a promise.
- 'In a month there'll be five thousand.' The first word, followed by a time, means 'in', as in 'in a month's time'. The governor answers with a threat.
- 'Watch out: if there aren't, I'll fire you.' The first word literally means 'look', but here it's a warning. Then Ksenia sees who is calling.
- 'Viktor Stepanovich, Tretyakov is calling you!' Tretyakov is the powerful man in Moscow that the governor is terrified of. What nobody in the room knows is that it's really Arkady, using a phone app that fakes the number, bought with little Lyosha's five hundred roubles, and doing his best Tretyakov voice. The governor silences the room.
- 'Right, quiet, everyone! Hello?' The last word is just how Russians answer the phone. 'Tretyakov' starts shouting at once.
- 'Can you hear me?' He uses the casual 'you' to a regional governor, which shows who is the boss here. Then the question that matters.
- 'Why is Anikeev under arrest over there?' Anikeev is Arkady's own surname, so he is asking about himself. The phrase for 'under arrest' is easy to hear. The governor explains, nervously.
- 'Well, he got caught taking a bribe.' The verb means 'got caught', and it's masculine, because it's about Arkady. 'Tretyakov' explodes.
- 'What bribe?' The question word changes its ending to match the bribe, which is a feminine noun. Then an order.
- 'Release him immediately!' An order made with just the basic form of the verb sounds very harsh and official. The governor is confused.
- 'Pyotr Olegovich, but you said yourself…' He means the orders about Arkady's case came from Tretyakov himself. But 'Tretyakov' won't listen.
- 'What, you don't understand the first time?' Literally 'from the first time'. It's the same bossy voice Arkady used earlier to make the children laugh. The governor gives in, and then dares to ask one more thing.
- 'Understood.' Literally 'I understood', in the masculine past form. It's how you say 'got it' to a boss.
- 'Pyotr Olegovich, and what about the matter of the polar explorer?' A famous polar explorer has died, and Tretyakov promised to sort out his funeral. Arkady has no idea, so he improvises.
- 'I've already sorted it all out.' The verb means 'decided' or 'solved'. Then his wonderful solution: the explorer wanted his ashes scattered over the Arctic Ocean, but people wanted a grave for their hero.
- 'One third goes to the cemetery, two thirds over the ocean.' The words for one third and two thirds are worth knowing, and the cemetery word is long but clear. The governor is overwhelmed.
- 'Brilliant. Simply brilliant.' The first word sounds just like the English 'genius'. And 'Tretyakov' signs off in style.
- 'That's it, don't thank me. And sort out Anikeev.' The verb for 'sort out' is the same one as before, now as a casual command. Arkady has just ordered his own release.
