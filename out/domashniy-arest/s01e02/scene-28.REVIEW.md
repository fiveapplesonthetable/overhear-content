## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'I can't believe it!' Literally, 'I can't to believe': the verb for 'can' is followed by a plain infinitive, just like in English. Then he explains why.
- 'I did it all for five hundred roubles.' The little word in front of the price means 'for', in the sense of 'in exchange for'. Five hundred roubles is what the faking app cost, and it came out of little Lyosha's piggy bank. Samson's teenage daughter Yulia has a question.
- 'So they'll let you go soon?' The first word means 'so' or 'that means', a handy way to draw a conclusion. Arkady is sure of it, but his stomach comes first.
- 'Soon.' A one-word answer. Then a famous way of saying you are very hungry.
- 'But for now I'm hungry, I could eat an elephant!' Russians eat an elephant where English speakers eat a horse. The verb for 'to eat' here looks exactly like the word for 'there is'. Yulia points at something.
- 'There's chicken, over there.' The word in the middle points at something a little way off, like 'over there'. Arkady has bigger ideas than chicken.
- 'And whose fridge is this?' The question word means 'whose', and it changes its ending to match the thing owned. A fridge is masculine, so it stays in this short form.
- 'That's Klavdia Viktorovna's.' The old neighbour's name takes the ending that means 'of': literally, 'that is of Klavdia Viktorovna'. Arkady notices something on the door.
- 'And why is there a lock on it?' Literally, 'and why is there a lock there?' There's no verb at all: in the present tense, Russian simply leaves out 'is'. The answer is a good one.
- 'She keeps bribes for the doctors in there.' The old lady hides little gifts for her doctors, which the children call bribes. The verb, 'keeps', comes last, which sounds very natural in speech. That word catches Arkady's ear.
- 'Bribes? Very interesting.' He knows a thing or two about bribes, of course. And with that, he breaks the lock and opens the fridge. Lyosha is horrified.
- 'What are you doing? Mum will kill us!' The first question, literally 'you what?', is how people react to something outrageous. 'Us' comes first in the second sentence, for emphasis. Arkady waves it away.
- 'Right, everyone calm down.' That's a command for several people, and the ending also makes it polite. Then his reason.
- 'They're coming for me any minute.' There's no 'they' in it: the plural verb on its own means 'people', 'they'. The word at the start is usually 'now', but here it means 'very soon'. He promises to send them delicacies they've never tasted. Yulia is still a little doubtful.
- 'Is that allowed?' The last word means 'it is allowed' or 'you may'. So Arkady simply repeats it.
- 'It is.' Out come crab and caviar, though Arkady sniffs that these hardly count as crabs. Then he turns to Lyosha, who isn't touching anything.
- 'And why are you just sitting there? Come on, eat.' In everyday speech, 'what' often stands in for 'why'. The word for 'come on' is really a command from the verb 'to give'. Lyosha has a good reason not to eat.
- 'Mum said I'm allergic to caviar.' Word for word, 'at me there is an allergy to caviar'. That's how Russian says someone has something. Remember this line. Arkady doesn't believe a word of it.
- 'Mum says that so you won't ask for caviar.' The word in the middle means 'so that', and the verb after it goes into the past tense, even though it's about the future. Then the cruel part.
- 'She has no money.' Literally, 'at her there is no money'. After 'no', money takes the ending that means 'of'. So Lyosha gives in and tries it.
- 'All right. Oh, it's tasty!' The last word on its own means 'it tastes good', and you'll hear it at every Russian table. Arkady is triumphant.
- 'There, you see? And no allergy at all.' The word before allergy means 'no ... at all', and it makes the denial stronger. Meanwhile, on a building site, Samson is phoning Nina: his boss has given him a trip to Crimea, with a heated seawater pool, and she should go and buy a swimsuit.
