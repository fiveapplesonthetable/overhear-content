## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Good afternoon!' Literally, 'good day'. It's the standard polite greeting for most of the day.
- 'Welcome.' Word for word, 'with the arrival by air'. Russians greet people who have just flown in with this little phrase. The partner is polite too.
- 'Good afternoon. Thank you.' So far, so friendly. But Pasha has to confess something.
- 'Gentlemen, I apologise.' Literally, 'I ask forgiveness'. It's a formal, businesslike way to say sorry. He calls it a technical delay.
- 'The money will be there.' The word for money is always plural in Russian, so the verb 'will be' is plural too. The partner wants a date.
- 'And when will it be there?' He puts the question word last, which makes it sound sharp and impatient. Pasha has an excuse ready.
- 'I need to do a little work on the former mayor.' After the word for 'with', both 'former' and 'mayor' take the 'with/as' form. The former mayor is Arkady, and Pasha's 'work' means squeezing the money out of him. The partner reminds him of his promise.
- 'You told me the money would be here today.' In Russian you keep the tense of the original promise: word for word, 'you told me, the money will be today'. Then Stepan Gennadyevich, the older partner, loses patience.
- 'What did we even fly out here for?' The verb means 'to arrive by flying'; the same root was in the greeting at the start.
- 'I said from the start: nothing will come of it with him.' He means Pasha. The partner explains how much they have already done.
- 'We got everything ready and flew in for the deal.' Two past-tense verbs, both in the plural because the subject is 'we'. Pasha tries to calm them down.
- 'I understand perfectly.' The adverb in the middle comes from the word for 'beautiful', so literally 'I understand beautifully'. Stepan isn't impressed.
- 'Why did you even come here? Empty-handed!' Notice he says the informal 'you' to Pasha, not the polite one, which is a sign of contempt here. The last word is literally 'empty'. Pasha reminds him that he has already paid something.
- 'Stepan Gennadyevich, I did pay the advance!' A first name and a patronymic, the respectful way to address an older man. The little word after 'I' adds stress, like 'I did'. Now the partner names his terms.
- 'Here's what we'll do: two days.' Literally 'let's do it like this', the usual way to announce a decision. After the number two, the word for 'day' takes a special ending.
- 'Or we move the project to Kaluga.' Kaluga is another town, not far from Moscow. The present tense here is used for a firm plan. And Stepan adds the painful part.
- 'And the advance stays with us.' The phrase at the end, literally 'at us', is how Russians say 'we have it' or 'with us'. Pasha panics.
- 'Hold on!' The polite form, because he's begging important people. Then his complaint.
- 'Two days is far too little.' The word for 'little' here means 'not enough' when it describes time or money. Stepan has a nasty answer.
- 'Too little for people who do nothing.' Literally 'for those who do nothing'. In Russian, 'nothing' needs a 'not' before the verb as well: a double negative that is perfectly correct. Pasha makes an offer.
- 'All right, let's say three. On my personal responsibility.' He puts his own name on the line. And the partner gives in, a little.
- 'Three days. I can't give you more.' Literally 'more I can't'. With that, the meeting is over.
