## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'What is this, I'm asking you!' A boss's favourite question, shouted at a room full of people. The 'you' is the plural, polite form, because he is shouting at everybody.
- 'What are you staring at, you monkeys?' The odd word at the end is the name of the monkey people in Kipling's Jungle Book, a favourite insult of grumpy bosses.
- 'Have you lost all fear?' Literally, 'have you completely lost fear?' Then Arkady drops the act and explains his problem.
- 'So how can I call this Tretyakov?' The person you phone takes the form for 'to': this, and the name, both change their endings.
- 'What will I say to him?' Both verbs in this line are in the future. And he imagines the call.
- 'Hello, Pyotr Olegovich, it's Anikeev.' The long, polite greeting, with the first name and the father's name. And then he plays Tretyakov's answer.
- 'Anikeev? Well then, caught stealing? I'll put you in prison!' The verb in the middle means 'to get caught stealing from your job', and the last verb, literally 'I'll sit you down', is the everyday way to say 'I'll lock you up'. The children love it.
- 'That's just like him!' Literally, 'very similar'. Yulia agrees, and she has an idea.
- 'It really is.' Literally, 'truly similar'.
- 'Why don't you call the governor yourself?' The children use the polite 'you' with Arkady. The word for 'self' is in the plural, to match. Her idea: Arkady can phone the governor and pretend to be Tretyakov. Arkady isn't convinced.
- 'And then what?' Two tiny words, very useful. Yulia tells him a story from school.
- 'Once I phoned pretending to be the headmistress and cancelled a test.' Both verbs are past and feminine, because Yulia is a girl. The little word in the middle means 'as'. Arkady wants to understand how it works.
- 'From your own phone? And everyone believed it?' The first part means 'from one's own phone': the word for 'own' always points back to the person doing the action. Yulia explains.
- 'No, there are apps for that. They change the number.' The call comes from your phone, but the other person sees someone else's number. Arkady thinks it over.
- 'What nonsense. But on the other hand, what have I got to lose?' The first part is a common way to say 'rubbish'. The last part is literally 'with what do I risk?' He decides to try it.
- 'Right, let's try your app.' 'Let's' is made with a word that literally means 'give', plus the verb in the 'we' form. But there's a problem.
- 'But I haven't got it. I deleted it.' To say 'I don't have it', Russian says 'at me there isn't it'. There was a scandal at school, so she got rid of it.
- 'Now you'd have to buy a new one.' The word in the middle means 'it's necessary'. So Arkady asks the obvious question.
- 'How much?' One word, and you'll use it in every shop.
- 'About five hundred roubles.' Putting the number after the noun makes it approximate, so this means 'roughly five hundred'.
- 'Five hundred. And I haven't got a penny.' The smallest Russian coin stands in for 'a penny'. The former mayor is completely broke.
