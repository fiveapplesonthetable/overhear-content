## Walk-through narration (English only, no target-language words quoted)

- 'Here, Pasha. My pyramid.' The pyramid is a neat stack of banknotes on the table. The first word, 'here', points at it. Then he tests his deputy.
- 'Go on, tell me: how much is here?' The first word is a playful 'come on, go on', and the question word means 'how much'. Pasha takes a guess.
- 'I don't know. Forty, I think.' He means forty million. Notice 'I think' needs no 'I' in Russian here: the verb ending already says who is thinking. Arkady sighs.
- 'Oh, Pasha, Pasha. You can't count.' Literally, 'you don't know how to count'. The verb for 'to count' sits in its basic form before the verb meaning 'can, know how to'.
- 'And this is my deputy!' The word for 'deputy' is a long one, but you'll hear it a lot in this series. Then Arkady takes a few bundles off the top and puts them away in a smaller briefcase.
- 'Now it's forty.' So Pasha was wrong, and now the pile matches his guess exactly. Arkady is very pleased with his little joke.
- 'Now, Happy New Year!' The same two opening words again, 'well, now'. Pasha has a clever idea.
- 'Listen, Arkasha, have you ever tried investing it?' The verb at the end means 'to put money into something'. Remember it, because Arkady is about to throw it back at him.
- 'Money should work, after all.' The word for money is always plural in Russian, so the verb is plural too. Arkady answers with a gesture.
- 'Here's a bonus for you, Pasha.' The first two words, literally 'on you', mean 'here, take this'. He hands Pasha a bundle of notes.
- 'Invest it.' A one-word command, from the same verb Pasha used. Pasha is surprised.
- 'Arkasha, I didn't expect that. Thank you!' The verb is past tense and masculine, because Pasha is a man. Then Arkady gets to the real business of the evening.
- 'Listen, I'm going out for an hour and a half.' The verb means leaving by car, and the phrase at the end is literally 'for one and a half hours'.
- 'You stay with Vika, got it?' The command literally means 'sit for a while'. The little question at the end, 'understood?', is very common. Pasha has a practical question.
- 'But what will I say?' Like the verb for going out, this one looks present but means the future. Arkady already has a story ready.
- 'Say a firework hit the power station.' The first word is the command 'say'. A New Year firework is the perfect excuse.
- 'Three districts without electricity.' The word for 'without' makes the next word change its ending. The light has gone out, so no one can go anywhere. And to make the lie true, he gives one more order.
- 'And phone Tsygankov, have them cut the power.' Tsygankov is the man in charge of the town's electricity. The word in the middle means 'let them'. So Arkady is ready to plunge three districts into darkness for his alibi. As soon as he's gone, Pasha makes the call.
- 'Vitaly Alekseyevich, Happy New Year! Please cut the power in three districts.' He uses the man's first name and his father's name, which is polite, but the casual 'you' in the command, so they know each other well. And he explains with a saying.
- 'Well, the cat has smelled the cream.' A Russian saying: someone has gone off after something tempting. Pasha guesses that Arkady is off to see a woman. The song that follows asks how much money you need to be happy.
